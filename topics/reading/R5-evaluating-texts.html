R5: Evaluation

Foundation Higher All Boards

Develop critical reading skills by evaluating writers' choices, the effectiveness of texts, and forming supported judgements about quality and impact.

Lesson Overview
Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Evaluate the effectiveness of a writer's choices in achieving their purpose
  • Form judgements supported by evidence and reasoning
  • Consider alternative interpretations and evaluate their validity
  • Assess the impact of texts on different readers
  • Use subject terminology accurately when evaluating

Prerequisites

  • Confidence with language and structure analysis (R3, R4)
  • Understanding of how writers make deliberate choices
  • Ability to support opinions with evidence

Materials & Equipment

  • Range of texts at different quality levels
  • evaluation vocabulary list (effective, powerful, compelling, successful, etc.)
  • Model evaluation paragraphs
  • Texts showing the same topic treated differently by different writers

Lesson 1: Making Judgements

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Effective or Not?

Display two different openings to a story about a storm. Students decide which is more effective and begin forming reasons. Introduce: evaluation requires judgement + justification.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Evaluation means making a judgement about how effective something is and supporting that judgement with evidence. Teach students:
  • Judgement words: effective, ineffective, compelling, powerful, successful, convincing, unconvincing, memorable, forgettable
  • Hedging language: "largely effective", "arguably the most powerful", "to some extent" — shows nuanced thinking
  • Consider the purpose: a text designed to terrify should be evaluated differently from one designed to inform. "Is it effective?" always means "effective at doing what?"
  • Consider the audience: a text aimed at children will use different techniques from one aimed at adults
Key exam skill: in AQA Paper 1 Question 5, students must evaluate the effectiveness of the writer's choices. In AQA Paper 2, they evaluate viewpoints. Teach the formula: "The writer's choice of [technique/word/structure] is effective because [reason + evidence], which [effect on reader/purpose]."
Worked Example

Question: "Evaluate the effectiveness of the writer's description of the storm."
Model answer: The writer's description of the storm is largely effective in creating a sense of overwhelming power. The extended metaphor of the storm as a living creature — "it screamed", "it clawed at the windows", "it devoured the garden" — builds a consistent image of the storm as a predatory beast, which makes the destruction feel deliberate rather than random. However, the effect is somewhat weakened by the extended paragraph in the middle that details the family's preparations — while this adds realism, it breaks the tension built by the storm imagery. The most effective moment is the final sentence: "And then, silence." The abrupt shift from violent language to stillness is genuinely startling and mirrors the storm's sudden departure. Overall, the writer succeeds in making the reader feel the storm's terrifying force, even if the pacing could be tighter.

Practice Task

Evaluate the effectiveness of this extract as a piece of descriptive writing:

"The old bookshop smelled of dust and possibility. Shelves stretched from floor to ceiling, crammed with volumes that leaned against each other like drunken friends. A cat slept on a stack of leather-bound classics, completely indifferent to the literary treasures beneath it. In the corner, a reading lamp cast a warm pool of light over a single armchair, as if inviting the reader to sit down and never leave."

The writer creates an appealing and atmospheric description through carefully chosen details. The opening phrase "dust and possibility" is effective because it combines the literal (old bookshops are dusty) with the figurative (books contain possibilities), immediately signalling that this is a place of potential. The simile "like drunken friends" is vivid and slightly humorous, giving the shelves an informal, welcoming quality rather than presenting the bookshop as stuffy or pretentious. The cat is a particularly effective detail — its indifference to "literary treasures" provides gentle irony and makes the scene feel lived-in and real rather than idealised. The final image of the armchair "inviting the reader to sit down and never leave" is effective because it directly addresses the reader, creating a sense of personal invitation. Overall, the description successfully conveys both the physical atmosphere and the emotional appeal of the bookshop.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide an evaluation sentence starter: "This is effective/ineffective because…" and "The writer achieves/does not achieve their purpose because…"
Stretch:
Give students two texts on the same topic by different writers. Ask them to evaluate which is more effective and consider whether their judgement might differ for different audiences.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Thumbs Up/Down

Read statements about a text: "The opening is effective" / "The ending is weak" / "The language creates atmosphere." Students indicate agreement/disagreement and must justify with one piece of evidence.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can form clear judgements about effectiveness, supported by evidence and reasoning
  • Getting there: Can give opinions but needs support with justification
  • Not yet: Gives unsupported opinions or confuses evaluation with description

Lesson 2: Evaluating for Purpose and Audience

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Purpose Detectives

Display three short texts: a letter to a newspaper, a blog post, and a speech. Students identify the purpose and intended audience of each. Discuss: how does the writer adapt their approach for each?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Evaluation always depends on purpose and audience. Teach students to consider:
  • Purpose: Is the text trying to inform, persuade, argue, entertain, describe, or narrate? Effectiveness depends on whether it achieves this purpose.
  • Audience: Who is the text for? A text aimed at children will use different techniques from one aimed at adults. A formal report will differ from a blog post.
  • Context: When and where was the text written? A Victorian novel will have different conventions from a modern one.
Model evaluating a text for its specific purpose: "As a persuasive article, this text is effective because it uses rhetorical questions to engage the reader, personal anecdotes to create empathy, and statistics to build credibility. However, as a piece of journalism, it could be criticised for lacking balance — the opposing viewpoint is mentioned only briefly."
Worked Example

Question: "Evaluate how effectively this letter achieves its purpose of convincing the council to reverse their decision."
Model answer: The letter is largely effective as a piece of persuasive writing. The writer opens with a personal anecdote about their child's experience at the library, which is effective because it creates immediate emotional engagement — the reader (the council) is reminded that real families are affected. The use of statistics ("847 children attended reading sessions last year") strengthens the argument by providing factual evidence alongside the emotional appeal. However, the letter is weakened by the final paragraph, which shifts from persuasion to accusation ("Your decision is callous and short-sighted"). This aggressive tone may alienate the very people the writer is trying to convince, undermining the earlier, more measured argument. Overall, the letter succeeds in making a compelling case but could be more effective with a more diplomatic conclusion.

Practice Task

Evaluate how effectively these two texts achieve their different purposes:

Text A (newspaper report): "A house fire on Elm Street left three families homeless yesterday evening. Firefighters from the central station responded at 6:45pm and battled the blaze for two hours. No injuries were reported, but all three families lost most of their belongings. The Red Cross has provided temporary accommodation."

Text B (charity appeal): "Imagine losing everything in a single evening. Your photographs. Your children's toys. The clothes on your back. Yesterday, three families on Elm Street didn't have to imagine — it happened to them. They need your help now."

Text A achieves its purpose as a newspaper report effectively. It provides the key facts (who, what, when, where) in a clear, objective manner without emotional language. The tone is neutral and informative, appropriate for a news report. The inclusion of specific details (6:45pm, two hours, Red Cross) adds credibility.

Text B achieves its purpose as a charity appeal effectively. It uses direct address ("you", "your") to involve the reader personally, imperative language ("imagine") to provoke emotional engagement, and the rhetorical device of listing personal items (photographs, toys, clothes) to make the loss feel concrete and real. The shift from "imagine" to "it happened to them" closes the emotional distance between reader and victim. While Text A informs, Text B persuades — and each is highly effective at its specific purpose.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a "Purpose and Audience" framework: "This text is for [audience] and its purpose is [purpose]. It achieves this by [technique]…"
Stretch:
Give a text that seems to have multiple purposes (e.g. inform AND persuade). Ask students to evaluate how successfully it balances these competing aims.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Audience Swap

Take a sentence from a text and rewrite it for a different audience. Discuss how the change in audience changes the effectiveness of different techniques.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can evaluate effectiveness in relation to specific purpose and audience
  • Getting there: Can identify purpose but evaluation is not always linked to it
  • Not yet: Evaluates texts without considering purpose or audience

Lesson 3: Evaluation in Exam Questions

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Word Analysis

Display exam command words: "describe", "explain", "analyse", "evaluate". Students match each to its meaning. Focus: "evaluate" means "make a judgement and justify it" — it goes beyond analysis.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Focus on exam-specific evaluation skills:
  • AQA Paper 1 Q5: "How has the writer used language and structure to engage the reader?" — requires evaluation of effectiveness
  • AQA Paper 2 Q2: "How does the writer use language and structure to present [topic] in this source?" — requires evaluation of how techniques achieve effects
  • AQA Paper 2 Q4: "Compare how the two writers present their viewpoints" — requires evaluation of which writer is more effective
Teach the "So What?" test: after every analytical point, ask "So what? Why does this matter? How does this affect the reader?" If you cannot answer, your analysis is incomplete. Show the difference between analysis ("the writer uses a metaphor") and evaluation ("the writer's use of metaphor is effective because it makes the reader feel…"). For 8-mark questions (AQA Paper 2), teach students to structure: introduction with overall judgement, two developed paragraphs each evaluating a different technique, and a concluding judgement.
Worked Example

Question (8 marks): "Compare how the two writers present the idea of loneliness in Source A and Source B."
Model answer structure:
Introduction: Both writers present loneliness as an isolating experience, but they achieve this through very different techniques. Writer A focuses on physical emptiness while Writer B focuses on emotional disconnection.
Paragraph 1 (Source A): Writer A creates a powerful sense of physical isolation through the extended metaphor of empty space. The description of the character sitting "in a room so vast that her voice disappeared before it reached the walls" uses hyperbole to suggest loneliness is not just emotional but physical — it fills and empties the spaces around a person. The verb "disappeared" has connotations of erasure, suggesting the character's very identity is being consumed by loneliness. This is effective because it makes the reader feel the overwhelming, suffocating nature of the experience.
Paragraph 2 (Source B): Writer B presents loneliness differently, focusing on the pain of being unseen. The metaphor of being "a ghost at the feast" suggests the character is present but invisible — surrounded by people who fail to notice them. The alliterative "ghost" and "ghost" is perhaps too neat, making the loneliness feel almost literary rather than raw. However, the final line — "and nobody asked me how I was" — lands with devastating simplicity. The plain, unadorned language contrasts with the metaphor, suggesting that beneath the literary devices, loneliness is ultimately about the absence of human connection. This is arguably more powerful than Writer A's approach because it is so understated.
Conclusion: Both writers are effective, but in different ways. Writer A's approach is visceral and atmospheric; Writer B's is quietly devastating. Which is more effective depends on the reader — those who respond to imagery may prefer Writer A, while those who prefer understatement may find Writer B more moving.

Practice Task

Write an evaluation paragraph for this exam-style question: "How effectively does the writer create a sense of danger in this extract?"

"The path narrowed until there was barely room for both feet. To the left, the rock face rose sheer and grey. To the right, the cliff dropped away to a sea that was still, too still, the colour of slate. Jamie realised his hands were shaking. He gripped the rope tighter and told himself to keep moving. Below, a wave slapped against the rocks with a sound like breaking bones."

The writer creates a highly effective sense of danger through a combination of physical description and sensory detail. The path "narrowed until there was barely room for both feet" immediately establishes the physical precarity of Jamie's position, while the contrasting descriptions of the rock face rising "sheer and grey" on one side and the cliff dropping away on the other create a visual sense of entrapment — Jamie is hemmed in by danger on both sides. The simile "the colour of slate" suggests cold, hard unforgiving rock, while the detail that the sea was "still, too still" is particularly effective — the repetition of "still" implies that the calm is unnatural and temporary, building suspense. The visceral detail of Jamie's shaking hands makes the danger personal rather than abstract, and the final simile — "a sound like breaking bones" — is brutally physical, suggesting the consequences of a fall without stating them directly. The writer is largely successful in making the reader feel Jamie's fear.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a "So What?" prompt card. After each analytical point, students must answer: "So what? Why does this matter to the reader?"
Stretch:
Give students a full GCSE reading paper and ask them to identify which questions require evaluation (command words: evaluate, compare, assess, judge). Then answer one fully.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Mark Scheme Match

Display a student answer and a mark scheme. Students award marks and explain their reasoning. Discuss where the answer meets or falls short of each mark band.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can write evaluative paragraphs that make clear judgements, supported by evidence and reasoning, linked to purpose and audience
  • Getting there: Can make judgements but needs to develop justification further
  • Not yet: Describes rather than evaluates, or makes unsupported judgements

Homework Suggestions

  • Read two articles about the same topic and evaluate which is more effective at informing or persuading the reader
  • Write a "book review" paragraph evaluating the effectiveness of a novel's opening chapter
  • Complete an 8-mark comparison question from a GCSE past paper, focusing on evaluation
  • Find an advertisement and evaluate how effectively it uses language and structure to achieve its purpose
