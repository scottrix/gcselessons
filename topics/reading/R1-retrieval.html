R1: Retrieval and Literal Comprehension

Foundation Higher All Boards

Develop the ability to locate and understand explicit information in texts. This is the foundation skill for all reading comprehension tasks in GCSE English Language.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Identify and extract explicit information from a range of text types
  • Use skimming and scanning techniques to locate information efficiently
  • Answer retrieval questions accurately using evidence from the text
  • Understand the difference between explicit and implicit information
  • Paraphrase retrieved information in your own words

Prerequisites

  • Ability to read fluently at age-appropriate level
  • Basic understanding of different text types (narrative, non-fiction, poetry)
  • Familiarity with making notes or highlighting key information

Materials & Equipment

  • Range of non-fiction texts (newspaper articles, reports, letters)
  • Fiction extracts (short stories, novel excerpts)
  • Highlighters and pens
  • Exercise books for written responses
  • Printed comprehension question sheets

Lesson 1: What is Retrieval?

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Information Hunt

Give students a short newspaper article (approximately 200 words). Ask them to find three specific facts as quickly as possible: the name of a person mentioned, a date, and a location. Discuss how they found each piece of information — did they read the whole article or jump to specific parts?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Explain that retrieval questions ask you to find information that is explicitly stated in the text — the answer is "right there" on the page. Unlike inference questions, you do not need to read between the lines.
  • Skimming — reading quickly to get the general idea. Useful for understanding what a text is about before answering questions.
  • Scanning — moving your eyes quickly over the text looking for specific words, names, dates, or phrases. You are not reading every word.
  • Close reading — reading a section carefully to extract precise information.
Demonstrate each technique with a model text. Show students how exam questions often quote directly from the text — words from the question will appear (or be very similar to words) in the passage. Key exam technique: underline key words in the question before searching the text. Look for synonyms and paraphrasing — examiners rarely copy the exact sentence.
Worked Example

Question: "What reason does the writer give for the decline in bird populations?"
Strategy: Underline "reason", "decline", "bird populations". Scan the text for these words or near-synonyms (e.g. "fall in numbers of birds", "fewer birds"). The answer will be in a sentence nearby.
Model answer: The writer states that "the widespread use of pesticides has destroyed the insects that many bird species depend on for food" (paragraph 4).

Practice Task

Read the following extract from a newspaper article about urban green spaces, then answer the questions below.

"The campaign to save Meadow Lane green space has gathered momentum over the past six months. What began as a petition signed by just twelve residents has grown into a movement supported by over two thousand people. The local council has proposed building sixty new flats on the site, arguing that the area desperately needs affordable housing. However, opponents point out that Meadow Lane is one of the last remaining green spaces in the ward and provides a vital habitat for hedgehogs, foxes, and numerous bird species. Dr Sarah Chen, an ecologist at the university, warned that removing the green space would have 'significant consequences for local biodiversity.' The council has agreed to postpone its decision until a full environmental impact assessment has been completed."

1. How many people initially signed the petition?
2. What does the local council want to build on the green space?
3. What three animal species are mentioned as using the green space?
4. What has the council agreed to do before making a decision?

1. Twelve residents initially signed the petition.
2. The council has proposed building sixty new flats on the site.
3. Hedgehogs, foxes, and bird species.
4. The council has agreed to complete a full environmental impact assessment before making a decision.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide texts with clear paragraph numbering. Give sentence starters for answers: "The writer states that…" or "According to the text…". Use shorter texts with bold or highlighted keywords.
Stretch:
Ask students to find where the examiners might "trick" them — where information appears to be stated but is actually the writer's opinion. Challenge them to retrieve information that spans multiple paragraphs.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Fire Retrieval

Read a short passage aloud (or display on screen). Students write down as many specific facts as they can recall in one minute. Then compare — which facts were explicit (directly stated) and which required inference?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can retrieve explicit information accurately, using text evidence to support answers
  • Getting there: Can find most information but occasionally confuses explicit details with personal assumptions
  • Not yet: Struggles to locate information in the text or gives answers based on personal opinion rather than the text

Lesson 2: Different Text Types

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Text Type Sorting

Display six short extracts (2-3 sentences each) from different text types: newspaper article, letter, report, review, speech, and narrative. Students sort them into categories. Discuss how the structure and language differ across types.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
GCSE English Language exams use a variety of text types. Students must be able to retrieve information from all of them.
  • Non-fiction texts: newspaper articles, magazine features, letters, reports, speeches, travel writing, autobiography extracts
  • Fiction texts: extracts from novels (19th century, modern, contemporary), short stories
  • Poetry: single poems or paired comparisons
  • Visual texts: charts, graphs, infographics (mainly in non-fiction papers)
Teach students that non-fiction texts often have clear structural features that make retrieval easier: headings, subheadings, bullet points, statistics, and quotations. Fiction texts require more careful reading as information is embedded within narrative, description, or dialogue. Demonstrate with a non-fiction text: show how scanning for numbers, dates, and capitalised words (names, places) can quickly locate factual information. Then show how the same technique works in fiction — dialogue tags ("said", "exclaimed"), character names, and time markers ("the next morning", "years later").
Worked Example

Non-fiction question: "Give two statistics the writer uses to support their argument."
Strategy: Scan for numbers and percentages. Underline them, then check they relate to the writer's argument (not counter-arguments or general facts).
Fiction question: "What time of day does the scene take place?"
Strategy: Scan for time-related words: morning, evening, dawn, dusk, clock times, or descriptions of light/sound/temperature that indicate time.

Practice Task

Read this extract from a letter to a newspaper editor:

"Dear Sir/Madam, I am writing to express my deep concern about the proposed closure of Greenhill Library. Since opening in 1987, the library has served as a cornerstone of our community, providing free access to books, internet facilities, and educational programmes. Last year alone, over 15,000 visitors used the library — a figure that represents a 12% increase on the previous year. The council's claim that 'usage has declined' is simply not supported by the evidence. Furthermore, the library employs twelve full-time staff and eight volunteers, all of whom would lose their positions if the closure goes ahead. I urge the council to reconsider this short-sighted decision. Yours faithfully, Margaret Thompson."

1. In what year did Greenhill Library open?
2. How many visitors used the library last year?
3. What percentage increase in usage does the writer claim occurred?
4. How many people would lose their jobs if the library closes?
5. What two services does the writer say the library provides?

1. 1987
2. Over 15,000 visitors
3. 12% increase
4. Twelve full-time staff and eight volunteers (20 people total)
5. Free access to books and internet facilities (also educational programmes)

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a glossary of text type features. Use colour-coding: highlight structural features in different colours (headings in blue, statistics in red, quotes in green).
Stretch:
Give students a text with deliberate misleading information (e.g. a statistic that appears to support the argument but actually undermines it). Ask them to identify which statistics genuinely support the writer's case.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Which Text Type?

Read out five statements from different text types. Students hold up cards or write: letter, article, report, speech, or narrative. Discuss any that were tricky to classify.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can retrieve information accurately from any text type, adapting strategy as needed
  • Getting there: Works well with non-fiction but struggles with fiction or poetry
  • Not yet: Cannot adapt retrieval strategy to different text types

Lesson 3: Exam-Style Retrieval Questions

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Exam Question Practice

Display a GCSE-style retrieval question (1 mark). Students must answer it in under 30 seconds. "The writer describes the weather as 'bitter'. What type of weather does this suggest?" (Cold, harsh, extreme). Build speed and confidence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
In GCSE English Language exams, retrieval questions are typically worth 1-4 marks. Teach students the different command words:
  • "Give/State/Name" — write exactly what the text says. Usually 1 mark.
  • "What/Who/When/Where" — find the specific detail. Usually 1-2 marks.
  • "How does the writer…" or "Explain how…" — retrieval plus some analysis. Usually 2-4 marks.
  • "Find and copy" — quote directly from the text. 1 mark.
Key exam technique for 1-mark questions: give a short, precise answer. Do not waffle — examiners want the exact information. For 2-4 mark questions, you need to identify the information AND explain or develop it. Show students how to "hunt" for answers: re-read the paragraph the question refers to, look for the keywords or their synonyms, then extract the relevant phrase or sentence. Practice with a full reading comprehension paper. Model the process of working through each question: read the question, identify command words and key words, locate in text, formulate answer.
Worked Example

Question (4 marks): "Explain how the writer creates a sense of tension in paragraphs 3 and 4."
Strategy: This is retrieval PLUS analysis. First, find the techniques the writer uses (short sentences, repetition, specific word choices). Then explain the effect on the reader. You need to retrieve evidence AND comment on it.
Model answer: In paragraph 3, the writer uses a short, punchy sentence: "I froze." This creates tension because its brevity contrasts with the longer descriptive sentences before it, suggesting the narrator has stopped moving and is suddenly alert to danger. In paragraph 4, the writer builds tension through repetition of "footsteps" — first "distant footsteps", then "louder footsteps", then "footsteps outside the door". This repetition creates a sense of approaching threat that escalates with each sentence.

Practice Task

Read the following extract and answer the questions.

"The train pulled into the station at exactly 7:42, just as it had every morning for the past thirty years. But today, something was different. The platform, usually crowded with commuters rushing to work, was completely empty. Not a soul in sight. Thomas checked his watch again — 7:42, right on time. He stepped off the train and his footsteps echoed across the deserted station. The information board above him flickered and went dark. A cold draught blew through the tunnel, carrying with it the faint smell of smoke. Thomas felt the hairs on the back of his neck stand up. Something was very wrong."

1. What time does the train arrive? (1 mark)
2. How long has Thomas been catching this train? (1 mark)
3. How does the writer show that something is unusual about the station today? (2 marks)
4. What sensory details does the writer use to build an unsettling atmosphere? (2 marks)

1. 7:42
2. Thirty years
3. The writer shows something is unusual by describing the platform as "completely empty" and saying "Not a soul in sight" — normally it is "crowded with commuters rushing to work" (paragraph 1).
4. The writer uses: sound (footsteps echoing, suggesting emptiness), sight (the information board going dark), touch (a cold draught), and smell (the faint smell of smoke). These sensory details create an unsettling atmosphere because they combine unfamiliar sensory experiences in a normally familiar place.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a question template: "In paragraph ___, the writer says… This means/suggests…" Use highlighters to mark evidence in the text before writing answers. Reduce the number of questions.
Stretch:
Give students an unfamiliar text type and ask them to write their own retrieval questions for a partner. Discuss which questions are worth 1 mark vs 2-4 marks and why.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Speed Round

Display a passage and five 1-mark retrieval questions. Students have 2 minutes to answer all five. Discuss which were easiest and why — usually the ones where they knew exactly what to scan for.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can answer retrieval questions accurately and efficiently, using appropriate strategies for different question types and mark allocations
  • Getting there: Can answer 1-mark questions confidently but needs support with 2-4 mark questions that require explanation
  • Not yet: Struggles to locate information or gives answers that do not match the marks available

Homework Suggestions

  • Read a newspaper article and write down five explicit facts, then explain how you found each one (skim, scan, or close read)
  • Complete a GCSE-style reading comprehension paper focusing only on the retrieval questions
  • Write three 1-mark retrieval questions and three 2-mark retrieval questions about a text you have been reading
  • Keep a retrieval diary: each day, read a short text and note one piece of explicit information you could be asked about in an exam
