R3: Language Analysis

Foundation Higher All Boards

Analyse how writers use word choice, figurative language, and literary techniques to create meaning, mood, and atmosphere.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 4
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Identify and explain the effect of word choice (connotation and denotation)
  • Recognise and analyse similes, metaphors, personification, and other figurative language
  • Understand how language creates mood, atmosphere, and character
  • Write analytical paragraphs using PEER structure
  • Compare language effects across different parts of a text

Prerequisites

  • Confidence with inference (R2)
  • Understanding of basic literary techniques (simile, metaphor, personification)
  • Ability to select evidence from a text

Materials & Equipment

  • Range of texts with rich, varied language (novels, short stories, non-fiction)
  • Language analysis vocabulary list
  • PEER writing frame (Point, Evidence, Explain, Reader)
  • Highlighters for identifying techniques
  • Example analytical paragraphs (modelled and student-written)

Lesson 1: Word Choice (Connotation vs Denotation)

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Same Meaning, Different Feeling

Display two sentences: "He walked to the shop" vs "He trudged to the shop." Ask: what is different? Both describe the same action, but trudged suggests heaviness, tiredness, or reluctance. Introduce denotation (literal meaning) vs connotation (associated feelings and ideas).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach that every word carries both a denotation (dictionary definition) and connotations (associated ideas, feelings, images). Writers choose words deliberately for their connotations.
  • Positive connotations: "gleaming" (implies newness, care), "serene" (implies peace, beauty)
  • Negative connotations: "grimy" (implies neglect, dirt), "clammy" (implies discomfort, unease)
  • Emotional connotations: "whispered" (intimacy, fear, secrecy), "bellowed" (anger, authority, loss of control)
Teach students to ask: "Why THIS word and not another?" If a writer chose "strolled" instead of "walked", they are suggesting something about the character's mood or attitude. The question "how does the writer use language to present X?" is always asking about the effect of specific word choices. Show a passage and model the analytical process: identify a word, explain its connotations, explain how this creates an impression.
Worked Example

Extract: "The castle loomed above them, its dark towers clawing at the sky."

Analysis: The writer uses the verb "loomed" rather than "stood" or "towered". "Loomed" has connotations of threat and menace — things that loom are often dangerous or intimidating. Combined with "dark towers" and the verb "clawing" (which suggests aggressive, animal-like behaviour), the writer creates a sense that the castle is hostile and threatening rather than simply large.

Practice Task

Analyse the language in these sentences. For each, identify one key word and explain its effect.

1. "The garden sprawled in every direction, unruly and magnificent."
2. "She crept along the corridor, barely daring to breathe."
3. "The politician breezed through the interview, his smile never quite reaching his eyes."

1. "Sprawled" — this verb usually describes a person lying carelessly, suggesting the garden is wild, untamed, and expansive. Combined with "unruly", the writer presents the garden as beautiful but chaotic.
2. "Crept" — suggests stealth, fear, and caution. The character is moving slowly and quietly, implying danger. "Barely daring to breathe" reinforces that any noise could have serious consequences.
3. "Breezed" — suggests ease and effortlessness, implying the politician is smooth and confident. However, "his smile never quite reaching his eyes" undermines this, suggesting the ease is performative and insincere.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a word bank of "strong" synonyms for common verbs (walked → trudged, stumbled, sauntered, marched). Students replace weak verbs and discuss the effect of each change.
Stretch:
Give a passage where the writer uses words with mixed connotations. Ask students to explain how this creates ambiguity or complexity in meaning.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Synonym Swap

Display a sentence with a bold word. Students suggest alternatives and discuss how each would change the tone and meaning of the sentence.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can identify key words and explain their connotations and effect on the reader
  • Getting there: Can identify interesting words but struggles to explain connotations in detail
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between denotation and connotation

Lesson 2: Figurative Language

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Literal vs Figurative

Display: "Time is money." Ask: is this literally true? No — time is not currency. So what does it mean? Time is valuable, wasting it has consequences. This is figurative language — meaning beyond the literal words.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Cover the main figurative language techniques students need for GCSE:
  • Simile — comparison using "like" or "as": "Her voice was like ice" (cold, sharp, cutting)
  • Metaphor — direct comparison without "like/as": "The world was a stage" (life is performance, roles are temporary)
  • Personification — giving human qualities to non-human things: "The wind howled" (suggests aggression, sadness, or wildness)
  • Pathetic fallacy — weather/setting reflecting mood: rain during a sad scene, sunshine during happiness
  • Onomatopoeia — words that sound like what they describe: "crash", "whisper", "buzz"
  • Hyperbole — exaggeration for effect: "I've told you a million times"
  • Zoomorphism — attributing animal qualities to humans: "She pounced on the opportunity"
For each technique, explain: identify the technique → explain what is being compared/described → explain the effect. Never just label a technique — always explain WHY the writer used it.
Worked Example

Extract: "The fog crept through the streets like a thief, stealing the colour from everything it touched."

Analysis: The writer uses a simile comparing fog to a thief — both move silently, stealthily, and take something away. The personification of fog "creeping" and "stealing" makes it seem deliberate and malevolent, as if the fog has intent. "Stealing the colour" is a metaphor suggesting the fog drains the world of vibrancy and life. Together, these techniques create an atmosphere of quiet menace and desolation.

Practice Task

Identify and analyse the figurative language in these extracts:

1. "The house groaned under the weight of years, its timbers creaking like old bones."
2. "Hope was a bird that sang in her chest, even on the darkest mornings."
3. "The city never slept — it twitched and muttered through the small hours, restless and wired."

1. Personification — "groaned" gives the house human suffering, suggesting age and decay. Simile — "like old bones" reinforces the idea of aging, fragility, and discomfort. The combined effect suggests a house that is weary and deteriorating.
2. Metaphor — hope is compared to a bird, suggesting it is alive, free, and capable of rising above困难. "Sang in her chest" personifies the feeling of hope as music — uplifting and involuntary. "Darkest mornings" uses pathetic fallacy to suggest despair, making the bird metaphor more powerful by contrast.
3. Personification — the city is given human sleeplessness. "Twitched and muttered" suggests the city is restless and unwell, creating an atmosphere of urban anxiety. "Restless and wired" reinforces the idea of a place that cannot find peace.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a "technique identification sheet" with definitions and examples. Use colour-coding: highlight similes in blue, metaphors in red, personification in green.
Stretch:
Give texts with layered figurative language (multiple techniques in one sentence). Ask students to explain how techniques work together to create a combined effect.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Technique Speed Round

Display 10 short sentences. Students write the name of the figurative technique used in each (simile, metaphor, personification, etc.). Review as a class.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can identify and analyse a range of figurative language techniques, explaining their effect on the reader
  • Getting there: Can identify techniques but explains effect only briefly
  • Not yet: Cannot identify figurative language or confuses different techniques

Lesson 3: Writing Analysis (PEER Structure)

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

What's Missing?

Display a weak analytical paragraph: "The writer uses good language. The words are interesting and create a mood." Ask: what is missing? (Specific evidence, explanation of effect, reader response). Introduce PEER as the solution.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach the PEER structure for analytical paragraphs:
  • P — Point: state what the writer is doing or what effect is created ("The writer creates a sense of isolation")
  • E — Evidence: quote from the text ("The writer describes the room as 'empty except for the echo of her footsteps'")
  • E — Explain: analyse the language — connotations, techniques, word choices ("The word 'empty' has connotations of abandonment and loss, while 'echo' suggests a space so bare that sound bounces back, emphasising the character's solitude")
  • R — Reader response: explain the effect on the reader ("This makes the reader feel sympathy for the character and understand the depth of their loneliness")
Model writing PEER paragraphs. Show that evidence should be short (a phrase, not a whole paragraph) and should be woven into the sentence, not just dropped in. Common mistakes to address: writing about what happens instead of how language is used; quoting too much; explaining the technique instead of its effect.
Worked Example

Weak: "The writer uses personification. The fog crept through the streets. This makes it sound scary."

Strong (PEER): "The writer creates a sinister atmosphere through personification of the fog. The verb 'crept' suggests stealth and deliberate movement, as if the fog has malicious intent — it does not simply drift or roll, it creeps like a predator. This makes the reader feel uneasy, as if the fog is an active threat rather than a natural weather phenomenon, building tension and a sense of vulnerability."

Practice Task

Using the extract below, write two PEER paragraphs analysing the writer's language choices.

"The market was alive with noise. Vendors bellowed their prices, children shrieked with delight as they chased each other between the stalls, and somewhere a radio crackled out a tune that nobody seemed to be listening to. The air was thick with the smell of frying onions and overripe fruit. Everything jostled against everything else — colours, sounds, scents — in a cheerful, overwhelming chaos."

PEER 1: The writer creates a vivid sense of the market's energy through carefully chosen verbs. "Bellowed" rather than "called" suggests the vendors are loud and forceful, competing for attention, while "shrieked" conveys the high-pitched excitement of children. The verb "crackled" for the radio suggests imperfect sound — tinny, fragmented — yet the writer adds that "nobody seemed to be listening", which implies the music is just another layer of background noise in an already overwhelming sensory environment. This makes the reader feel immersed in the bustling atmosphere, almost able to hear the chaos.

PEER 2: The writer uses personification to bring the market to life. "The market was alive" directly attributes life to an inanimate place, while "everything jostled against everything else" gives objects human-like physical behaviour — jostling is what people do in a crowd. The final phrase "cheerful, overwhelming chaos" uses oxymoron — chaos is usually negative, but "cheerful" reframes it positively. This suggests the writer finds the market's disorder delightful rather than stressful, and the reader is invited to share this appreciation.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a PEER template with sentence starters: "The writer creates [effect] through…" / "The word [quote] suggests…" / "This makes the reader feel…"
Stretch:
Ask students to write a PEER paragraph without using the word "shows" or "creates" — they must use more varied and precise analytical verbs (establishes, conveys, reinforces, underscores, highlights).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Paragraph Surgery

Display a paragraph with deliberate errors (too much quotation, no explanation, no reader response). Students identify what needs fixing and suggest improvements.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can write structured PEER paragraphs with embedded evidence and detailed analysis
  • Getting there: Can write PEE paragraphs but reader response is underdeveloped
  • Not yet: Writes about what happens rather than how language creates effects

Lesson 4: Comparing Language Effects

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Two Descriptions

Display two descriptions of the same place (e.g. a forest) — one positive, one negative. Students identify three language differences and explain how each creates a different impression.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
In GCSE exams, students are often asked to compare how language is used across two texts or across different parts of the same text. Teach comparison skills:
  • Compare, don't just describe — "While Writer A uses…, Writer B instead uses…" is stronger than "Writer A uses… Writer B uses…"
  • Look for differences in tone — two writers describing the same topic may have very different attitudes
  • Consider different techniques — one writer may favour metaphor while another uses short sentences for impact
  • Note similarities too — if both writers use personification, discuss how the effects differ
For AQA Paper 1 Question 2, students compare two texts. For AQA Paper 2, they compare viewpoints. Teach them to organise by technique or by effect, not by text. Model a comparative analysis paragraph showing how to discuss two texts in the same paragraph.
Worked Example

Comparative paragraph: "Both writers use personification to describe the sea, but with very different effects. Writer A describes the waves as 'dancing playfully', using positive connotations of joy and lightness that create an inviting, cheerful atmosphere. Writer B, however, writes that 'the sea spat and snarled at the shore', using aggressive, animal-like verbs that personify the sea as hostile and dangerous. While Writer A invites the reader to see the sea as beautiful, Writer B presents it as a threat — the contrast suggests that our perception of nature depends entirely on our emotional state."

Practice Task

Compare how these two writers present the concept of home:

Writer A: "Home was a warm pocket in the world — a place where the kettle was always on and the door was never locked."

Writer B: "Home had become a place he passed through, like a hotel room between engagements. Nothing in it was truly his."

Write a comparative paragraph analysing how each writer presents the idea of home differently.

Both writers use metaphor to present home, but with contrasting effects. Writer A uses the metaphor of a "warm pocket" — small, cosy, and protective — combined with domestic details (the kettle, the unlocked door) that suggest warmth, safety, and welcome. The word "always" implies reliability and permanence. Writer B, by contrast, uses the simile "like a hotel room between engagements" to suggest transience and detachment — a hotel room is temporary, impersonal, and never truly yours. The phrase "nothing in it was truly his" reinforces this sense of alienation. While Writer A presents home as a place of emotional belonging, Writer B suggests that home can become meaningless when emotional connection is lost.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a comparison frame: "Writer A uses ___ which suggests ___. However, Writer B uses ___ which suggests ___. The difference in language suggests…"
Stretch:
Give students three or more texts to compare. Ask them to identify which writer creates the most powerful effect and justify their choice with evidence.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Agree or Disagree

Display a comparative statement: "Both writers use metaphor to create an atmosphere of danger." Students vote agree/disagree and justify their position using evidence.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can compare language effects across texts, using comparative connectives and analysing both similarities and differences
  • Getting there: Can describe each writer's language separately but needs support with comparison
  • Not yet: Describes each text independently without comparing them

Homework Suggestions

  • Find three examples of figurative language in a novel you are reading and write PEER paragraphs about each
  • Write a description of a place using at least five different figurative techniques, then annotate each one with its intended effect
  • Compare the language used in two newspaper articles about the same topic — how do their word choices reflect different attitudes?
  • Complete a GCSE language analysis question using the PEER structure
