R2: Inference and Deduction

Foundation Higher All Boards

Learn to read between the lines and draw conclusions from what is implied rather than directly stated. Inference is the most tested reading skill at GCSE.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 4
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the difference between explicit (stated) and implicit (suggested) information
  • Use clues in the text to infer meaning about characters, events, and settings
  • Support inferences with evidence from the text
  • Deduce the meaning of unfamiliar words from context
  • Analyse how writers create meaning through suggestion

Prerequisites

  • Confidence with retrieval skills (R1)
  • Ability to read a passage and summarise what happened
  • Basic understanding of tone, mood, and atmosphere

Materials & Equipment

  • Extracts from novels and short stories with rich implied meaning
  • Non-fiction extracts where writer's viewpoint is implied
  • Highlighters (different colours for evidence vs inference)
  • Whiteboard for modelling thinking processes
  • Printed inference sentence frames

Lesson 1: What is Inference?

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

The Smile

Show a photograph of someone smiling. Ask: "How are they feeling?" Most students will say "happy". Now show a photograph of someone smiling but with slightly red eyes. Ask again. The second image suggests happiness but also possible tears — students are now inferring. Explain: inference is reading between the lines using clues.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Define key terms:
  • Explicit information — directly stated in the text. "She was angry." = she was angry.
  • Implicit information — suggested but not directly stated. "She slammed the door and stormed upstairs." = she was angry (but it does not say so).
Teach the PIE technique for answering inference questions:
  • P — Point: state your inference clearly ("The narrator feels anxious")
  • I — Identify: give the evidence ("The writer describes their 'racing heart' and 'sweating palms'")
  • E — Explain: explain how the evidence supports your inference ("Physical symptoms of anxiety suggest the narrator is experiencing fear or nervousness")
Key principle: every inference must be backed by evidence. "I think she is sad because I feel she is sad" is not an inference — it is an opinion. "She is sad because the writer says she 'stared at the empty chair'" is an inference supported by evidence. Model this process with a short text extract. Think aloud as you demonstrate how a reader makes inferences: "I notice the writer uses the word 'trembling'. This suggests the character is scared or cold. Looking at the context — they are alone in a dark room — I think the writer means they are frightened."
Worked Example

Extract: "Mum polished the silver photo frame for the third time that morning. She placed it back on the mantelpiece, stepped back, adjusted it slightly, then stepped back again."

Question: What can you infer about how Mum is feeling?
Answer: Mum appears to be anxious or nervous, possibly trying to distract herself. The evidence for this is that she has polished the same frame three times in one morning — an unusually repetitive action that suggests she is not really focused on the polishing but rather on something else that is worrying her. The fact that she keeps adjusting the frame suggests restlessness and an inability to settle.

Practice Task

Read this extract and infer what you can about the character's situation and feelings.

"David checked his phone for the fourteenth time. No messages. He poured himself another coffee, even though his hands were already shaking. Through the café window, he watched the street. Every time the door opened, his head snapped round. Each time it was a stranger, his shoulders sagged. The barista had started giving him strange looks. He ordered a third coffee and pretended to read his newspaper, but the same sentence had been staring up at him for twenty minutes."

Write three inferences about David, each supported by evidence from the text.

1. David is waiting for someone important. Evidence: he has checked his phone fourteen times and watches the door every time it opens.
2. David is very anxious or worried. Evidence: his hands are shaking (even from caffeine), his "head snapped round" alertly, and his shoulders sag with disappointment — these are physical signs of anxiety.
3. David is trying to appear normal despite his anxiety. Evidence: he "pretended to read his newspaper" but has not taken in a single sentence in twenty minutes, suggesting he is performing normalcy for the benefit of the barista.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a sentence frame: "I think [character] is feeling ___ because the writer says ___." Use texts with obvious emotional cues (crying, laughing, shouting). Highlight key words in the text.
Stretch:
Give texts where the emotion is ambiguous — the character could be feeling more than one thing. Ask students to consider multiple possible interpretations and decide which is most supported by the evidence.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Two Truths, One Lie

Read three statements about a character from a text — two are well-supported inferences, one is not. Students identify the lie. Discuss why the weak inference lacks evidence.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can make clear inferences supported by specific textual evidence, using the PIE structure
  • Getting there: Can identify what a character is feeling but struggles to explain how the text suggests it
  • Not yet: Confuses personal opinion with inference, or makes inferences without evidence

Lesson 2: Inferring from Non-Fiction

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Tone Detective

Display two letters to a newspaper — one supportive of a new housing development, one against. Students identify: (1) the writer's attitude, (2) how you know, even though neither writer says "I support/oppose this".

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Inference is not only about characters' feelings — it is also about the writer's viewpoint, attitude, and purpose. In non-fiction texts, writers rarely state their opinion directly; they imply it through:
  • Word choice (connotation) — "confident" vs "arrogant" describe the same trait but imply opposite attitudes
  • Selection of evidence — a writer who only includes negative statistics about a topic is implying a negative viewpoint
  • Tone and register — formal language implies authority; informal language implies accessibility or frustration
  • Rhetorical questions — imply the answer the writer expects ("How can we stand by and do nothing?" implies we should act)
  • Sarcasm and irony — saying the opposite of what is meant ("What a brilliant idea" when describing a disaster)
Demonstrate with a model non-fiction extract. Show how to identify the writer's attitude by looking at loaded language, rhetorical devices, and what the writer chooses to emphasise or omit.
Worked Example

Extract: "The so-called 'improvements' to our high street have transformed a once-thriving community hub into a soulless corridor of charity shops and betting offices. The council speaks of 'regeneration' — yet the only thing being regenerated appears to be the bank accounts of property developers."

Question: What is the writer's attitude to the high street improvements?
Answer: The writer is strongly opposed to the improvements. Evidence: they use "so-called" before 'improvements', which implies sarcasm — they do not believe the changes are genuine improvements. The phrase "soulless corridor" is highly negative, suggesting the street has lost its character and warmth. The contrast between "once-thriving" and the current state implies decline rather than progress. The final sentence uses irony — "regeneration" should mean renewal for the community, but the writer implies it only benefits property developers, suggesting corruption or greed.

Practice Task

Read this extract from an article about social media:

"Parents worried about their children spending hours on social media might pause to consider their own screen time habits before lecturing the younger generation. The average adult spends four hours a day on their phone — barely two hours less than the teenagers they criticise. Perhaps the real question is not 'how do we get our children off their phones?' but 'why have we created a world where a glowing screen is the most interesting thing in the room?' Social media did not invent loneliness. It merely gave it a Wi-Fi connection."

1. What is the writer's attitude to parents who criticise their children's social media use?
2. What does the final sentence suggest about social media's role in society?

1. The writer is critical of these parents, suggesting they are hypocritical. Evidence: "might pause to consider their own screen time habits before lecturing" implies parents should reflect on their own behaviour first. The comparison of adult and teen screen time (four hours vs "barely two hours less") undermines the parents' moral authority to criticise.
2. The final sentence suggests that social media does not cause loneliness — it merely provides a new way for an existing problem to manifest. The personification of loneliness "given a Wi-Fi connection" is both witty and poignant, implying that human disconnection is an older, deeper problem than technology alone can explain.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a "tone word bank" (critical, supportive, sarcastic, concerned, amused, angry, neutral). Use colour-coding: highlight positive connotation words in green, negative in red.
Stretch:
Give a text where the writer's attitude is more subtle or ambivalent. Ask students to identify where the writer seems to hold two views simultaneously.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Attitude Spectrum

Read a sentence from a text. Students place themselves on an imaginary spectrum from "strongly agrees" to "strongly disagrees" with the writer's implied viewpoint. Discuss the range of positions.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can identify the writer's implied attitude using specific language evidence
  • Getting there: Can identify the general attitude but struggles to explain how the text creates it
  • Not yet: Reads only the surface meaning and cannot identify implied attitudes

Lesson 3: Deducing Meaning from Context

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Word Detective

Display a sentence with an unfamiliar word: "The children were absolutely euphoric when school was cancelled." Ask students to deduce the meaning from context — what does euphoric mean? How does the context help? (School cancelled + children = happy/excited. Euphoric must mean extremely happy.)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach students to deduce the meaning of unfamiliar words using context clues:
  • Definition clue — the word is defined nearby: "A pomologist — a person who grows fruit — explained the process."
  • Synonym clue — a similar word is used nearby: "The room was opulent, luxurious and dripping with gold."
  • Antonym clue — an opposite word provides contrast: "Unlike her gregarious sister, Rosa was painfully shy."
  • Example clue — examples illustrate the meaning: "The garden was full of perennials — roses, lavender, and foxgloves that returned every year."
  • Tone/mood clue — the overall feeling of the passage suggests meaning: if everything else is negative, an unknown word is probably negative too.
Demonstrate with a passage containing several challenging vocabulary items. Model the thought process: read the sentence, look at surrounding sentences, consider the overall tone, make a deduction, check it makes sense. Important exam skill: the question "What does [word] suggest?" is testing inference. You need to explain the connotation, not just the dictionary definition.
Worked Example

Extract: "The house had a certain moribund quality about it. The paint peeled in long strips like dead skin. The garden had surrendered to weeds, and the windows — what few panes remained intact — stared out like cloudy, cataract eyes."

Question: What does "moribund" suggest about the house?
Answer: "Moribund" suggests the house is in a state of near-death or extreme decay. Evidence: the surrounding description reinforces this — peeling paint compared to "dead skin", a garden that has "surrendered", and broken windows like "cataract eyes" (eyes affected by a disease of old age). All these comparisons link the house to illness, death, and aging, so "moribund" must mean something closely associated with dying or lifelessness.

Practice Task

Read this extract and answer the questions about word meanings.

"The politician's vacuous promises failed to resonate with an electorate growing increasingly incredulous. Town hall meetings descended into cacophony as citizens, no longer willing to be placated by platitudes, demanded substantive answers to the crises affecting their communities."

1. What does "vacuous" suggest about the politician's promises?
2. Explain what "incredulous" tells us about the electorate's state of mind.
3. What does "cacophony" suggest about the town hall meetings?

1. "Vacuous" suggests the promises are empty, meaningless, or lacking substance. The context supports this: the citizens are "no longer willing to be placated by platitudes" — platitudes are also empty statements, reinforcing that the promises have no real content.
2. "Incredulous" suggests the electorate no longer believes what they are told. They have become sceptical and distrustful, refusing to accept statements at face value. The context — they "demanded substantive answers" — shows they want real substance, not more empty words.
3. "Cacophony" suggests the meetings were loud, chaotic, and discordant. The word implies a mixture of harsh, clashing voices rather than orderly debate, reflecting the citizens' anger and frustration.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a "context clues checklist" card. Highlight the challenging word and underline surrounding context clues. Use texts where context strongly supports meaning.
Stretch:
Give words where the dictionary meaning and the contextual connotation differ slightly. Ask students to explain the difference between "what the word means" and "what the word suggests in this context."

Plenary (5 minutes)

Guess the Word

Display five sentences with blank spaces where a word has been removed. Students must guess the missing word based on context clues. Discuss the different strategies used.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can deduce word meanings accurately from context and explain the process of deduction
  • Getting there: Can guess meanings but struggles to explain how context helped
  • Not yet: Cannot use context clues and relies on guessing randomly

Lesson 4: Inference in Exam Questions

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Spot the Difference

Display two answers to the same inference question — one that earns full marks and one that does not. Students identify which is better and why. Model answer focuses on evidence and explanation; poor answer gives an opinion without evidence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Explain the command words used in inference questions across exam boards:
  • "What does this suggest about…" — make an inference and support it
  • "How does the writer show…" — identify techniques and explain their effect
  • "Explain what is meant by…" — define in context, showing understanding of connotation
  • "What impression of…" — identify implied qualities or feelings
Teach the "Inference Sandwich" structure:
  1. Top bun — your inference (what you think it suggests)
  2. Filling — the textual evidence (a quote or reference)
  3. Bottom bun — your explanation (how the evidence proves your inference)
Model writing full-inference answers to exam-style questions. Show common pitfalls: answers that are too vague ("the writer uses good language"), answers without evidence, and answers that only repeat the question. For 4-mark questions, teach students to provide TWO inferences, each with evidence and explanation.
Worked Example

Question (4 marks): "How does the writer create a sense of the narrator's loneliness in paragraphs 2 and 3?"

Weak answer (1 mark): "The writer shows the narrator is lonely because they are on their own."

Strong answer (4 marks): "The writer creates a powerful sense of loneliness through the narrator's relationship with silence. In paragraph 2, 'the silence pressed against her ears' — personifying silence as something physical and oppressive suggests that the emptiness around her is overwhelming, not peaceful. In paragraph 3, she 'counted the tiles on the ceiling' — a trivial, repetitive activity that implies she has nothing and no one to occupy her time. The use of the past tense 'had counted' and 'had named' (referring to cracks in the wall) suggests this is a long-established routine of isolation, not a one-off experience."

Practice Task

Read this extract and answer the 4-mark question below.

"The old man sat on the bench every day at the same time, rain or shine. He never spoke to anyone, and no one spoke to him. He watched the world go by — children running to school, couples walking hand in hand, friends laughing at café tables — with an expression that might have been contentment or might have been resignation. It was impossible to tell. He brought a paper bag with him each day. Inside, two biscuits, always the same kind. He ate them slowly, breaking each one into exactly four pieces. At precisely three o'clock, he folded the bag, put it in his pocket, and walked away. He never once looked back."

How does the writer create a sense of routine and isolation in this passage? (4 marks)

The writer creates a sense of routine through the precision of the man's actions: he sits "at the same time", breaks biscuits "into exactly four pieces", and leaves "at precisely three o'clock". This extreme regularity suggests his life is so empty that these small rituals are all he has to structure his day. The isolation is shown through the contrast between the man's solitary routine and the lively world around him — "children running", "couples walking hand in hand", "friends laughing". The man is a still, silent observer watching connection from which he is excluded. The phrase "no one spoke to him" confirms this is not just solitude by choice but loneliness imposed by circumstance. The detail that "he never once looked back" reinforces the finality of his isolation — there is nothing behind him worth returning to.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a model answer and ask students to identify where the writer uses evidence, inference, and explanation. Use sentence starters: "This suggests…" and "The writer creates this impression by…"
Stretch:
Give students a range of inference questions from different exam boards. Ask them to identify which are worth 1, 2, or 4 marks and explain why, then answer them to the appropriate depth.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Peer Marking

Students swap their 4-mark answers and use a rubric to award marks: 1 mark for a clear inference, 2 marks for inference + evidence, 3 marks for inference + evidence + explanation, 4 marks for two developed inferences.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can write fully developed inference answers using the sandwich structure, with two inferences for 4-mark questions
  • Getting there: Can make inferences and provide evidence but needs to develop explanations further
  • Not yet: Gives opinions rather than inferences, or provides no evidence to support claims

Homework Suggestions

  • Read a short story and write down three inferences about the main character, each with textual evidence
  • Find five unfamiliar words in a newspaper article and deduce their meanings using context clues
  • Complete two GCSE-style inference questions (1-mark and 4-mark) using the PIE technique
  • Write a "day in the life" diary entry for a character from a text, inferring what their routine might be like based on clues in the original
