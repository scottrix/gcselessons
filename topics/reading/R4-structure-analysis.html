R4: Structure Analysis

Foundation Higher All Boards

Analyse how writers organise texts, use structural techniques, and create meaning through the arrangement and ordering of content.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Identify and explain the effect of structural features (openings, endings, paragraphing, shifts)
  • Understand how chronological and non-chronological ordering creates effects
  • Analys the effect of viewpoints and perspectives on structure
  • Write about structure using appropriate terminology
  • Analyse how texts are organised for different purposes and audiences

Prerequisites

  • Confidence with language analysis (R3)
  • Understanding of basic narrative structure (beginning, middle, end)
  • Awareness of different text types and their conventions

Materials & Equipment

  • Range of fiction and non-fiction texts showing varied structures
  • Structure analysis vocabulary list
  • Timeline templates for plotting narrative events
  • Example texts with deliberate structural choices (e.g. circular narratives, in medias res)

Lesson 1: Structural Features

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Opening Gambit

Display five different opening lines from famous novels. Students rank them from most to least interesting. Discuss: what makes an opening effective? How does the structure of the opening set up the rest of the text?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach the key structural features students must understand:
  • Opening — first paragraph/lines. Hooks the reader, establishes tone, introduces key elements. Questions: What do we learn immediately? What questions are raised?
  • Ending — final paragraph/lines. Resolves or deliberately leaves unresolved. Questions: Does it answer the questions raised? Does it mirror the opening (circular structure)?
  • Paragraphing — paragraph breaks signal shifts in time, place, topic, or perspective. Short paragraphs create emphasis and pace.
  • Shifts — changes in time, place, or focus. "Later that day…" marks a time shift. Moving from one character's perspective to another marks a perspective shift.
  • Circular structure — the ending mirrors the opening, suggesting a journey or return
  • Cumulative structure — building up through a series of events or examples, often for emphasis
  • In medias res — starting in the middle of the action, then explaining later
Key exam skill: always ask "WHY has the writer structured the text this way?" The structure is always a deliberate choice.
Worked Example

Question: "How does the writer use the opening of the text to engage the reader?"
Model answer: The writer opens with a short, dramatic sentence: "The house was on fire." This in medias res opening drops the reader directly into a crisis without context, creating immediate tension and urgency. The brevity of the sentence — just five words — contrasts with the detailed description that follows, making it punchy and memorable. The reader is compelled to continue reading to find out: whose house? How did it start? Is anyone inside? The writer has deliberately withheld information to create a question that the reader needs answered.

Practice Task

Analyse the structure of this opening paragraph:

"It was the last day of term, and nobody seemed to notice. The corridors were the same shade of institutional green they had always been. The lockers still bore the scratches and stickers of years gone by. But for Maya, something had shifted. She could feel it in the way the light fell through the windows — thinner somehow, as if the building itself knew that everything was about to change."

1. How does the writer establish a contrast in this opening?
2. What structural choices does the writer make to create intrigue?

1. The writer contrasts the mundane normality of the school ("same shade of institutional green", "lockers still bore the scratches") with Maya's heightened awareness that "something had shifted". This contrast between the ordinary setting and Maya's internal sense of change creates intrigue — the reader wants to know what Maya knows that the other characters do not.
2. The writer uses two structural choices: (1) starting with the end of term — a moment of transition — suggests the story will be about change or endings, and (2) ending with a metaphor ("as if the building itself knew") that personifies the school, suggesting the change will be significant enough to affect the physical environment. The paragraph moves from general observation (the school) to specific focus (Maya's perspective), narrowing the reader's attention.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a structural features checklist. Highlight structural features in the text with different colours (openings = blue, shifts = red, endings = green).
Stretch:
Give a text with an unusual structure (e.g. reverse chronology, multiple timelines). Ask students to explain why the writer chose this structure and what effect it creates.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Structure Map

Give students a short text. They draw a simple flowchart showing the structural journey: where does it start, what shifts occur, how does it end? Compare maps as a class.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can identify structural features and explain their effect on the reader
  • Getting there: Can identify features but explains effect only briefly
  • Not yet: Cannot identify structural features beyond basic paragraphing

Lesson 2: Chronology and Ordering

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Jumbled Story

Give students five sentences from a story in random order. They must rearrange them into a logical sequence. Then discuss: what if we told the story in a different order? How would the effect change?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach how ordering creates meaning:
  • Chronological order — events told in time order. Creates clarity and allows the reader to follow a journey or development.
  • Non-chronological order — events told out of time. Creates mystery, suspense, or allows contrasts between past and present.
  • Flashback — jumping back in time. Reveals backstory, explains character motivation, creates dramatic irony.
  • Flash-forward — jumping forward. Creates anticipation or foreboding.
  • Circular narrative — ending where the story began. Suggests nothing has changed, or the character has come full circle.
  • Frame narrative — a story within a story. Creates distance, reliability questions, or layers of meaning.
In non-fiction, ordering also matters: presenting the strongest argument first (primacy effect) or last (recency effect) changes impact. Ordering evidence chronologically builds a case; ordering by importance emphasises key points.
Worked Example

Question: "How does the writer's decision to tell the story out of chronological order create tension?"
Model answer: The writer begins the story at the end — the narrator standing at the edge of the cliff — before jumping back in time to explain how they arrived there. This non-chronological structure creates tension because the reader already knows the outcome (the cliff) but not the journey. Each flashback raises the stakes: "What happened to make this person end up here?" The structure transforms what might be a simple story of events into a mystery that the reader is compelled to solve.

Practice Task

Analyse how chronological and non-chronological ordering are used in these extracts:

Extract A (chronological): "First, the letter arrived. Then came the phone call. By evening, she had packed her bags. The next morning, she was gone."

Extract B (non-chronological): "She was gone by morning. Hours earlier, she had packed her bags with trembling hands. Earlier still, the phone call had sealed her decision. And it had all begun with a single letter."

How does the different ordering create different effects?

Extract A uses chronological order to create a sense of calm, deliberate progression. The sequence words ("first", "then", "by evening", "next morning") suggest the character is methodical and in control — each action follows logically from the last. The reader experiences events as the character does, step by step.

Extract B reverses the chronology, starting with the outcome ("she was gone") before working backwards. This creates immediate intrigue — the reader knows the destination but not the reason. "Trembling hands" and "sealed her decision" hint at emotional turmoil, and the phrase "it had all begun" explicitly frames the letter as the catalyst. The reverse order transforms a simple sequence of events into a mystery: the reader must piece together the emotional journey alongside the factual one.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide timeline templates. Students plot events on a timeline, then discuss how reordering them would change the story's effect.
Stretch:
Give a text with multiple time shifts. Students create a "timeline map" showing the order of events as told vs the order they actually happened, then analyse the gap.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Reorder Challenge

Display a paragraph from a story. Students rewrite the first two sentences in reverse order. Discuss how this changes the opening effect.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can analyse how chronological and non-chronological ordering create different effects on the reader
  • Getting there: Can identify the ordering used but struggles to explain its effect
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between chronological and non-chronological structure

Lesson 3: Structure in Non-Fiction and Poetry

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Layout Analysis

Display a newspaper article and a blog post on the same topic. Students identify structural differences: headline style, paragraph length, use of images, subheadings, quotes. Discuss how structure reflects purpose and audience.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Extend structural analysis beyond narrative fiction:
  • Non-fiction structure: Headings, subheadings, bullet points, statistics placement, quotation placement, call-to-action endings. Non-fiction is structured to inform, persuade, or argue — structure serves purpose.
  • Poetic structure: Stanza breaks, line breaks, enjambment, couplets, rhyming patterns. Poets use structure to control pace, emphasis, and meaning.
  • Speech structure: Opening hook, building arguments, addressing counterarguments, powerful closing statement. Speeches are structured to persuade and inspire.
Key exam skill for AQA Paper 2: explain how the structure of a non-fiction text is designed for its purpose and audience. Who is the intended reader? How does the structure cater to them?
Worked Example

Question (AQA Paper 2): "How is this text structured to engage the reader?"
Model answer: The writer structures the article to build a compelling argument. The opening uses a shocking statistic ("Every twelve minutes, a family loses their home") to immediately grab the reader's attention — a rhetorical strategy designed to create emotional investment before the argument begins. The body of the article alternates between personal stories (individual families) and broader statistics (national figures), a structure that alternates between emotional appeal and factual evidence. This structure is effective because it prevents the reader from becoming desensitised to either type of evidence alone. The article ends with a direct address to the reader ("You can make a difference"), a structural choice that shifts responsibility from the general to the personal, leaving the reader feeling both empowered and obligated.

Practice Task

Analyse the structure of this poem excerpt:

"I have eaten
the plums
that were in
the icebox

and which
you were probably
saving
for breakfast

Forgive me
they were delicious
so sweet
and so cold"

(William Carlos Williams, "This Is Just to Say")

How does the poet use line breaks and stanza breaks to create effect?

The poet uses short, enjambed lines that break mid-phrase ("that were in / the icebox"), which forces the reader to slow down and consider each small detail. This mimics the deliberate, almost ritualistic act of eating the plums — each line is savoured, just as the plums were. The stanza breaks create pauses that suggest hesitation or guilt — the speaker pauses before admitting "Forgive me" and before the final justification "so sweet / and so cold." The final two lines, isolated in their own stanza, land with a quiet defiance that undercuts the apology — the speaker is not truly sorry, and the sweetness of the plums is worth the transgression.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a non-fiction features word bank (headline, subheading, bullet point, statistic, quotation). Students match features to their structural purpose.
Stretch:
Give two poems with different structures (e.g. one with regular stanzas, one free verse). Compare how the structural choices reflect the content.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Purpose Match

Display four texts with different structures. Students match each to its purpose (persuade, inform, entertain, argue) and explain how the structure reveals the purpose.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can analyse structural choices in both fiction and non-fiction, explaining how structure serves purpose and audience
  • Getting there: Can identify structural features but needs support explaining their purpose
  • Not yet: Only identifies basic features (paragraphs, headings) without explaining effect

Homework Suggestions

  • Read the opening chapter of a novel and analyse three structural choices the author makes
  • Compare the structure of a newspaper article with a blog post on the same topic
  • Analyse the structure of a poem, focusing on line breaks and stanza breaks
  • Write two versions of the same story — one chronological, one non-chronological — and explain which is more effective
