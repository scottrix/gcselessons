R6: Comparison

Foundation Higher All Boards

Compare texts effectively by identifying similarities and differences in language, structure, viewpoint, and purpose.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Identify similarities and differences between two texts
  • Compare language, structure, and viewpoint across texts
  • Use comparative connectives effectively (however, similarly, whereas, while)
  • Write comparative paragraphs that discuss both texts
  • Evaluate which text is more effective and why

Prerequisites

  • Confidence with language analysis (R3)
  • Confidence with structure analysis (R4)
  • Understanding of evaluation (R5)

Materials & Equipment

  • Paired texts on similar topics (non-fiction sources)
  • Comparative connectives list
  • Venn diagram templates
  • Model comparative paragraphs
  • Highlighters for identifying similarities and differences

Lesson 1: Comparison Fundamentals

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Spot the Difference

Display two descriptions of the same place (e.g. a city). Students list similarities and differences in a Venn diagram. Discuss: comparison is not just listing — it is analysing WHY things are similar or different.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach the fundamentals of comparison:
  • Compare, don't list — "Both writers use simile, but Writer A's similes are humorous while Writer B's are disturbing" is stronger than "Writer A uses simile. Writer B also uses simile."
  • Use comparative connectives: similarly, likewise, also (for similarities); however, whereas, while, in contrast, on the other hand (for differences)
  • Compare by category: compare language with language, structure with structure, viewpoint with viewpoint — not language in Text A then structure in Text A
  • Consider WHY: why do the writers differ? Different purpose? Different audience? Different context? Different viewpoint?
Model a "blocked" comparison (discussing one text then the other) vs an "integrated" comparison (discussing both texts in the same paragraph). Show that integrated comparison is stronger.
Worked Example

Blocked (weaker): "Writer A uses the metaphor 'time is a river' to suggest that time flows constantly. Writer B uses the metaphor 'time is a prison' to suggest that time traps us."

Integrated (stronger): "While Writer A uses the metaphor 'time is a river' to suggest that time flows inevitably forward, Writer B presents a contrasting view with the metaphor 'time is a prison', suggesting that time confines and traps us. Both writers use metaphor to abstract the concept of time, but the effects are opposed: Writer A implies acceptance of time's passage, while Writer B implies resistance and frustration."

Practice Task

Compare these two extracts about technology:

Writer A: "Our phones have become extensions of ourselves — we reach for them instinctively, as naturally as reaching for a glass of water."

Writer B: "We have become slaves to our devices, checking them compulsively like lab rats pressing a lever for a reward that never comes."

Both writers use simile to compare our relationship with technology to an involuntary physical action, but with very different implications. Writer A's comparison to "reaching for a glass of water" suggests technology has become a natural, essential part of life — as necessary and unremarkable as hydration. The word "instinctively" reinforces this, implying the behaviour is now biological rather than chosen. Writer B, by contrast, uses the simile "like lab rats pressing a lever" to dehumanise the phone user, comparing them to experimental animals conditioned to repeat a behaviour. The phrase "reward that never comes" suggests futility and manipulation. While Writer A presents technology use as natural and neutral, Writer B presents it as a form of control — and the contrast in similes reveals fundamentally different attitudes to the same behaviour.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a comparison frame: "Writer A [technique] while Writer B [technique]. Both/However, the effect is…"
Stretch:
Give three texts to compare. Students must identify which two are most similar and explain why, then compare all three.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Connective Challenge

Display two statements about different texts. Students choose the best comparative connective: Similarly? However? While? Both? Discuss why each connective works.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can compare texts using integrated paragraphs and appropriate connectives
  • Getting there: Can identify similarities and differences but compares by listing rather than integrating
  • Not yet: Discusses each text separately without comparison

Lesson 2: Comparing Viewpoints

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Two Perspectives

Display two letters to a newspaper about the same issue — one for, one against. Students identify the writers' viewpoints and how they differ. Introduce: viewpoint comparison is central to AQA Paper 2.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
In AQA Paper 2, Question 4 asks students to compare viewpoints across two non-fiction sources. Teach:
  • Identify the viewpoint: what is each writer arguing/believing/suggesting?
  • Compare the strength: which writer is more convincing and why?
  • Compare the approach: do they use similar or different techniques to present their viewpoint?
  • Compare the tone: is one writer more emotional, more measured, more angry, more hopeful?
Key exam technique: organise your comparison by EFFECT (what the writers achieve) rather than by TEXT (what each writer does). This forces integrated comparison. Teach students to structure their answer: introduce both viewpoints, then write two to three comparative paragraphs, each focusing on a different aspect (language, evidence, tone, etc.).
Worked Example

Question (AQA Paper 2 Q4): "Compare how the writers present their viewpoints about school uniforms."
Model answer:
Both writers are critical of school uniform policies, but they adopt markedly different approaches. Writer A presents a measured, evidence-based argument, citing research from Finland and Sweden where uniform policies have been abandoned, and noting that "there is no credible evidence that uniforms improve academic performance." This factual approach gives the argument credibility and authority. Writer B, by contrast, takes a more personal, emotional approach, describing how their daughter "felt like a prisoner in a grey polyester cage" and arguing that uniforms "crush individuality before it has a chance to bloom." While Writer A's language is restrained and academic, Writer B's is vivid and emotive — "prisoner" and "crush" are violent verbs that create a sense of oppression.

Despite these differences, both writers share a core belief: that uniforms restrict personal freedom. They converge on this point even as their methods diverge. However, Writer A is arguably more persuasive for an academic audience, while Writer B may resonate more with parents and students who have personal experience of uniform policies. The difference in approach likely reflects different intended audiences — Writer A is writing for a policy document, while Writer B is writing for a parenting magazine.

Practice Task

Compare the viewpoints of these two writers about homework:

Writer A: "Homework is an outdated relic of Victorian education that persists through institutional inertia. Studies consistently show that beyond thirty minutes, additional homework has negligible impact on achievement. We are exhausting our children for no measurable gain."

Writer B: "Homework teaches discipline, time management, and the ability to work independently — skills that no exam can measure but every employer values. Remove homework and you remove one of the few opportunities students have to learn responsibility."

Writer A opposes homework, presenting it as an outdated practice with no measurable benefit. The metaphor "outdated relic of Victorian education" suggests homework belongs to a past era and has not evolved with modern understanding. The phrase "negligible impact on achievement" uses academic language to create an evidence-based tone, while "exhausting our children" introduces emotional appeal. Writer B supports homework, but argues for its value beyond academic results — they present it as character-building. The tricolon "discipline, time management, and the ability to work independently" creates a sense of cumulative benefit, while "skills that no exam can measure" positions homework as valuable precisely because it teaches what cannot be tested. The key difference is that Writer A focuses on measurable outcomes (academic achievement) while Writer B focuses on intangible qualities (character, responsibility) — and this fundamental disagreement about what education is for underlies their opposing viewpoints.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a viewpoint identification frame: "Writer A believes… because… Writer B believes… because… The key difference is…"
Stretch:
Give texts where the writers partially agree. Students must identify areas of agreement and disagreement and explain the significance of each.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Agree or Disagree?

Read statements summarising two writers' viewpoints. Students identify which is accurate and which misrepresents the writer. Discuss how subtle misrepresentation can change meaning.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can compare viewpoints accurately, identifying both agreement and disagreement, with analysis of how each writer presents their position
  • Getting there: Can identify the viewpoints but comparison is surface-level
  • Not yet: Describes each viewpoint separately without comparing them

Lesson 3: Extended Comparison Writing

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Planning Framework

Display a comparison planning template: aspect → Text A evidence → Text B evidence → comparison. Students plan a comparison paragraph in 3 minutes.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach extended comparison writing for 8-mark questions:
  • Introduction: State both viewpoints/techniques and give an overall comparison ("Both writers present X, but while Writer A focuses on Y, Writer B emphasises Z")
  • Body paragraphs: Each paragraph compares one aspect — language, evidence, tone, structure — across both texts
  • Conclusion: Make a final evaluative judgement about which is more effective and why
Teach the "double APIE" — for each paragraph, analyse both texts before reaching a comparative conclusion. Show students how to avoid the trap of only discussing one text per paragraph. Common mistakes: writing "Text A does this. Text B does that." without comparison; only comparing one aspect; forgetting to evaluate.
Worked Example

8-mark model answer structure:
Introduction: Both writers present climate change as an urgent crisis, but they target different emotions — Writer A appeals to logic while Writer B appeals to fear.
Paragraph 1: Compare the opening strategies. Writer A begins with a statistic ("1.5 degrees of warming has already occurred"), establishing an authoritative, factual tone. Writer B begins with a metaphor ("the earth is running a fever"), creating an immediate emotional connection through personification. Both are effective hooks, but they engage different readers — the analytical thinker vs the emotionally responsive reader.
Paragraph 2: Compare the language choices. Writer A's vocabulary is precise and scientific ("anthropogenic emissions", "pre-industrial levels"), which builds credibility with an informed audience. Writer B uses accessible, vivid language ("our children will inherit a scorched and bitter world"), which creates emotional impact but sacrifices precision. The contrast suggests different purposes: Writer A is arguing for policy change, while Writer B is arguing for personal behaviour change.
Conclusion: Both texts are effective for their intended audiences. Writer A is more persuasive for policymakers; Writer B is more likely to motivate individual action. The most effective response to climate change may require both approaches.

Practice Task

Write a full 8-mark comparison answer for this question: "Compare how the two writers present the experience of moving to a new country."

Writer A: "The airport was a universe of foreign sounds. I stood in the arrivals hall, suitcase in hand, understanding nothing — not the announcements, not the signs, not the faces passing me by. I had never felt so completely alone."

Writer B: "New country, new self. I threw myself into the chaos of the unfamiliar with a reckless joy that surprised even me. Every wrong turn was an adventure, every mispronunciation a comedy. I was lost, and I loved it."

Both writers present the experience of arriving in a new country as disorienting, but with opposite emotional responses. Writer A focuses on isolation and incomprehension — the airport is "a universe of foreign sounds", a metaphor that emphasises the overwhelming scale of the unfamiliar. The tricolon "not the announcements, not the signs, not the faces" reinforces the completeness of the character's alienation, while "understanding nothing" confirms total disconnection. The writer creates a sense of vulnerability and fear.

Writer B, by contrast, presents the same experience — being "lost" in a new country — as exhilarating. The opening sentence "New country, new self" uses parallel structure to suggest transformation, while "reckless joy" combines impetuousness with happiness, implying that embracing uncertainty is liberating. The list "every wrong turn was an adventure, every mispronunciation a comedy" reframes potential embarrassments as positive experiences. Where Writer A sees isolation, Writer B sees freedom.

The key difference lies in attitude: Writer A presents the new country as something to be endured, while Writer B presents it as something to be embraced. Both are realistic portrayals of relocation, and the contrast suggests that the experience depends entirely on individual perspective — a point that both writers would likely agree on, despite their different responses.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a planning template with sentence starters for each section: Introduction, Body 1, Body 2, Conclusion. Reduce to 6 marks if needed.
Stretch:
Give three texts and ask students to write a 12-mark comparison, discussing which two are most similar and which is the outlier.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Self-Assessment

Students highlight their own answer in two colours: yellow for comparison statements (containing "while", "however", "both", "similarly"), and pink for single-text descriptions. Count the ratio — ideally comparison should outweigh description.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can write a structured 8-mark comparison with integrated paragraphs, appropriate connectives, and evaluative conclusion
  • Getting there: Can compare but paragraphs are not fully integrated
  • Not yet: Discusses each text separately without meaningful comparison

Homework Suggestions

  • Find two newspaper articles about the same topic and write a comparison of the writers' viewpoints
  • Complete an AQA Paper 2 Question 4 from a past paper (8-mark comparison)
  • Create a "comparison toolkit" of useful connectives and sentence starters
  • Compare two poems or song lyrics, focusing on how each presents a similar theme
