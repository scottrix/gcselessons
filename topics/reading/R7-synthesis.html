R7: Synthesis

Foundation Higher All Boards

Combine and integrate information from multiple sources to create a coherent summary or response. Essential for AQA Paper 2 Question 3.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Select relevant information from multiple sources
  • Combine ideas from different texts into a coherent response
  • Summarise effectively without losing key meaning
  • Use appropriate vocabulary to integrate source material
  • Synthesise for the specific requirements of exam questions

Prerequisites

  • Confidence with retrieval skills (R1)
  • Ability to identify main ideas in a text
  • Basic note-taking skills

Materials & Equipment

  • Multiple short texts on the same topic (news articles, reports, blog posts)
  • Highlighters and note-taking templates
  • Model synthesis responses
  • Venn diagrams for comparing sources

Lesson 1: What is Synthesis?

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Recipe Analogy

Explain: synthesis is like following a recipe. You take individual ingredients (ideas from different sources) and combine them to create something new (a coherent response). The result is more than the sum of its parts.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Synthesis is specifically tested in AQA Paper 2 Question 3: "Summarise how [topic] is presented in both texts." Teach:
  • Read all sources first before writing anything
  • Highlight key points from each source, using different colours for each
  • Identify overlaps: where do sources agree? Where do they differ?
  • Group related ideas: combine points from different sources about the same sub-topic
  • Write in your own words — do not copy sentences from the sources
  • Attribute when needed: "Source A argues… while Source B suggests…"
Key exam technique: the question usually asks you to summarise "how [topic] is presented" — this means you need to cover the range of viewpoints, not just pick one. Model the process: read Source A, extract key points. Read Source B, extract key points. Identify where they overlap. Group by theme. Write a synthesis paragraph that combines ideas from both sources.
Worked Example

Question (AQA Paper 2 Q3): "Summarise how both sources present the impact of social media on young people."
Source A key points: social media causes anxiety; 40% of teens report feeling worse about themselves after using it; comparison culture damages self-esteem; but it also provides community for isolated young people.
Source B key points: social media is not inherently harmful; parental guidance matters; platforms should do more to protect users; young people are more resilient than adults think; but cyberbullying is a serious problem.

Synthesis response: Both sources acknowledge that social media can have negative effects on young people, though they differ in emphasis and blame. Source A focuses on the psychological impact, noting that social media causes anxiety and damages self-esteem through comparison culture, while Source B takes a broader view, arguing that social media is not inherently harmful but that platforms must do more to protect users. However, both sources recognise that social media can provide benefits: Source A notes it offers community for isolated young people, while Source B argues that young people are more resilient than adults assume. The sources converge on the idea that the problem is not social media itself but how it is used — Source A implicitly and Source B explicitly.

Practice Task

Read these two short sources and synthesise how they present the value of reading:

Source A: "Reading for pleasure is in decline among teenagers, with only 35% regularly reading for enjoyment. Researchers at the University of Sussex found that reading reduces stress by up to 68% — more than listening to music or going for a walk. Books build vocabulary, empathy, and critical thinking in ways that screens simply cannot replicate."

Source B: "The death of reading has been greatly exaggerated. While traditional book reading may be declining, teenagers are reading more than ever — blogs, social media posts, fan fiction, and online articles. The format has changed, but the fundamental act of reading remains alive. What matters is not what we read but that we read."

Both sources address the value of reading among young people, but they define "reading" differently. Source A focuses on traditional reading — books — and argues that its decline is concerning because reading reduces stress, builds vocabulary, and develops empathy in ways that screens cannot. The statistic "68% stress reduction" provides authoritative evidence for reading's benefits. Source B challenges the premise that reading is in decline, arguing that teenagers read extensively in digital formats — blogs, fan fiction, social media — and that the format matters less than the act itself. The sources agree that reading is valuable, but disagree about whether traditional reading is being replaced or simply evolving. Source A implies nostalgia for print, while Source B embraces the digital transformation of literacy.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a note-taking template with columns for each source. Students extract key points before attempting to write. Reduce to one source initially.
Stretch:
Give three or four sources on the same topic. Students must synthesise all of them, identifying where sources agree, disagree, and add unique perspectives.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Key Points Round-Up

Each student writes one key point from each source on a sticky note. Stick them on the board and group them by theme. Discuss how the points relate to each other.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can combine ideas from multiple sources into a coherent, organised synthesis response
  • Getting there: Can extract key points from sources but struggles to combine them
  • Not yet: Summarises only one source or copies directly from the text

Lesson 2: Synthesis in the Exam

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Four Corners

Display four statements about a topic drawn from different sources. Students move to corners labelled "strongly agree", "agree", "disagree", "strongly disagree". Discuss: whose viewpoint is each statement from?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Focus on exam technique for AQA Paper 2 Q3 (8 marks):
  • Read the question carefully: what specific aspect of the topic must you synthesise?
  • Read all sources: A, B, and C (if applicable)
  • Plan your response: group ideas by theme, not by source
  • Write three to four paragraphs covering the full range of viewpoints
  • Use attribution: "Source A…", "According to Source B…", "Source C suggests…"
  • Cover the whole range: do not just pick the viewpoint you agree with
Mark scheme: 8 marks requires synthesis of both sources, covering a range of ideas, with ideas clearly grouped and expressed accurately. 5-6 marks for partial synthesis. 3-4 marks for summary of one source only. Teach students to check: have I included ideas from ALL sources? Have I grouped related ideas? Have I used my own words?
Worked Example

Question (8 marks): "Summarise how both sources present the challenges faced by young carers."
Model answer: Both sources highlight the significant challenges young carers face, though they emphasise different aspects. Source A focuses on the emotional toll, describing how young carers "carry the weight of adult responsibilities before they have finished growing up" and noting that many feel isolated from their peers. The metaphor of carrying weight suggests a burden that is both physical and psychological. Source B takes a more systemic approach, arguing that young carers are "invisible to the institutions that should support them" and criticising schools for failing to identify pupils with caring responsibilities. While Source A focuses on the personal experience, Source B focuses on institutional failure.

Both sources agree that young carers sacrifice their own childhood. Source A states this directly: "They miss out on the ordinary things — playing football, going to parties, simply being children." Source B reinforces this with a statistic: 39% of young carers report missing school. The sources converge on the idea that society is failing these young people, but diverge on where the blame lies — Source A implies the burden falls on families, while Source B explicitly criticises the system.

Practice Task

Complete this synthesis task under timed conditions (15 minutes for 8 marks):

"Summarise how both sources present attitudes towards environmental protest."

Source A (newspaper article): "Environmental protesters who block roads and disrupt public services are alienating the very people they need to win over. While their cause is just, their methods are counterproductive. A recent poll found that 62% of the public now views climate protesters negatively — a figure that has doubled in two years."

Source B (blog post): "Disruption is the language of the unheard. When polite requests are ignored, when petitions are filed and forgotten, when scientists are sidelined by politicians — what else is left? History shows that every major social change required disruption. The suffragettes were hated in their time too."

Both sources address attitudes towards environmental protest, but from fundamentally different perspectives. Source A takes a pragmatic stance, arguing that while the cause is just, disruptive tactics are counterproductive. The statistic that 62% of the public views protesters negatively is presented as evidence that the movement is losing public support. The phrase "alienating the very people they need to win over" suggests protesters are undermining their own goals. Source B defends disruption as a necessary tactic, arguing that "the language of the unheard" — a phrase echoing Martin Luther King — frames protesters as people whose legitimate concerns have been ignored. Source B explicitly invokes historical precedent, comparing protesters to the suffragettes to suggest that methods condemned in their time may later be celebrated. While Source A evaluates protest by its practical effectiveness, Source B evaluates it by its moral necessity. Both sources agree that climate change is important, but they disagree profoundly about how to campaign for change.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a synthesis planning sheet: Source A key points | Source B key points | Themes that overlap | Write grouped response
Stretch:
Give three sources with conflicting viewpoints. Students must synthesise all three and write a conclusion about the range of views expressed.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Self-Check

Students read their own response and tick: ✓ mentions Source A, ✓ mentions Source B, ✓ uses own words, ✓ groups ideas by theme, ✓ covers the full range of viewpoints. Discuss any gaps.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can write an 8-mark synthesis response covering both sources, with ideas grouped by theme and expressed in own words
  • Getting there: Can synthesise but may miss some viewpoints or group poorly
  • Not yet: Summarises only one source or copies directly from the text

Homework Suggestions

  • Find two articles about the same topic and write a synthesis paragraph combining their key points
  • Complete an AQA Paper 2 Question 3 from a past paper
  • Create a "synthesis toolkit" with useful phrases: "Both sources…", "While Source A…, Source B…", "The sources agree that…"
  • Read three editorials about a current issue and write a one-paragraph synthesis of the different viewpoints
