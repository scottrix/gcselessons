Lesson 3: Application: essay structure

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: AO4 (5-10%), Top Tip. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What are the three parts of an essay?

Answer: Introduction, Body Paragraphs, Conclusion. Introduction sets up argument, body develops with evidence, conclusion summarises and evaluates.

Q2: What does PEEL stand for?

Answer: Point - Make your argument. Evidence - Quote the text. Explain - Analyse the quotation. Link - Connect to question/theme/context.

Q3: What should an introduction include?

Answer: Introduction should: identify text and writer, respond to the question, state your main argument (thesis). Keep it 2-3 sentences.

Q4: What should you avoid in a conclusion?

Answer: Don't introduce new points. Don't just repeat the introduction. Don't say "In conclusion." The conclusion should summarise and evaluate, not add new ideas.

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response 25 marks: How does Priestley present the theme of social responsibility in "An Inspector Calls"? <div class="

Priestley presents social responsibility as both a moral imperative and a practical necessity, structuring the play to expose the consequences of its absence. The Inspector's declaration that "we are members of one body" employs an organic metaphor that positions society as an interdependent organism — the collective pronoun "we" deliberately includes the Birlings, dismantling their belief that responsibility is individual rather than communal. Structurally, Priestley arranges each confession chronologically, building dramatic intensity as Eva's descent becomes more desperate with each Birling's actions. Sheila's realisation after the Inspector's questioning — "But these girls aren't cheap labour — they're people" — marks the play's turning point, as the exclamatory shift from abstraction to humanity signals her moral awakening. Written in 1945 but set in 1912, the play's dramatic irony allows Priestley to condemn pre-war individualism from a post-war perspective where collective responsibility had proven essential for national survival.
