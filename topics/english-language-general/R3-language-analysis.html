Lesson 2: Core Concepts: language analysis

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on language analysis. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Definition: Language analysis examines how writers choose and use words, phrases and literary devices to create specific effects on the reader.
Alliteration: Repetition of consonant sounds for emphasis or musicality. "The slippery snake slithered silently."
Onomatopoeia: Words that sound like what they describe. "Crash, bang, hiss, whisper."
Sibilance: Repeated 's' sounds creating a hissing effect. "The soft, silky snake slid through the grass."
P: oint - Make a clear point about the language used E vidence - Quote the specific words or phrase E xplain - Explain the effect created L ink - Link to the writer's purpose or reader's response
Examiner's Tip: Focus on 2-3 language features in depth rather than listing many superficially. Always link to effect on the reader.

Just naming devicesAlways explain the effect

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: What is the difference between a metaphor and a simile?

Answer: A metaphor directly says something IS something else, while a simile compares using "like" or "as".

Lesson 3: Application: language analysis

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Alliteration, Onomatopoeia, Sibilance. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What is the difference between a metaphor and a simile?

Answer: A metaphor directly says something IS something else, while a simile compares using "like" or "as".

Q2: What does PEEL stand for?

Answer: Point, Evidence, Explain, Link - a structure for writing analytical paragraphs.

Q3: Why should you avoid writing "this makes the reader want to read on"?

Answer: It's too vague and generic - you need to explain the specific emotional or intellectual response created.

Q4: What is emotive language and why do writers use it?

Answer: Emotive language uses words designed to provoke emotional responses, used to manipulate the reader's feelings about a topic.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: language analysis

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on language analysis from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Use precise terminology but focus on effects | Embed short quotations into your sentences | Consider the writer's purpose and context | Focus on depth over breadth - analyse fewer features in more detail | Use phrases like "this suggests", "this conveys", "this implies" | Always link back to the question focus
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often "feature spot" - naming devices without explaining their effect. Wrong: "The writer uses a metaphor to describe the city." Correct: "The metaphor 'the city was a beast' dehumanises the urban environment, presenting it as predatory and threatening, as though it could consume the individual." Students often use vague reader-response phrases that say nothing specific. Wrong: "This makes the reader want to read on" or "This is effective." Correct: "This creates a sense of unease in the reader, as the familiar landscape becomes threatening." Students often analyse quotations that are too long, losing focus on the key words.
