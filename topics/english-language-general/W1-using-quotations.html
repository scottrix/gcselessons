Lesson 2: Core Concepts: using quotations

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on using quotations. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Remember: Every point you make needs evidence. "Show, don't just tell." A quotation shows exactly what the writer did.
Technique: Introduce the quotation with your own words, then quote only the key words. The quotation becomes part of YOUR sentence.
Recommended: 5-8 key quotations per major text. Choose quotations that can be used for multiple themes or characters. Quality over quantity.
Top Tip: In closed-book exams, quotation recall under time pressure is critical. Spend the first 5 minutes of planning time jotting down the 3-4 quotations you will use. This prevents you forgetting key quotes mid-essay and ensures every paragraph has evidence ready before you start writing.

Lesson 3: Application: using quotations

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Technique, Recommended, Top Tip. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What makes a good quotation for analysis?

Answer: Good quotations are: rich in language techniques (metaphor, imagery), significant to character/theme, concise (short enough to analyse word by word), and flexible (can be used for multiple points).

Q2: What's the difference between dropping and embedding a quotation?

Answer: Dropping: quotation stands alone as separate sentence. Embedding: quotation is part of your sentence structure. Embedded quotations flow naturally and show stronger integration.

Q3: How many quotations should you memorise per text?

Answer: Recommended 5-8 key quotations per text. Choose quotations that work for multiple themes/characters. Quality over quantity - better to know a few well than many poorly.

Q4: After analysing language, what should you link to?

Answer: After analysing language, link to: themes (what idea is explored), character development, and context (when/why written, audience response).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

Extended question: Full-Mark Response 25 marks: How does Shakespeare present Macbeth's struggle with his conscience in Act 1, Scene 7? <div class="

Shakespeare presents Macbeth's conscience as a powerful but ultimately insufficient restraint against ambition. In his soliloquy, Macbeth acknowledges he has "no spur to prick the sides of my intent, but only vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself." The equestrian metaphor is devastatingly precise: "spur" implies motivation, yet Macbeth concedes he has "no spur" — his only driver is "vaulting ambition," where the adjective "vaulting" suggests something that leaps beyond its natural limits. The verb "o'erleaps" conveys reckless overextension, while the reflexive "itself" reveals that ambition's destructive energy will ultimately turn inward. Shakespeare reinforces Macbeth's moral awareness through his recognition that Duncan's "virtues will plead like angels," where the simile elevates the king's goodness to a divine standard against which Macbeth's "bloody instructions" are measured. The juxtaposition of "virtues" and "bloody" underscores the moral chasm Macbeth must cross.
