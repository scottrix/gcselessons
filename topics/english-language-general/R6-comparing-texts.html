Lesson 2: Core Concepts: comparing texts

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on comparing texts. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Definition: Comparison involves identifying similarities and differences between texts, considering perspectives, methods, and how writers convey their viewpoints.
Write about both texts in each paragraph, using comparative connectives to link them.
Examiner's Tip: You must compare BOTH perspectives AND methods. Use evidence from both texts equally. Spend about 20-25 minutes on this question.
Comparison Exam Strategy: This is AQA Paper 2 Q4 (16 marks) - spend 20-25 minutes. You must compare BOTH perspectives AND methods. Use an integrated approach: write about both texts in every paragraph, using comparative connectives (however, similarly, whereas, in contrast). Read both texts first and annotate them side by side. Identify each writer's viewpoint, then find 2-3 points of comparison. Examiners reward responses that weave comparison throughout, not those that write about each text separately.

Lesson 3: Application: comparing texts

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Examiner&#x27;s Tip, Comparison Exam Strategy. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What are the two main things you should compare between texts?

Answer: Perspectives (viewpoints) and methods (how they convey those viewpoints).

Q2: What is the difference between an integrated approach and writing about texts separately?

Answer: Integrated approach compares within each paragraph using connectives; separate approach writes about each text individually without comparing.

Q3: Name three comparative connectives for showing differences.

Answer: However, in contrast, whereas, on the other hand, unlike.

Q4: Why should you use evidence from both texts?

Answer: To show you can analyse both texts and make valid comparisons - it proves your comparison is based on evidence.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: comparing texts

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on comparing texts from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Read both texts and identify each writer's viewpoint | Use comparative connectives throughout | Write about both texts in each paragraph | Compare methods as well as perspectives | Quote from both texts equally | Look for similarities AND differences | Don't analyse language in depth - focus on comparison
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often write about each text in separate paragraphs, treating them as two mini-essays. Wrong: A paragraph about Source A, then a paragraph about Source B with no links between them. Correct: "While Source A presents city life as vibrant and exciting through the metaphor of 'a symphony of noise,' Source B rejects this romanticism, describing the city as 'a concrete cage' - the contrast in metaphors reveals fundamentally opposed perspectives." Students often compare content only, ignoring the writers' methods. Wrong: "Source A says the city is good. Source B says the city is bad." Correct: "Source A persuades through sensory celebration, while Source B uses emotive language
