Lesson 2: Core Concepts: spelling rules and patterns

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on spelling rules and patterns. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

AO6 Requirement: Use a range of vocabulary and sentence structures for clarity, purpose and effect, with accurate spelling and punctuation. Spelling errors can cost you marks across all writing questions.
Exceptions to remember: weird, seize, neither, either, foreign, height, their, heir
English Terms: metaphor, simile, personification, alliteration, onomatopoeia, juxtaposition, semantic field, foreshadowing, narrative, perspective
Tip: When in doubt, write the word down. Often your eye will catch an error that your brain misses.
Spelling Exam Strategy: Spelling accuracy directly affects your AO6 mark (16 marks). Frequent errors can cap you at Level 1 or 2, regardless of how good your content is. Before the exam: Memorise the top 10 commonly misspelt words (accommodation, separate, definitely, necessary, occurrence, beginning, disappear, environment, privilege, embarrass). Learn the mnemonic for each. During writing: If you're unsure how to spell a word, use a simpler alternative you can spell correctly. "Huge" is better than a misspelt "collosal". Accuracy always beats ambition. Proofreading (last 5 minutes): Read through your work specifically looking for spelling errors. Check homophones (their/there/they're, your/you're, its/it's) — these are the most common and most easily fixed errors. Mark allocation: AO6 Level 4 (13-16 marks) requires "accurate" spelling. Level 2 (5-8 marks) describes "some errors." Even 3-4 spelling mistakes can drop you a whole level. What examiners look for: Consistent accuracy, especially with high-frequency words and subject terminology. They don't expect perfection on every obscure word, but basic errors (their/there, seperate) are heavily penalised.

Lesson 3: Application: spelling rules and patterns

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: AO6 Requirement, Exceptions to remember, English Terms. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Which is correct: "their book" or "there book"?

Answer: "their book" - their shows possession (belonging to them)

Q2: Spell the word meaning "to influence": affect or effect?

Answer: "affect" - affect is the verb meaning to influence

Q3: What is the correct spelling: neccessary or necessary?

Answer: necessary - one c, two s

Q4: Add the suffix -ing to the word "hop" - what do you get?

Answer: hopping - double the consonant after a short vowel

Q5: Complete this sentence: "You're going to lose / loose your keys."

Answer: lose - lose means to misplace; loose means not tight

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

Exam Tips: Proofread your work - leave 2-3 minutes at the end | Circle words you're unsure about and come back to them | Use alternative words if you can't spell something | Memorise the top 10 commonly misspelt words | Practise homophones - they catch many students out | Check subject-specific vocabulary in context
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often write "could of" and "should of" instead of "could have" and "should have". Wrong: "I could of gone to the party if I had finished my work." Correct: "I could have gone to the party if I had finished my work." — "of" sounds like the contracted "'ve" but is grammatically incorrect. Students often confuse homophones — words that sound the same but have different meanings and spellings. Wrong: "Their going to the park over they're to play with there dog." Correct: "They're going to the park over there to play with their dog." — they're = they are, there = place, their = belonging to them. Students often misspell double-letter words by forgetting one of the pairs. Wrong
