Lesson 2: Core Concepts: critical style

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on critical style. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Shift: Move from describing ("This shows Macbeth is ambitious") to analysing ("Shakespeare presents ambition as destructive through the metaphor of...").
Always ask: HOW does the writer create this effect? Never just state WHAT happens - explain HOW it's achieved through language, form, and structure.
Top Tip: A Grade 9 critical style is invisible — the examiner does not notice your language because it sounds authoritative and effortless. The quickest way to lose marks is to write informally or to describe the plot. Before the exam, memorise 5-7 analytical verbs (presents, implies, conveys, subverts, reinforces, emphasises, challenges) and commit to never using "shows" or "uses" again.

Lesson 3: Application: critical style

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Shift, Always ask, Top Tip. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What's the difference between describing and analysing?

Answer: Describing: telling what happens ("Macbeth kills Duncan"). Analysing: explaining HOW and WHY the writer presents it ("Shakespeare uses the motif of blood to show Macbeth's guilt after the murder").

Q2: Give three examples of analytical verbs.

Answer: Analytical verbs: presents, portrays, depicts, suggests, implies, highlights, emphasises, conveys, reinforces, contrasts (any three).

Q3: What does AO1 require about personal response?

Answer: AO1 requires an "informed personal response" - your interpretation supported by evidence, evaluating the writer's methods and their effects.

Q4: Rewrite informally: "This shows that Macbeth is really ambitious and it makes him do bad things."

Answer: Formal: "Shakespeare presents Macbeth's ambition as a destructive force that leads to moral corruption."

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

Extended question: Full-Mark Response 30 marks: How does Dickens present Scrooge's transformation in "A Christmas Carol"? <div class="

Dickens presents Scrooge's transformation not as a sudden conversion but as a forced moral awakening, achieved through the progressive intensification of supernatural visitation. The initial description of Scrooge as "solitary as an oyster" deploys a simile that encapsulates both his self-imposed isolation and his hidden potential — the oyster, after all, may contain a pearl. However, it is the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come that catalyses genuine change: the vision of Scrooge's death, greeted with "obsequious" greed by those who strip his belongings, confronts him with the ultimate consequence of his misanthropy — not merely loneliness but erasure. Dickens's shift from the satirical caricature of Stave One to the emotionally vulnerable figure who begs "Assure me that I yet may change these shadows" constitutes a carefully managed structural arc. For Dickens's Victorian readership, many of whom subscribed to Malthusian economics that justified neglect of the poor, Scrooge's transformation served as a direct rebuke: if the most hardened capitalist can be redeemed, no reader can claim moral exemption from social responsibility. Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often confuse personal opinion with personal response, writing informally about their feelings. Correct: Shakespeare renders Macbeth's psychological disintegration with compelling intensity, evoking a complex pity that is nonetheless tempered by the audience's awareness of his moral agency.
