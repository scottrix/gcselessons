Lesson 2: Core Concepts: understanding perspectives

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on understanding perspectives. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Definition: A perspective or viewpoint is the writer's attitude, opinion or stance on a subject. It's what they think or feel about the topic they're writing about.
Word Connotations: The feelings and associations words evoke "Stubborn" vs "determined" - same behaviour, different attitude
Emotive Language: Words designed to provoke emotional responses "Murder" vs "killing" vs "death" - different emotional weights
Bias: A tendency to favour one side or perspective, often unfairly.
Examiner's Tip: Always support your identification of perspective with evidence. Quote words that reveal the writer's attitude. Consider WHY they hold this view - what might have shaped their perspective?
Perspective Exam Strategy: Understanding perspectives underpins several questions across both papers. For AQA Paper 2, identifying the writer's viewpoint is essential for Q3, Q4, and Q5. When approaching a text, first ask: "What does the writer think about this topic, and how do I know?" Look for emotive language, word connotations, direct statements, and what the writer chooses to include or omit. Examiners reward students who identify not just what the writer thinks, but how strongly they feel and why they might hold that view.

Lesson 3: Application: understanding perspectives

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Word Connotations, Emotive Language, Bias. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What is the difference between perspective and bias?

Answer: Perspective is a writer's viewpoint or opinion; bias is when that perspective unfairly favours one side, often by misrepresenting or ignoring opposing views.

Q2: Name three language features that might reveal a writer's perspective.

Answer: Emotive language, word connotations, tone, direct statements, rhetorical devices.

Q3: How can word connotations show perspective?

Answer: Words with positive connotations show approval; negative connotations show disapproval. The choice reveals the writer's attitude toward the subject.

Q4: What questions should you ask to understand a writer's perspective?

Answer: What does the writer think? How do they feel? Why might they think this? What evidence supports my interpretation?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

The writer conveys a deeply critical perspective on social media, presenting it as a corrosive force that damages young people's mental health and distorts their sense of self. This viewpoint is established immediately through the damning metaphor describing social media as "a hall of mirrors," suggesting it reflects distorted versions of reality that trap young people in a cycle of comparison and inadequacy. The writer's contempt is further revealed through their choice of emotive language: "toxic," "obsessive," and "insidious" create a semantic field of harm that positions social media as actively dangerous rather than merely unhelpful. The writer also conveys their perspective through structural manipulation. They open with a personal anecdote about a teenager who "deleted every app and felt reborn," placing this transformative experience at the start to anchor the reader's sympathy before introducing their broader argument. The writer then builds their case through a series of rhetorical questions - "How many hours are stolen by infinite scroll?" - which are designed not to invite genuine debate but to lead the reader inexorably toward the writer's conclusion. Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often confuse the writer's perspective with the topic of the text. Wrong: "The writer's perspective is about education." Correct: "The writer's perspective is that the education system is failing disadvantaged students, revealed through their angry tone and choice of words like 'abandoned' and 'neglected.'" Students often state the writer's viewpoint without showing how it is conveyed. Wrong: "The writer doesn't like technology." Correct: "The writer is deeply critical of technology, describing smartphones as 'electronic leashes' - the metaphor suggesting they trap and control people rather than liberate them."
