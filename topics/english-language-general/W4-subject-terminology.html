Lesson 2: Core Concepts: subject terminology

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on subject terminology. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Important: Terminology helps you discuss WHAT writers do. But don't just name techniques - always explain their EFFECT.
Don't overuse terminology: A strong essay doesn't name every technique. Use terminology when it helps you explain HOW a writer creates effect.
Top Tip: AO2 specifically rewards "relevant subject terminology where appropriate" — the keyword is "appropriate." A Grade 9 response uses terminology surgically: name the technique, then immediately explain its effect. Never list terms. An essay that names three techniques with precise effect analysis will always outscore one that names ten techniques with no explanation.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: What's the difference between a metaphor and a simile?

Answer: Metaphor says something IS something else ("life's a walking shadow"). Simile compares using "like" or "as" ("solitary as an oyster"). Metaphors create stronger, more direct connections.

Lesson 3: Application: subject terminology

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Don&#x27;t overuse terminology, Top Tip. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What's the difference between a metaphor and a simile?

Answer: Metaphor says something IS something else ("life's a walking shadow"). Simile compares using "like" or "as" ("solitary as an oyster"). Metaphors create stronger, more direct connections.

Q2: What is a soliloquy and why is it useful for playwrights?

Answer: A soliloquy is a speech where a character alone on stage speaks their thoughts aloud. It reveals inner thoughts directly to the audience, creating intimacy and showing psychological state.

Q3: What does dramatic irony mean?

Answer: Dramatic irony is when the audience knows more than the characters. It creates tension, makes audience complicit, and highlights characters' mistakes or blindness.

Q4: Name three structural terms and their effects.

Answer: Any three from: enjambment (creates flow), caesura (creates pause), cyclical structure (suggests repetition), foreshadowing (creates anticipation), climax (height of tension).

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response 30 marks: How does Shakespeare use language, form, and structure to present Macbeth's psychological deterioration? <div class="

Shakespeare charts Macbeth's psychological deterioration through an escalating sequence of soliloquies that progressively fragment his rational voice. In the early "dagger" soliloquy, the metaphor of a "dagger of the mind" suggests a mind still capable of distinguishing illusion from reality — the premodifying noun "mind" acknowledges the vision is internal. By Act V, however, the soliloquy "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow" abandons metaphor for nihilistic declaration: "Life's but a walking shadow, a poor player." The structural shift from complex figurative language to direct, declarative despair mirrors Macbeth's collapse from tortured conscience to emotional emptiness. The repetition in "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow" — an example of epistrophe — creates a relentless, mechanical rhythm that structurally enacts the futility Macbeth now feels: time continues without meaning. Furthermore, the iambic pentameter, which has governed the play's verse, is maintained even as the content becomes hollow — Shakespeare's form insists on order even as Macbeth's mind disintegrates, heightening the tragic irony for an audience who witnesses his public composure masking private ruin.

Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often name a technique without explaining what it does to the reader, treating terminology as a checklist to tick off. Wrong: Shakespeare uses a metaphor here. This is an example of imagery. Correct: The metaphor transforms Macbeth's ambition into a force that "o'erleaps itself," suggesting to the audience that ambition is inherently self-destructive — it exceeds its own boundaries and brings about its own collapse. Students often confuse similar terms, particularly metaphor and simile, or soliloquy and monologue. Wrong: Macbeth's soliloquy "Is this a dagger" is a monologue where he talks to Lady Macbeth.
