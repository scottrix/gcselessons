Lesson 2: Core Concepts: paragraphing

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on paragraphing. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

AO5 & AO6: Paragraphing demonstrates your ability to organise ideas effectively. Examiners look for well-structured writing with clear paragraph breaks and coherent organisation.
Purpose: A strong topic sentence sets up your paragraph, guides the reader, and makes your argument clear from the start.
Tip: Use long paragraphs to develop ideas and short paragraphs for impact. A one-sentence paragraph can be powerful in narrative writing.
Paragraphing Exam Strategy: Good paragraphing shows the examiner you can organise your ideas — it's essential for both AO5 (structure) and AO6 (cohesion). Before writing: Plan your paragraph structure. For an argument, know what each paragraph will argue. For a narrative, know the TiP ToP shifts. For an article, know which point each paragraph makes. During writing: Use topic sentences to open paragraphs. Link each paragraph to the previous one using connectives or referencing. Vary paragraph length — a short, one-sentence paragraph can create dramatic impact. Mark allocation: AO5 (24 marks) rewards well-organised writing with clear paragraph structure. AO6 (16 marks) rewards cohesion — how well your paragraphs link together to form a coherent whole. What examiners look for: Clear paragraph breaks at logical points. Each paragraph developing one main idea. Topic sentences that guide the reader. Connectives and references that create smooth transitions between paragraphs.

Lesson 3: Application: paragraphing

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: AO5 &amp; AO6, Purpose, Tip. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What does TiP ToP stand for?

Answer: Time, Place, Topic, Person - the four main reasons to start a new paragraph

Q2: Write a topic sentence for a paragraph about a writer's use of rhetorical questions.

Answer: Example: "The writer employs rhetorical questions to engage the reader and prompt reflection."

Q3: When should you start a new paragraph in dialogue?

Answer: Start a new paragraph for each new speaker

Q4: What does PEEL stand for?

Answer: Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link - a structure for analytical paragraphs

Q5: Why might a writer use a one-sentence paragraph?

Answer: To create impact, shock, emphasis, or a dramatic moment

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: paragraphing

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on paragraphing from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Plan your paragraphs before you start writing | Use TiP ToP to know when to start new paragraphs | Start each paragraph with a clear topic sentence | Use connectives to link paragraphs smoothly | Vary paragraph length for effect | Check dialogue has proper paragraph breaks | Leave time to review structure at the end
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often write one giant paragraph with no breaks, making the response impossible to follow. Wrong: A 300-word block of text with no paragraph breaks, mixing multiple ideas, time shifts, and speakers. Correct: Use TiP ToP (Time, Place, Topic, Person) to find natural paragraph breaks. Each paragraph should develop one main idea or moment. In dialogue, start a new paragraph for each new speaker. Students often start every paragraph the same way, creating a monotonous rhythm. Wrong: "Firstly... Secondly... Thirdly... Furthermore... In conclusion..." — every paragraph opens with a generic connective. Correct: Vary your paragraph openers — start with a topic sentence, a reference
