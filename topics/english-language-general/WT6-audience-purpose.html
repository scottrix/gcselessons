Lesson 2: Core Concepts: audience and purpose

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on audience and purpose. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Golden Rule: Every piece of writing should be shaped by who will read it (audience) and what you want to achieve (purpose). These two factors determine your tone, vocabulary, structure, and style.
What age group are they? What do they already know about the topic? What is their likely attitude toward the subject? What tone will engage them? What vocabulary level is appropriate?
Top Tip: The exam usually requires a formal or semi-formal tone. However, you must still adapt your writing to the specific audience - a speech to teenagers will be different from a letter to an MP, even if both are formal.
Consider: Age appropriateness - younger audiences need simpler vocabulary Subject knowledge - experts understand technical terms Cultural awareness - references must be accessible Tone requirements - emotive words for persuasion, neutral for information
Examiner's Tip: The exam question will tell you your audience and purpose. Read them carefully! A speech "to your school assembly" needs a different tone than "to a local council meeting." Circle the audience and purpose, then plan your tone and style before writing.
Audience and Purpose Exam Strategy: Understanding audience and purpose is the foundation of every transactional writing task. Get this wrong and your entire response loses direction. Before writing (first 2 minutes): Circle the audience and purpose in the question. Ask yourself: Who is reading this? What do I want them to think, feel, or do? Your answer to these two questions determines every choice you make — tone, vocabulary, structure, and register. During planning: Write your audience and purpose at the top of your plan. Check every paragraph against them: "Does this point serve my purpose? Is this language appropriate for my audience?" Mark allocation: AO5 (24 marks) explicitly rewards register matched to audience and purpose. If your tone doesn't suit your audience, you cannot score above Level 3. What examiners look for: A response that consistently shows awareness of who it's for and what it's trying to achieve. The tone, vocabulary, and structure should all reflect the audience and purpose identified in the question.

Lesson 3: Application: audience and purpose

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Golden Rule, Top Tip, Consider. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: How would your writing differ if addressing a headteacher versus addressing your peers?

Answer: Headteacher: formal register, respectful tone, evidence-based arguments, no contractions. Peers: informal register, relatable tone, personal examples, contractions acceptable.

Q2: Explain the difference between writing to persuade and writing to inform.

Answer: Persuade aims to convince the reader to agree or take action, using rhetorical devices and emotional appeal. Inform aims to provide facts objectively, with neutral language and clear explanations.

Q3: A speech about healthy eating - how would tone differ for primary school children versus adults?

Answer: Primary children: simple vocabulary, fun analogies, engaging tone, direct address like "your body needs..." Adults: more sophisticated vocabulary, statistics and evidence, respectful tone, addressing them as decision-makers.

Q4: Why is it important to avoid contractions in formal writing?

Answer: Contractions (can't, don't) are associated with speech and informal writing. Writing them in full (cannot, do not) creates a more formal, professional tone suitable for letters to officials, articles, and most exam tasks.

Exam-Style Question

Exam Tips: Circle or highlight the audience and purpose in the question | Plan your tone and style before writing | Match your vocabulary to your audience's knowledge level | Keep your purpose in mind throughout - every paragraph should serve it | Maintain consistent register - don't switch between formal and informal | Use direct address for speeches and articles to connect with audience | Check that your tone matches what the question asks for
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often ignore the specified audience and write generically. Wrong: Writing a speech for Year 7 students in the same formal, academic style you'd use for a letter to an MP. Correct: Adapt everything — a speech to Year 7s needs relatable examples, simpler vocabulary, direct address, and an engaging, enthusiastic tone. A letter to an MP needs formal register, evidence-based arguments, and respectful but firm language. Students often lose sight of purpose halfway through their response.
