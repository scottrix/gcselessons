Lesson 2: Core Concepts: punctuation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on punctuation. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Point: Punctuation is essential for AO6 (Technical Accuracy). Errors cost marks, but correct and sophisticated use can lift your writing to higher levels. Punctuation isn't just rules - it's about communication and effect.
Effect: Short sentences ending with full stops create a sense of finality, certainty, or drama. Each full stop is a pause, a breath, a moment of emphasis.
Comma Splice Error: Never join two complete sentences with just a comma. This is one of the most common punctuation errors in exams. Wrong: The door opened, she walked in. Right: The door opened. She walked in. Right: The door opened, and she walked in.
Semi-Colon vs Full Stop: A semi-colon suggests a closer connection than a full stop. Use it when you want to show that two sentences are thematically linked.
Em Dash: In creative writing, use the em dash (—) without spaces, or with spaces on either side. Be consistent throughout your work.
Speech Punctuation Rules: Open speech marks before the first word spoken Punctuation goes inside the closing speech marks Start a new line for each new speaker Use a comma, full stop, question mark, or exclamation mark inside the quotes Use a comma after speech tags like "she said" or "he asked"
TermMeaningExample
The dog wagged it's tail.The dog wagged its tail.
Her parent's are here.Her parents are here. (No possession - no apostrophe)
I went to get some pizza's.I went to get some pizzas. (Plural - no apostrophe)
The teams captain.The team's captain.
She ran! She couldn't believe it! The door opened! There he was!She ran. She couldn't believe it. The door opened. There he was.
Comma spliceThe door opened, she walked in.The door opened. She walked in. / The door opened, and she walked in.
Missing comma after fronted clauseWhen the bell rang everyone froze.When the bell rang, everyone froze.
Its/it's confusionThe dog licked it's paw.The dog licked its paw.

Practice (10 minutes)

Lesson 3: Application: punctuation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Point, Effect, Comma Splice Error. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Add the missing punctuation: "the door opened she walked in slowly and looked around"

Answer: "The door opened. She walked in slowly and looked around." OR "The door opened. She walked in, slowly, and looked around." (Several acceptable versions depending on intended pace and emphasis.)

Q2: What's the difference between these two sentences? "The students' books" vs "The student's books"

Answer: "The students' books" = the books belonging to multiple students (plural possession). "The student's books" = the books belonging to one student (singular possession).

Q3: When would you use a colon instead of a full stop?

Answer: Use a colon when the second part explains, illustrates, or expands on the first part. A full stop separates unrelated sentences. Colons create anticipation.

Q4: Correct the speech punctuation: "Hello she said how are you"

Answer: "Hello," she said. "How are you?" OR "Hello," she said, "how are you?"

Q5: Explain when to use a semi-colon instead of a comma.

Answer: Use a semi-colon to join two complete, related sentences. Use a comma to separate clauses within a sentence or items in a list. Never use a comma to join two complete sentences (comma splice).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: punctuation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on punctuation from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Leave 5 minutes at the end to proofread your work | Read aloud in your head - hear where pauses should be | Check every speech mark pair - open and close | Double-check its/it's, your/you're, their/they're | Use sophisticated punctuation (colons, semi-colons) for higher marks | But only use punctuation you're confident with - errors cost marks | Check apostrophes in every possessive noun | Verify commas after fronted subordinate clauses
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often create comma splices by joining two complete sentences with just a comma. Wrong: "The door opened, she walked in." Correct: "The door opened. She walked in." OR "The door opened, and she walked in." OR "The door opened; she walked in." Students often confuse "its" and "it's" — the most common apostrophe error in exams. Wrong: "The dog wagged it's tail. Its going to rain." Correct: "The dog wagged its tail. It's going to rain." — "its" = possession (no apostrophe), "it's" = it is (with apostrophe). Students often punctuate speech incorrectly, especially the comma after speech tags. Wrong: "Hello". She said. OR "Hello" she said. Correct: "Hello," she said. — The comma
