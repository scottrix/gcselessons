Lesson 2: Core Concepts: narrative writing

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on narrative writing. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Definition: Narrative writing tells a story with a beginning, middle, and end. It features characters, a plot with events, a setting, and often explores themes through conflict and resolution.
The Five-Part Structure: Exposition: Introduce characters, setting, situation Rising Action: Build tension through complications Climax: The turning point or peak of tension Falling Action: Consequences of the climax Resolution: Loose ends tied up, new equilibrium
Show Character Through Action: Instead of telling us someone is brave, show them facing fear. Instead of telling us someone is kind, show them helping another character.
Dialogue Rules: Each character should have a distinct voice Keep it realistic - people interrupt, trail off, use incomplete sentences Avoid info-dumps - don't use dialogue to explain the plot Use action beats instead of endless "he said, she said"
Exam Tip: Don't try to write a complete novel. Focus on ONE key moment or incident, and explore it fully. Quality over quantity.
Examiner's Tip: The best narratives focus on a single key moment and explore it in depth. Don't try to cover years of story - find the drama in one significant incident.

Lesson 3: Application: narrative writing

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: The Five-Part Structure, Show Character Through Action, Dialogue Rules. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What are the five parts of a classic story arc?

Answer: Exposition (introduction), Rising Action (complications), Climax (turning point), Falling Action (consequences), Resolution (conclusion).

Q2: What does "in medias res" mean and why is it effective?

Answer: "In medias res" means starting in the middle of the action. It's effective because it immediately grabs the reader's attention and creates intrigue from the first line.

Q3: Name two ways to reveal character without directly stating traits.

Answer: Through dialogue (how they speak), through actions (what they do), through reactions (how they respond to events), through interactions with others, through telling details of appearance or behaviour.

Q4: What is the difference between first person and third person narrative voice?

Answer: First person uses "I" and is limited to the narrator's perspective, creating intimacy. Third person uses "he/she/they" and can be more flexible, showing multiple perspectives or maintaining distance.

Q5: Rewrite this weak opening to be more engaging: "One day I went to the shops and something happened."

Answer: Example: "The shops were the last place I wanted to be on a Tuesday. But that was before I saw the man in the grey coat watching me from across the street."

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: narrative writing

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on narrative writing from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Plan before you write - know your beginning, middle, and end | Focus on ONE key incident - don't try to cover too much | Start with impact - hook the reader immediately | Show character through action and dialogue, not description | Use short sentences to build tension in key moments | Include a turning point - something must change | End with resonance - leave a final impression | Leave time to check spelling and punctuation
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often try to cover too much story in too little time. Wrong: Telling a life story — "She was born in 2005, went to school, then one day something happened when she was 15." Correct: Focusing on one key moment — "The letter arrived on a Tuesday. She recognised the handwriting immediately, and her hands began to shake." Students often tell emotions instead of showing them. Wrong: "He was really angry and upset about what happened." Correct: Revealing emotion through action — "He slammed his fist onto the table. The coffee cup jumped.
