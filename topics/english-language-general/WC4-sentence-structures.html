Lesson 2: Core Concepts: sentence structures

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on sentence structures. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Point: Sentence variety is essential for AO6 marks. Writing that uses only one sentence type feels monotonous and shows limited technical control. Varied sentences create rhythm, pace, and impact.
Short Sentences = Impact: A short sentence after several long ones stands out. It hits the reader harder because it's surrounded by more complex structures.
FANBOYS - Coordinating Conjunctions: For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So
Subordinating Conjunctions: Because, although, when, while, if, since, before, after, unless, as, though, whereas
Pace Control: Long sentences: Slow, flowing, reflective, descriptive Medium sentences: Balanced, explanatory, narrative Short sentences: Fast, tense, dramatic, emphatic
Rule of Three: Three short sentences in a row create a powerful rhythmic effect. More than three can feel choppy; fewer loses the impact.
TermMeaningExample
TimeSays when something happensWhen the bell rang, everyone froze.
PlaceSays where something happensWhere the road curved, a figure waited.
CauseGives a reasonBecause it was raining, they stayed inside.
ConditionSets a conditionIf you go out, take an umbrella.
ConcessionShows contrastAlthough she was tired, she kept working.
PurposeShows intentionShe saved money so that she could travel.
RelativeGives more informationThe man who lived next door moved away.
AdverbSlowly, she turned to face him.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: What is the difference between a simple, compound, and complex sentence?

Answer: Simple = one clause (one subject, one verb). Compound = two or more independent clauses joined by coordinating conjunctions (and, but, or). Complex = one independent clause and one or more dependent clauses joined by subordinating conjunctions (because, although, when).

Lesson 3: Application: sentence structures

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Point, Short Sentences = Impact, FANBOYS - Coordinating Conjunctions. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What is the difference between a simple, compound, and complex sentence?

Answer: Simple = one clause (one subject, one verb). Compound = two or more independent clauses joined by coordinating conjunctions (and, but, or). Complex = one independent clause and one or more dependent clauses joined by subordinating conjunctions (because, although, when).

Q2: Name three ways to vary sentence openings.

Answer: Start with: adverbs (Slowly, ...), -ing verbs (Running, ...), prepositions (Across the field, ...), connectives (However, ...), time phrases (Moments later, ...), subordinate clauses (Although tired, ...), similes (Like a ghost, ...).

Q3: When would you use short sentences in your writing?

Answer: For dramatic impact, to build tension, to speed up pace, to emphasise a key moment, to create contrast with longer sentences, for action scenes.

Q4: Rewrite this passage to vary the sentence structure: "She walked to the window. She looked outside. It was raining. She felt sad."

Answer: Example: She walked to the window. Looking outside, she saw the rain falling steadily. A wave of sadness washed over her.

Q5: What effect does a long sentence have on pace compared to a short sentence?

Answer: Long sentences slow the pace, creating a flowing, reflective, or descriptive feel. Short sentences speed up the pace, creating tension, urgency, and impact.

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

Exam Tips: Plan for variety - consciously use different sentence types | Save short sentences for key moments of impact | Use complex sentences to add depth and detail | Vary your sentence openers - avoid repetitive patterns | Read your work aloud - listen for rhythm and flow | Use sentence length to control pace intentionally | Check: do you have a mix of simple, compound, and complex? | Mark scheme specifically rewards "varied sentences"
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often write all sentences at the same length, creating a monotonous rhythm. Wrong: "She walked to the door. She opened it slowly. She looked outside. She saw the rain. She felt sad." Correct: Varying length for rhythm and effect — "She walked to the door. Opening it slowly, she peered into the grey morning. The rain fell steadily, each drop a small percussion on the already-sodden ground. Miserable." Students often create run-on sentences by joining clauses with commas.
