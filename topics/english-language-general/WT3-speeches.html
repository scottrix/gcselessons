Lesson 2: Core Concepts: speeches

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on speeches. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Features: A good speech is memorable, persuasive, and connects emotionally with the audience. It uses rhetorical devices to create impact and is designed to be heard, not just read.
Introduction (Opening): Hook: Start with a striking statement, question, or anecdote Establish your credibility and purpose Connect with your audience
Main Body: Present 3-4 key points Use one paragraph per point Support with evidence and examples Include rhetorical devices throughout Address counter-arguments if needed
Conclusion (Ending): Summarise your main message End with a powerful call to action Leave a lasting impression
Examiner's Tip: A speech needs to sound like a speech. Use techniques like direct address, rhetorical questions, and repetition. Your writing should feel different from an essay - it should have rhythm and be memorable when spoken aloud.
Speech Writing Exam Strategy: You have 45 minutes (40 marks: 24 AO5 + 16 AO6). A speech must sound like a speech when read aloud. First 5 minutes: Identify your audience and occasion. Plan: your hook, 3-4 key points, the rhetorical devices for each, and your closing call to action. Writing time: Open with a powerful hook. Use direct address ("you", "we") throughout. Include 3-4 different rhetorical devices. End with a memorable call to action. Mark allocation: AO5 (24 marks) rewards your content — clear viewpoint, effective speech conventions, and powerful structure. AO6 (16 marks) rewards technical accuracy — varied sentences for rhythm, appropriate vocabulary, correct SPaG. What examiners look for: A piece that sounds like a speech, not an essay. Direct address, rhetorical questions, repetition, and a clear speaking voice. The examiner should be able to "hear" this being delivered.

Lesson 3: Application: speeches

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Features, Examiner&#x27;s Tip, Speech Writing Exam Strategy. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What is the purpose of a "hook" in a speech opening?

Answer: A hook grabs the audience's attention immediately, making them want to listen. It could be a question, striking statement, or anecdote.

Q2: Name three rhetorical devices commonly used in speeches.

Answer: Rhetorical questions, repetition, rule of three, emotive language, direct address, imperatives - any three of these.

Q3: Write an opening paragraph for a speech about the importance of voting.

Answer: Your opening should include: a greeting, a hook (question or statement), direct address, and clear indication of the topic.

Q4: Why is direct address important in speeches?

Answer: Direct address creates a connection with the audience, making them feel involved and personally addressed. It transforms a speech from a lecture into a conversation.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: speeches

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on speeches from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Start with a strong hook - grab attention immediately | Use direct address throughout: "we", "you", "us" | Include a range of rhetorical devices | Vary your sentence lengths for rhythm and impact | End with a powerful, memorable conclusion | Include a clear call to action | Read your work aloud to check it sounds like a speech
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often write a speech that reads like an essay. Wrong: "In this speech I will discuss three reasons why recycling is important. Firstly, recycling reduces waste. Secondly, it saves energy." Correct: Use speech conventions — "Look around this room. Every single one of us has the power to change the future — starting today. How? By recycling. By refusing single-use plastic. By choosing the planet over convenience." Students often forget direct address, making the speech feel disconnected.
