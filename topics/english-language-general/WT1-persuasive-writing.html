Lesson 2: Core Concepts: persuasive writing

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on persuasive writing. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Definition: Persuasive writing aims to convince the reader to agree with your viewpoint or take a specific action. It uses a range of techniques to appeal to logic, emotion, and credibility.
Introduction: Hook the reader with a striking opening State your viewpoint clearly Outline what you'll discuss
Main Body: Present your strongest argument first Support each point with evidence Use a new paragraph for each point Address counter-arguments
Conclusion: Summarise your main points Reinforce your viewpoint End with a powerful call to action
Useful phrases for counter-arguments: "Some may argue that... However..." "Critics claim... But the evidence shows..." "While it's true that..., this doesn't mean..." "Opponents suggest... Nevertheless..."
Examiner's Tip: Plan before you write. Spend 5 minutes planning your arguments, evidence, and structure. This ensures a coherent, well-organised response.
TermMeaningExample
Being too aggressiveAlienates your audienceUse respectful, reasonable tone
Not supporting claimsArguments lack credibilityUse facts, statistics, examples
Ignoring the audienceWriting feels disconnectedUse direct address and appropriate tone
No counter-argumentShows one-sided thinkingAcknowledge and refute opposing views
Weak conclusionEnding lacks impactEnd with a clear call to action

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Name three persuasive techniques and explain how each works.

Answer: Rhetorical questions (make reader think), emotive language (evoke feelings), statistics (add credibility) - plus many other valid techniques.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Explain Back

Your student teaches the key points back to you without looking. Fill any gaps immediately.

Lesson 3: Application: persuasive writing

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Useful phrases for counter-arguments, Examiner&#x27;s Tip, Persuasive Writing Exam Strategy. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Name three persuasive techniques and explain how each works.

Answer: Rhetorical questions (make reader think), emotive language (evoke feelings), statistics (add credibility) - plus many other valid techniques.

Q2: Why is it important to address counter-arguments in persuasive writing?

Answer: It shows you've considered different viewpoints and strengthens your argument by preemptively refuting opposition.

Q3: Write an opening paragraph persuading your school to introduce a healthy eating policy.

Answer: Your paragraph should include: clear viewpoint, rhetorical question or hook, and persuasive techniques.

Q4: What is the purpose of a "call to action" in persuasive writing?

Answer: A call to action tells the reader exactly what you want them to do after reading, making your writing purposeful and memorable.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: persuasive writing

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on persuasive writing from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Always establish your viewpoint in the first paragraph | Use a range of techniques - don't repeat the same one | Support every point with evidence or examples | Address the counter-argument to show balanced thinking | End with a powerful, memorable call to action | Use paragraphs to structure your argument clearly | Check your spelling and punctuation carefully
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often list techniques without integrating them naturally. Wrong: "I will now use a rhetorical question. Why should we accept this? Now I will use a statistic. 80% of people agree." Correct: Weave techniques seamlessly into your argument — "Why should we accept a system that fails 80% of the people it claims to serve?" Students often state opinions without supporting evidence. Wrong: "Homework is bad and should be banned because students don't like it." Correct: Support every claim — "Homework should be reformed, not banned. Research from the University of London found that excessive homework — over two hours per night — correlates with increased stress and decreased acade
Stretch & Challenge (Grade 8-9):
  • Synoptic links: explain how persuasive writing connects to another English Language topic you have studied
  • Real-world: research one real-world use or example of persuasive writing
  • Critical: "What are the limitations of the models used in persuasive writing?"

Plenary (5 minutes)

Assessment Criteria
  • Got it: Confident explanation + correct worked examples
  • Getting there: Main points OK, needs support with detail
  • Not yet: Confused on key concepts - re-run Lesson 2

Homework & Consolidation

  • Consolidation: Re-answer any Lesson 3 practice questions answered incorrectly (20 mins)
  • Retrieval: Write flashcards for the key terms: Useful phrases for counter-arguments, Examiner&#x27;s Tip, Persuasive Writing Exam Strategy (10 mins)
  • Exam practice: One past-paper question on persuasive writing from the board websites (15 mins)
  • Extension: Explain persuasive writing to someone else in your own words (10 mins)
