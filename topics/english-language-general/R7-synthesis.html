Lesson 2: Core Concepts: synthesis

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on synthesis. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Definition: Synthesis means combining information from different sources to create a summary. You draw together ideas from both texts to show common themes or differences.
Step 1: Read both texts and identify the key information Step 2: Decide what each text says about the topic Step 3: Find similarities and/or differences Step 4: Write in your own words, using brief quotations Step 5: Organise logically - group related points
Remember: In synthesis questions, don't analyse language devices. Focus on summarising what each text says about the topic.
Examiner's Tip: This is NOT a language analysis question. Focus on what each text SAYS - the content and ideas. Use your own words and keep quotations brief. Spend about 10 minutes on this question.
Synthesis Exam Strategy: This is AQA Paper 2 Q2 (8 marks) - spend about 10 minutes. The critical distinction is: synthesis asks what the texts SAY, not HOW they say it. Do not analyse language devices - focus purely on content. Use your own words to summarise, with brief quotations for support. Weave both texts together in each sentence using comparative language. Examiners reward concise, accurate summaries that combine information from both sources.

Lesson 3: Application: synthesis

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Step 1, Examiner&#x27;s Tip, Synthesis Exam Strategy. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What is the main difference between synthesis and comparison?

Answer: Synthesis focuses on WHAT texts say (content); comparison focuses on HOW they convey meaning (methods).

Q2: Should you analyse language devices in a synthesis question?

Answer: No - synthesis is about summarising content, not analysing language.

Q3: How should you use quotations in synthesis?

Answer: Keep them brief and use them as evidence to support your summary points.

Q4: What sentence starter would you use to show a difference between texts?

Answer: "While Source A... Source B..." or "In contrast..." or "Unlike Source A, Source B..."

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: synthesis

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on synthesis from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Focus on content, not language analysis | Use evidence from both texts equally | Write in your own words where possible | Use synthesis sentence starters | Don't write too much - 8 marks needs a concise response | Group related points logically | Answer the specific question focus
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often analyse language devices in a synthesis question, treating it like a comparison question. Wrong: "Source A uses a simile to describe the school as 'a prison,' suggesting the writer feels trapped." Correct: "Source A presents school as a restrictive experience where students feel 'trapped,' while Source B describes it as a place of freedom where children 'explored and discovered.'" Students often write about each text separately instead of synthesising. Wrong: One paragraph entirely about Source A, then one paragraph entirely about Source B.
