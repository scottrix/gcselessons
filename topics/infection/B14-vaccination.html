B14: Vaccination

Foundation Higher All Boards

Learn how vaccines work to protect individuals and populations, understand herd immunity, and evaluate the benefits and risks of vaccination programmes.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Explain how vaccines stimulate the immune system to produce antibodies and memory cells
  • Describe the different types of vaccines (live-attenuated, inactivated, subunit, mRNA)
  • Explain the concept of herd immunity and its importance
  • Evaluate the benefits and risks of vaccination programmes

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of the immune response: lymphocytes, antibodies, memory cells (B13)
  • Knowledge of antigens and how they trigger immune responses

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Vaccination schedule poster
  • Data on disease incidence before and after vaccination programmes
  • Printed cards for herd immunity simulation

Lesson 1: How Vaccines Work

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Historical Success

Show a graph of measles cases in the UK before and after the MMR vaccine was introduced (1988). Ask: "What happened to the number of cases? Why?" Discuss the dramatic reduction in disease.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
What is a vaccine?
  • A vaccine contains antigens (or genetic instructions to make antigens) from a pathogen, but the pathogen cannot cause disease
  • When injected, the antigens stimulate the immune system
  • Lymphocytes recognise the antigens and produce specific antibodies
  • Memory cells are produced and remain in the body for years
  • If the real pathogen enters the body later, memory cells mount a rapid and effective immune response, preventing illness
Types of vaccines:
  • Live-attenuated: weakened form of the pathogen that can still replicate but doesn't cause disease. Produces a strong, long-lasting immune response. Example: MMR, chickenpox.
  • Inactivated (killed): pathogen has been killed (by heat or chemicals). Cannot replicate. Usually needs booster doses. Example: polio (Salk), rabies, hepatitis A.
  • Subunit (protein): only contains specific parts of the pathogen (e.g. surface proteins). Very safe. Example: hepatitis B, HPV, whooping cough (part of 5-in-1).
  • mRNA vaccines: contain genetic instructions (mRNA) that tell body cells to make a specific viral protein (antigen). The immune system responds to this protein. The mRNA is quickly broken down. Example: Pfizer and Moderna COVID-19 vaccines.
The UK childhood vaccination schedule includes: 8-in-1 (8 weeks), rotavirus (8 & 12 weeks), pneumococcal (12 weeks), Hib/MenC (12 months), MMR (12-13 months), 4-in-1 booster (3 years 4 months), etc.
Worked Example

"Explain how a vaccine provides long-term protection against a disease." Answer: (1) The vaccine contains antigens from the pathogen. (2) These antigens stimulate lymphocytes to produce specific antibodies. (3) Some lymphocytes become memory cells that remain in the body for years. (4) If the real pathogen enters later, the memory cells recognise it immediately. (5) They rapidly divide and produce large numbers of antibodies, destroying the pathogen before it causes illness.

Common Misconception

"Vaccines contain the disease-causing pathogen"

Vaccines contain antigens (or the instructions to make them) from the pathogen, but the pathogen has been weakened, killed, or only parts of it are used. The vaccine cannot cause the disease it protects against.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Simplify: "A vaccine is like a training exercise for your immune system. It shows your body what the enemy looks like so it can fight it next time." Focus on the key sequence: vaccine → antigens → antibodies + memory cells → future protection.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Compare the different types of vaccines in terms of effectiveness, duration of immunity, and safety. Research why some vaccines need boosters and others don't.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Key Sequence

Students write the sequence of events from vaccination to long-term immunity in four steps. Share and compare.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain how vaccines work and describe at least two types
  • Getting there: Understands that vaccines produce antibodies but cannot explain the role of memory cells
  • Not yet: Cannot explain how vaccines provide protection

Lesson 2: Herd Immunity and Evaluating Vaccination

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Population Question

Ask: "If 90% of people are vaccinated, are the unvaccinated 10% protected? How?" Introduce the concept of herd immunity.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Herd immunity (community immunity):
  • When a high enough proportion of the population is immune (through vaccination or previous infection), the pathogen cannot spread easily
  • Unvaccinated individuals (e.g. babies, immunocompromised people) are indirectly protected because there are fewer susceptible people to infect
  • The percentage needed for herd immunity varies by disease: measles needs ~95%, polio ~80-85%
  • If vaccination rates drop below the threshold, outbreaks can occur (e.g. measles outbreaks in communities with low MMR uptake)
Benefits of vaccination:
  • Protects the vaccinated individual from disease
  • Protects vulnerable people through herd immunity
  • Eradicated smallpox globally (1980)
  • Polio has been nearly eradicated
  • Reduces healthcare costs and burden on the NHS
Risks and concerns:
  • Side effects: most are mild (sore arm, fever, slight swelling). Serious side effects are extremely rare.
  • Allergic reactions: very rare, usually to a component of the vaccine
  • Thimerosal (mercury-based preservative) was removed from most childhood vaccines as a precaution — no evidence of harm at the levels used
  • The fraudulent 1998 Wakefield study linking MMR to autism was retracted and discredited — no scientific evidence supports this link
  • Some people cannot be vaccinated (immunocompromised) and rely on herd immunity
When evaluating, students should weigh the benefits to individuals and society against the very small risks.
Worked Example

"Explain herd immunity using measles as an example." Answer: Measles is highly contagious — one infected person can infect 12-18 others in an unvaccinated population. For herd immunity against measles, about 95% of the population needs to be immune. When this threshold is met, the virus cannot find enough susceptible people to sustain transmission. Unvaccinated people (e.g. babies too young for the vaccine) are protected because they are surrounded by immune people who cannot pass on the infection.

Common Misconception

"Herd immunity means you don't need to vaccinate"

Herd immunity only works if a HIGH ENOUGH proportion of the population is vaccinated. If too many people choose not to vaccinate, the herd immunity threshold is not reached and outbreaks occur. Herd immunity is a consequence of high vaccination rates, not a substitute for vaccination.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Use a simulation: lay out 20 counters (95% red = vaccinated, 5% blue = unvaccinated). Try to spread "infection" from one blue counter — it can't reach others because red counters block it. Then reduce red to 75% — infection spreads easily.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the global vaccination programme to eradicate polio. What challenges remain? Discuss anti-vaccination movements and how public health officials respond to misinformation.

Plenary (5 minutes)

For and Against

Students write one benefit and one risk of vaccination. Discuss as a class which is more significant and why vaccination programmes are considered safe and effective.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain herd immunity clearly and evaluate the benefits and risks of vaccination with balanced reasoning
  • Getting there: Can describe herd immunity but cannot evaluate vaccination effectively
  • Not yet: Cannot explain herd immunity or why vaccination programmes exist

Homework Suggestions

  • Research the smallpox eradication programme and write a paragraph explaining how vaccination led to the complete elimination of a disease
  • Create a timeline of key vaccination milestones (Jenner 1796, Salk polio 1955, MMR 1988, COVID-19 vaccines 2020)
  • Analyse data on measles cases before and after the Wakefield scare — explain what happened and why
  • Write a balanced argument: "Should vaccination be compulsory?" with evidence for both sides
