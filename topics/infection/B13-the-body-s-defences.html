B13: The Body's Defences

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand the body's non-specific and specific defence mechanisms, including physical barriers, white blood cells, and the production of antibodies.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the body's first line of defence (physical and chemical barriers)
  • Explain the role of phagocytes and lymphocytes in the immune response
  • Describe how antibodies are produced and how they fight pathogens
  • Explain the concept of immunity: active vs passive, natural vs artificial

Prerequisites

  • Knowledge of pathogens and how they cause disease (B12)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Diagrams of immune cells (phagocytes, lymphocytes)
  • Antibody-antigen diagram templates
  • Sorting cards for immunity types

Lesson 1: First Line of Defence — Barriers

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Think-Pair-Share

Ask: "Your body is constantly being attacked by pathogens, yet you rarely get sick. Why?" Students think, discuss, then share. Introduce the concept of defence mechanisms.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The body has three lines of defence:
  • First line of defence: physical and chemical barriers that prevent pathogens entering the body. Non-specific (work against all pathogens).
  • Second line of defence: non-specific internal responses (e.g. inflammation, fever, phagocytes)
  • Third line of defence: specific immune response (lymphocytes, antibodies)
First line of defence — barriers:
  • Skin: tough, waterproof barrier made of dead keratinised cells. Prevents entry of most pathogens. Secretes oils that are antibacterial.
  • Mucous membranes: line the respiratory and digestive tracts. Mucus traps pathogens and particles. Cilia (tiny hairs) in the trachea sweep mucus upwards to be swallowed (mucociliary escalator) — stomach acid then kills the pathogens.
  • Tears and saliva: contain lysozyme, an enzyme that breaks down bacterial cell walls.
  • Stomach acid: hydrochloric acid (pH 1-2) kills most bacteria swallowed with food.
  • Ear wax: traps dust and pathogens, preventing them from entering the ear canal.
  • Urine: washes bacteria out of the urethra. Acidic pH inhibits bacterial growth.
Worked Example

"Explain why the mucociliary escalator is an effective defence mechanism." Answer: Mucus traps pathogens and particles inhaled into the trachea. Cilia on the epithelial cells beat in a wave-like motion, sweeping the mucus upwards towards the throat. The mucus is then swallowed, and the stomach acid kills the trapped pathogens. This prevents pathogens from reaching the lungs.

Common Misconception

"The immune system only acts when you are already ill"

The first line of defence (barriers) is constantly active, preventing pathogens from entering in the first place. The immune system is always working — you just don't notice it when it's successful.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on three barriers: skin, mucus, stomach acid. Provide a matching activity: barrier → what it protects → how it works. Use simple language.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research why people with compromised immune systems (e.g. HIV/AIDS, chemotherapy patients) are more vulnerable to infections. Discuss how the body's barriers alone are sometimes insufficient.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Barrier Match

Write six barriers on one side and their functions on the other. Students draw lines to match. Speed challenge.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe at least four physical/chemical barriers with clear explanations of how they work
  • Getting there: Can name some barriers but cannot explain how they work
  • Not yet: Cannot describe the body's first line of defence

Lesson 2: White Blood Cells — Phagocytes and Lymphocytes

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Diagram Discussion

Show a diagram of a white blood cell engulfing a bacterium. Ask: "What is happening here? How does this help fight infection?" Discuss the role of white blood cells.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
If pathogens breach the first line of defence, white blood cells provide the second and third lines:
  • Phagocytes (non-specific): large cells that engulf and digest pathogens. They detect chemicals released by pathogens, move towards them (chemotaxis), surround the pathogen, and engulf it into a phagosome. The phagosome fuses with a lysosome (containing digestive enzymes) and the pathogen is destroyed. They present antigens on their surface to activate lymphocytes. Found in blood and tissues.
  • Lymphocytes (specific): smaller cells that produce antibodies. Each lymphocyte has receptors on its surface that are specific to one particular antigen (foreign molecule). When a lymphocyte encounters its specific antigen, it becomes activated and:
    1. Multiplies rapidly by mitosis to produce many identical copies (clonal expansion)
    2. Some become memory cells that remain in the body for years, providing long-term immunity
    3. Others become plasma cells that produce and secrete antibodies
Antibodies:
  • Y-shaped proteins that are specific to a particular antigen
  • They bind to the antigens on the pathogen's surface
  • This causes the pathogens to clump together (agglutination), making them easier for phagocytes to engulf
  • Antibodies can also neutralise toxins produced by bacteria
  • Each pathogen has many different antigens on its surface, and the body produces different antibodies for each
Worked Example

"Describe the role of lymphocytes in fighting infection." Answer: Lymphocytes are specific white blood cells. When a lymphocyte encounters a pathogen with a specific antigen that matches its receptor, it is activated. It divides rapidly by mitosis (clonal expansion). Some copies become memory cells (providing long-term immunity). Others become plasma cells that produce antibodies specific to that antigen. The antibodies bind to the pathogen, clumping them together for phagocytes to engulf and destroy.

Common Misconception

"Antibodies directly kill pathogens"

Antibodies do not kill pathogens directly. They work by: (1) binding to antigens and clumping pathogens together (agglutination) so phagocytes can engulf them more easily, (2) neutralising toxins, and (3) marking pathogens for destruction (opsonisation). Phagocytes do the actual killing.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Simplify: "Phagocytes eat germs. Lymphocytes make antibodies. Antibodies tag germs so phagocytes can eat them." Provide a flow diagram of the immune response.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research how HIV specifically attacks lymphocytes (CD4+ T cells) and explain why this weakens the entire immune system. Discuss how this relates to AIDS.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Two Cells

Students write two sentences: one about phagocytes and one about lymphocytes, describing their role in the immune response. Compare with a partner.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can clearly distinguish between phagocytes and lymphocytes and explain the antibody production process
  • Getting there: Knows the difference between the two cell types but cannot describe antibody production
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between phagocytes and lymphocytes

Lesson 3: Types of Immunity

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Memory Cells

Ask: "If you catch chickenpox once, you usually never get it again. Why?" Discuss memory cells and introduce the concept of immunity.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Types of immunity — two dimensions:
  • Active immunity: the body produces its own antibodies in response to an antigen. Slow to develop (days/weeks) but long-lasting because memory cells remain.
  • Passive immunity: antibodies are received from another source. Immediate protection but temporary (antibodies are eventually broken down and no memory cells are produced).
Four combinations:
TypeSourceExampleDuration
Natural activeOwn body after infectionCatching chickenpox → immune for lifeLong-lasting
Natural passiveAntibodies from motherAntibodies via placenta or breast milkTemporary (months)
Artificial activeVaccinationMeasles vaccine → body produces antibodiesLong-lasting (may need boosters)
Artificial passiveAntibody injectionAnti-venom for snake bitesTemporary
Memory cells:
  • Produced during active immune response (both natural and artificial)
  • Remain in the body for years (sometimes lifelong)
  • On second exposure to the same pathogen, memory cells recognise it immediately
  • Rapid clonal expansion produces large numbers of antibodies quickly
  • The response is so fast and effective that the person doesn't feel ill — they are immune
Worked Example

"Explain why a vaccinated person can fight a disease faster than someone encountering it for the first time." Answer: The vaccine has already triggered an active immune response, producing memory cells. When the person encounters the real pathogen, memory cells recognise the antigens immediately and rapidly divide to produce large numbers of plasma cells. These produce antibodies quickly and in large amounts, destroying the pathogen before it can multiply enough to cause symptoms.

Common Misconception

"Vaccines give you the disease"

Vaccines do not give you the disease. They contain weakened, dead, or parts of pathogens (antigens) that trigger an immune response without causing illness. The body produces antibodies and memory cells, providing future protection.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a 2x2 grid: natural/artificial across the top, active/passive down the side. Fill in one example in each box together. Focus on the key difference: active = body makes antibodies (long-term), passive = antibodies from outside (temporary).
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research monoclonal antibodies as a form of artificial passive immunity. Discuss why anti-venom is needed immediately (no time for active immunity to develop). Investigate why some vaccines need booster doses.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Classification Challenge

Give four scenarios. Students classify each as natural/artificial and active/passive immunity. Discuss answers.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can classify all four types of immunity with examples and explain why active immunity is long-lasting
  • Getting there: Can describe active vs passive but confuses natural vs artificial
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between the types of immunity

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a poster showing the three lines of defence with diagrams and labels
  • Write a comparison of phagocytes and lymphocytes in a table format
  • Research one vaccination programme (e.g. MMR) and explain how it protects the population (herd immunity)
  • Design a flowchart showing the immune response from pathogen entry to resolution
