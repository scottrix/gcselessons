B17: Plant Diseases

Foundation Higher All Boards

Learn how plants defend themselves against pathogens, how plant diseases are spread, and why biosecurity is important in agriculture.

Lesson Overview
Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe how plants are infected by pathogens (bacteria, fungi, viruses, protists)
  • Explain the physical and chemical defences plants have against pathogens
  • Describe how plant diseases are spread
  • Discuss the importance of biosecurity in preventing plant disease outbreaks

Prerequisites

  • Basic knowledge of plant structure (B9)
  • Understanding of pathogens from B12

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Images of plant diseases (e.g. Dutch elm disease, rose black spot, tobacco mosaic virus)
  • Case study worksheets on plant disease outbreaks

Lesson 1: How Plants Are Infected and How They Defend Themselves

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Observation

Show a photo of a plant with a disease (e.g. a tomato plant with blight). Ask: "What do you think caused this? How did it spread?" Discuss and introduce plant pathogens.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
How plants get infected:
  • Pathogens enter through natural openings (stomata, lenticels) or wounds (insect bites, mechanical damage)
  • Fungal spores land on leaf surfaces and germinate, sending hyphae into the plant tissue
  • Bacteria enter through wounds or stomata
  • Viruses are spread by vectors (insects like aphids) or contaminated tools
  • Protists (e.g. Phytophthora) spread through water and soil
Plant defences:
  • Physical barriers: bark (tough outer covering), waxy cuticle on leaves (prevents water and pathogen entry), cell walls (cellulose provides a barrier)
  • Chemical defences: some plants produce toxins that kill or deter pathogens (e.g. caffeine in coffee plants, menthol in mint). Some produce antibiotics naturally.
  • Hypersensitive response: when a plant detects a pathogen, it can deliberately kill the cells around the infection site. This "scorched earth" strategy isolates the pathogen and prevents it from spreading. The dead cells form a barrier of dead tissue.
  • Producing antibodies-like proteins: plants can produce proteins that counteract pathogen toxins.
Examples of plant diseases:
  • Dutch elm disease: fungal disease spread by bark beetles. Killed millions of elm trees in the UK in the 1970s.
  • Rose black spot: fungal disease causing black spots on leaves, leading to leaf drop.
  • Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV): virus causing mottled patterns on tobacco leaves. Spread by contact and contaminated tools.
  • Potato blight (Phytophthora infestans): protist that caused the Irish Potato Famine (1845-1852). Destroys leaves and tubers.
Worked Example

"Describe two physical and one chemical defence that plants have against pathogens." Answer: Physical: (1) bark provides a tough outer covering that prevents pathogen entry. (2) waxy cuticle on leaves prevents fungal spores from germinating by repelling water. Chemical: some plants produce toxins (e.g. nicotine in tobacco) that are poisonous to pathogens and herbivores.

Common Misconception

"Plants have an immune system like animals"

Right: Plants do not have white blood cells or antibodies like animals. Instead, they rely on physical barriers, chemical defences, and the hypersensitive response (killing infected cells). Plants lack a circulatory system to transport immune cells.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on three plant defences: bark, waxy cuticle, and producing toxins. Provide images for each. Simplify the hypersensitive response to: "the plant kills its own cells around the infection to stop it spreading."
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the Irish Potato Famine: what caused it, how did it spread, and what were the consequences? Why was one variety of potato so vulnerable? Discuss genetic diversity in crops.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Defence Match

Match each defence to its type: bark (physical), waxy cuticle (physical), toxins (chemical), hypersensitive response (active). Quick matching on mini-whiteboards.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe how plants are infected and at least three defence mechanisms
  • Getting there: Can name some defences but cannot explain how they work
  • Not yet: Cannot describe plant defences against pathogens

Lesson 2: Spread of Plant Diseases and Biosecurity

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Outbreak Story

Tell the story: "In 2012, a new strain of potato blight arrived in the UK from Europe. It spread rapidly across potato farms." Ask: "How might it have arrived? How could it be stopped?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
How plant diseases spread:
  • Wind: fungal spores and some bacteria can be carried long distances by wind
  • Water: rain splash spreads spores between plants; contaminated irrigation water spreads diseases across fields
  • Insects (vectors): aphids transmit viruses from plant to plant; bark beetles spread fungal spores
  • Soil: some pathogens survive in soil for years and infect plants through roots
  • Contaminated tools/equipment: pruning shears, boots, and machinery can carry pathogens between plants or farms
  • Imported plants/soil: buying infected plants from abroad can introduce new diseases
Biosecurity measures:
  • Quarantine: isolating new plants before introducing them to a garden or farm
  • Cleaning tools and equipment between uses
  • Washing boots before entering fields
  • Removing and destroying infected plants (roguing)
  • Using disease-resistant varieties (breeding or genetic engineering)
  • Monitoring and surveillance for early detection
  • Restricting import of plants from countries with known diseases
  • Using pesticides/fungicides (though this can lead to resistance)
Why biosecurity matters:
  • New diseases can devastate agricultural crops (food security)
  • They can threaten native ecosystems (biodiversity loss)
  • Economic costs to farmers and the food industry
  • Once established, diseases can be almost impossible to eradicate
Worked Example

"Suggest three biosecurity measures a gardener could take to prevent the spread of plant diseases." Answer: (1) Wash tools with disinfectant between plants, especially when moving between gardens. (2) Remove and destroy (do not compost) infected leaves and plants to prevent spores spreading. (3) Buy only healthy, certified plants from reputable suppliers. (4) Avoid overhead watering which spreads fungal spores.

Common Misconception

"Fungicides can completely prevent all plant diseases"

Fungicides can help control fungal diseases but cannot prevent all plant diseases (they don't work on bacterial, viral, or protist diseases). Overuse can lead to fungicide-resistant pathogens. Prevention through good hygiene, resistant varieties, and crop rotation is more sustainable.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on three biosecurity measures: clean tools, remove infected plants, buy healthy plants. Provide a checklist for students to complete when "visiting" a new garden.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research a recent plant disease outbreak (e.g. ash dieback, Xylella fastidiosa). How did it arrive? How is it being managed? What are the long-term consequences?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Prevention Plan

Students write three biosecurity measures a farm could take to prevent a disease outbreak. Share and discuss.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain how plant diseases spread and describe effective biosecurity measures
  • Getting there: Can name some ways diseases spread but cannot suggest effective prevention
  • Not yet: Cannot describe how plant diseases are spread

Homework Suggestions

  • Research one plant disease outbreak and create a case study report: what caused it, how it spread, and what was done about it
  • Create a poster showing five plant defences against pathogens with diagrams
  • Investigate a local garden or allotment: what biosecurity measures are already in place? What could be improved?
  • Write a guide for gardeners on preventing the spread of rose black spot
