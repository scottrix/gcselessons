B15: Antibiotics and Painkillers

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand how antibiotics work against bacteria, why they are ineffective against viruses, and the growing problem of antibiotic resistance.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Explain how antibiotics work by killing bacteria or preventing their growth
  • Describe why antibiotics are ineffective against viruses
  • Explain how antibiotic resistance develops and why it is a serious threat
  • Discuss strategies to reduce antibiotic resistance

Prerequisites

  • Knowledge of bacterial and viral pathogens (B12)
  • Understanding of natural selection (higher level)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Antibiotic resistance data/graphs
  • Printed cards for natural selection simulation
  • Case study worksheets

Lesson 1: How Antibiotics Work

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

True or False

"Antibiotics can cure the flu" (False). "Antibiotics kill bacteria" (True). "You should always finish your course of antibiotics" (True). "Antibiotics work against all pathogens" (False). Discuss answers.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Antibiotics:
  • Are medicines that kill bacteria or stop them from growing (bacteriostatic)
  • Work by targeting structures or processes unique to bacteria:
    • Penicillin: interferes with cell wall synthesis → bacteria burst because they cannot maintain their cell wall
    • Other antibiotics: target ribosomes (stopping protein synthesis), DNA replication, or cell membrane function
  • Effective against bacterial infections: throat infections, urinary tract infections, pneumonia, meningitis (bacterial), tuberculosis
  • NOT effective against viral infections: flu, common cold, COVID-19, chickenpox. Viruses replicate inside host cells using host machinery, so there are fewer targets for antibiotics to attack.
  • Prescription-only: must be prescribed by a doctor who identifies the bacterial infection
  • Course must be completed: stopping early may leave surviving bacteria that are partially resistant
Painkillers (analgesics):
  • Relieve pain but do not treat the underlying cause
  • Paracetamol: reduces pain and fever by acting on the brain (blocks pain signals in the CNS)
  • Ibuprofen: anti-inflammatory, reduces pain, fever, and swelling by blocking prostaglandin production
  • Aspirin: pain relief, anti-inflammatory, reduces blood clotting (low dose)
Worked Example

"Explain why a doctor prescribes antibiotics for a throat infection but not for the flu." Answer: A throat infection is usually caused by bacteria (e.g. Streptococcus), which antibiotics can kill by targeting bacterial structures like cell walls. The flu is caused by a virus, which replicates inside human cells using the host's own machinery. Antibiotics cannot target viruses because viruses lack the structures (e.g. cell walls, bacterial ribosomes) that antibiotics act on.

Common Misconception

"Antibiotics cure viral infections"

Antibiotics only work against bacteria. They have no effect on viruses. Taking antibiotics for viral infections is useless, wastes medicine, and contributes to the development of antibiotic-resistant bacteria.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Simple rule: "Antibiotics = bacteria only." Provide a list of diseases and students classify as "antibiotics help" or "antibiotics don't help." Focus on the key distinction.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research how penicillin was discovered by Alexander Fleming. Why did it take so long to develop into a usable medicine? Discuss the "golden age" of antibiotic discovery and the current "antibiotic drought."

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Decision

Read out 6 scenarios. Students decide: "Antibiotics" or "Not antibiotics." Discuss any tricky ones.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain how antibiotics work and why they don't work against viruses
  • Getting there: Knows antibiotics are for bacteria but cannot explain how they work
  • Not yet: Thinks antibiotics work against all pathogens

Lesson 2: Antibiotic Resistance

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Alarm Bell

Show a headline: "Superbug MRSA kills thousands." Ask: "What is a superbug? Why can't antibiotics kill it?" Introduce antibiotic resistance.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
How antibiotic resistance develops:
  1. In any bacterial population, there is natural variation due to random mutations in DNA
  2. Some mutations may happen to confer resistance to a particular antibiotic (e.g. the antibiotic cannot bind to its target)
  3. When antibiotics are used, susceptible bacteria are killed, but resistant bacteria survive
  4. Resistant bacteria reproduce by binary fission, passing the resistance gene to all offspring
  5. Over time, the population becomes dominated by resistant bacteria
  6. Resistance genes can also be transferred between bacteria via plasmids (horizontal gene transfer)
This is natural selection in action — the environment (antibiotic) selects for resistant individuals. Factors accelerating resistance:
  • Over-prescription of antibiotics (including for viral infections)
  • Patients not finishing their full course of antibiotics
  • Use of antibiotics in agriculture (livestock farming)
  • lack of new antibiotic development
  • Global travel spreads resistant bacteria worldwide
Consequences:
  • Common infections may become untreatable
  • Minor surgery could become dangerous
  • Chemotherapy (which weakens the immune system) becomes riskier
  • The WHO considers antibiotic resistance one of the greatest threats to global health
Strategies to reduce resistance:
  • Only prescribe antibiotics when truly needed (not for viral infections)
  • Patients must complete the full course
  • Develop new antibiotics
  • Reduce use of antibiotics in farming
  • Improve hygiene and infection prevention to reduce the need for antibiotics
Worked Example

"Explain how overuse of antibiotics leads to resistance." Answer: (1) Overuse exposes more bacteria to antibiotics. (2) Bacteria with random mutations that confer resistance survive while susceptible ones die. (3) Resistant bacteria reproduce, passing on the resistance gene. (4) Over time, the population shifts from mostly susceptible to mostly resistant. (5) The antibiotic becomes ineffective against that bacterial strain.

Common Misconception

"Bacteria become resistant because they adapt to the antibiotic"

Bacteria do not "decide" to become resistant or adapt during their lifetime. Resistance arises from random mutations that happen to confer survival advantages when antibiotics are present. The antibiotic selects for pre-existing resistant individuals — it does not cause the resistance.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Use a simplified analogy: 100 paper bacteria, 5 are naturally resistant. Use a "kill" rule (remove all non-resistant). Count what's left — they reproduce. Repeat. Show the population becomes mostly resistant. Focus on: "resistant ones survive and multiply."
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research MRSA, VRSA, and other antibiotic-resistant "superbugs." Investigate phage therapy as a potential alternative to antibiotics. Discuss the economic reasons why pharmaceutical companies are not developing new antibiotics.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Action Plan

Students list three things individuals can do to help reduce antibiotic resistance (e.g. don't demand antibiotics for colds, complete the full course, practise good hygiene).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain the mechanism of antibiotic resistance using natural selection and suggest strategies to reduce it
  • Getting there: Understands that overuse causes resistance but cannot explain the biological mechanism
  • Not yet: Cannot explain how antibiotic resistance develops

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a poster explaining antibiotic resistance with a flow diagram showing how it develops
  • Research MRSA: what is it, where is it found, how can it be prevented?
  • Write a newspaper article explaining why people should not demand antibiotics from their GP
  • Compare paracetamol and ibuprofen: how do they work differently, when should each be used?
