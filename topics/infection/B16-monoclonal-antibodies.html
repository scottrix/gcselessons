B16: Monoclonal Antibodies

Higher All Boards

Discover how monoclonal antibodies are produced and used in medicine, including targeted cancer therapy, pregnancy testing, and disease diagnosis.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Higher only
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Explain how monoclonal antibodies are produced using hybridoma technology
  • Describe at least three medical uses of monoclonal antibodies
  • Evaluate the benefits and risks of using monoclonal antibodies in medicine
  • Understand how monoclonal antibodies are used in diagnostic tests (e.g. pregnancy tests)

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of antibodies, antigens, and the immune response (B13)
  • Knowledge of lymphocyte function

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Diagram of hybridoma production process
  • Pregnancy test kit (for demonstration) or images
  • Printed flowchart of monoclonal antibody production

Lesson 1: Production of Monoclonal Antibodies

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Antibody Limitation

Ask: "If you inject a mouse with a human cancer cell, it will produce antibodies against the cancer. But the mouse's immune system produces millions of different antibodies. How could we get just ONE specific antibody in large quantities?" Discuss the problem and introduce monoclonal antibodies.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) are antibodies produced by identical cells that are all clones of a single parent cell. They are all the same and bind to one specific antigen. Production process (hybridoma technology):
  1. Inject a mouse with the specific antigen (e.g. a human cancer cell surface protein)
  2. Wait for the mouse to produce an immune response — its B lymphocytes will produce antibodies against the antigen
  3. Remove the spleen and extract the B lymphocytes
  4. Mix the B lymphocytes with tumour cells (myeloma cells) using polyethylene glycol (PEG) or electrical fusion to create hybrid cells called hybridomas
  5. Hybridomas have the key properties of both parent cells: they produce antibodies (from B cells) AND they divide indefinitely (from tumour cells)
  6. Screen the hybridomas to find the one that produces the correct antibody (the one specific to the desired antigen)
  7. Clone the successful hybridoma — grow large numbers of identical cells
  8. Harvest the monoclonal antibodies from the culture medium
Each batch of monoclonal antibodies is specific to one antigen — this is what makes them so valuable.
Worked Example

"Explain why hybridoma cells are used instead of just B lymphocytes to produce monoclonal antibodies." Answer: B lymphocytes produce the correct antibody but can only divide a limited number of times before dying. Hybridoma cells combine the antibody-producing ability of B lymphocytes with the ability to divide indefinitely (from tumour cells), providing an unlimited supply of identical antibodies.

Common Misconception

"Monoclonal antibodies are made from human cells"

Traditionally, monoclonal antibodies are produced using mouse cells — the mouse is injected with the antigen and its B cells are fused with mouse tumour cells. More recently, techniques have been developed to produce humanised monoclonal antibodies to reduce immune reactions in patients.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a numbered flowchart with blanks to fill in. Simplify the process into 5 key steps: inject mouse → extract B cells → fuse with tumour cells → select correct hybridoma → clone and harvest.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research humanised monoclonal antibodies — why are they preferred for human therapy? Discuss the ethical considerations of using animals in medical research.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Step Check

Students write the 5 key steps of monoclonal antibody production from memory. Compare with a partner and fill in gaps.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the hybridoma production process in correct order with clear explanations
  • Getting there: Can sequence the main steps but misses key details
  • Not yet: Cannot describe how monoclonal antibodies are produced

Lesson 2: Uses of Monoclonal Antibodies

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Pregnancy Test

Show a pregnancy test kit (or image). Ask: "How does this test work? What is it detecting?" Introduce how monoclonal antibodies are used in diagnostic tests.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Key uses of monoclonal antibodies:
  • Pregnancy testing: detects the hormone hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin) in urine. The test strip has monoclonal antibodies specific to hCG. If hCG is present (pregnant), the antibodies bind and produce a coloured line. Simple, fast, and accurate.
  • Cancer diagnosis: monoclonal antibodies can be designed to bind to specific antigens on cancer cells. This helps identify the type and stage of cancer. Example: HER2 testing in breast cancer.
  • Targeted cancer therapy: monoclonal antibodies can be attached to toxic drugs or radioactive substances. They bind specifically to cancer cells, delivering the treatment directly to the tumour while minimising damage to healthy cells. Example: trastuzumab (Herceptin) targets HER2-positive breast cancer cells.
  • Monitoring disease: measuring levels of specific proteins in blood to track disease progression (e.g. monitoring HIV viral load).
  • Immunoassays: used in laboratories to detect specific molecules in blood samples (e.g. testing for hormones, drugs, or pathogens).
  • Treating autoimmune diseases: some monoclonal antibodies can block the immune response that causes conditions like rheumatoid arthritis.
Benefits:
  • Highly specific — target only the correct cells/molecules
  • Fewer side effects than traditional treatments (e.g. chemotherapy) because they are targeted
  • Can be used for diagnosis and treatment (theranostics)
Limitations:
  • Very expensive to produce
  • May cause allergic reactions (immune response to the mouse-derived protein)
  • Not effective for all types of cancer
  • Must be given by injection or IV, not orally
Worked Example

"Explain how monoclonal antibodies are used to treat cancer." Answer: Monoclonal antibodies are designed to bind to specific antigens on cancer cells. They can be: (1) linked to a toxic drug and delivered directly to cancer cells (targeted therapy), reducing damage to healthy cells. (2) Used to mark cancer cells so the immune system can recognise and destroy them. (3) Used to block signals that cancer cells need to grow. This is more precise than chemotherapy, which kills all rapidly dividing cells.

Common Misconception

"Monoclonal antibodies cure all types of cancer"

Monoclonal antibodies only work against cancers that have specific antigens on their surface. Not all cancers have these antigens, and even when they do, the treatment may not work for every patient. They are one tool in cancer treatment, alongside surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on two uses: pregnancy testing and cancer diagnosis. For each, explain what is detected and why monoclonal antibodies are useful (specificity).
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research a specific monoclonal antibody drug (e.g. trastuzumab, rituximab). How does it work? What cancers does it treat? What are the side effects?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Use Match

Match each use to the condition/purpose: pregnancy test (detect hCG), cancer therapy (target cancer cells), disease monitoring (measure protein levels), immunoassay (detect molecules in blood).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe at least three uses with clear explanations of how monoclonal antibodies work in each case
  • Getting there: Can name some uses but cannot explain how they work
  • Not yet: Cannot describe any uses of monoclonal antibodies

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a flowchart of monoclonal antibody production using hybridoma technology
  • Research one monoclonal antibody drug and write a patient information leaflet about how it works
  • Compare monoclonal antibodies with traditional chemotherapy for cancer treatment: benefits and limitations
  • Investigate how monoclonal antibodies are used in COVID-19 testing (lateral flow tests)
