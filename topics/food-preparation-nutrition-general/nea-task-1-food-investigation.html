Key Fact: NEA Task 1 is a food investigation worth 15% of the total GCSE grade (AQA 8585). It is completed in controlled conditions over approximately 10 hours.
Key Fact: The investigation assesses students' understanding of the working characteristics, functional properties, and chemical processes of ingredients.
Key Fact: Task 1 involves: researching the given brief, conducting practical experiments, recording results, and writing a scientific report (1,500–2,000 words plus supporting evidence).
Key Fact: A typical Task 1 brief might ask you to investigate how different raising agents affect the volume and texture of a sponge cake, or how different fats affect pastry shortness.
Key Fact: The investigation must include: a hypothesis or prediction, controlled variables, a method, results table, analysis, and a conclusion linked to food science.
Key Fact: Controlled variables are essential: to test the effect of one ingredient, all other variables (temperature, quantities, timing, equipment) must be kept constant.

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response You are investigating how different raising agents affect the volume and texture of a sponge cake. Describe how you would conduct this investigation, including variables and how you would ensure reliable results. (8 marks) <div class="

I would investigate how three raising agents — baking powder, bicarbonate of soda, and self-raising flour — affect sponge cake volume and texture. The independent variable is the type of raising agent. The dependent variables are cake volume (measured in cm³ by displacement or height) and texture (assessed by sensory evaluation). Controlled variables include: 50g each of flour, sugar, butter, and one egg; oven temperature 180°C; baking time 20 minutes; same mixing method (creaming); same cake tin size and material; same cooling time before measurement. I would make three cakes, changing only the raising agent: Cake A uses baking powder (1 teaspoon), Cake B uses bicarbonate of soda (half teaspoon, with an acidic ingredient like lemon juice), Cake C uses self-raising flour (replacing plain flour). After baking and cooling, I would measure the height and volume of each cake, photograph cross-sections, and conduct a sensory evaluation with at least three testers rating texture, lightness, and overall acceptability on a 1–5 scale. Results would be recorded in a table comparing raising agent, height, volume, and sensory scores. I would repeat the experiment once to verify reliability. In

Exam Tips: Structure your investigation report clearly: Introduction, Research, Hypothesis, Method, Results, Analysis, Conclusion. | Always name your variables: independent (what you change), dependent (what you measure), controlled (what you keep the same). | Use food science terminology in your analysis: say 'denaturation of proteins' not 'the egg went hard', 'gelatinisation of starch' not 'the sauce thickened'. | Photograph every stage of your investigation — visual evidence is essential supporting evidence.
Common Errors: ✗ Task 1 is a cooking practical with no written work. ✓ Task 1 is primarily a scientific investigation with a written report (1,500–2,000 words). Practical experiments provide evidence, but the report demonstrating understanding of food science is what is assessed. ✗ You don't need to control variables in a food investigation. ✓ Controlling variables is essential. If you change more than one factor between tests, you cannot know which factor caused the observed difference in results. Only the independent variable should change. ✗ The conclusion just describes what happened. ✓ The conclusion must explain results using food science. Don't just say 'the cake with bicarbonate of soda was larger'
