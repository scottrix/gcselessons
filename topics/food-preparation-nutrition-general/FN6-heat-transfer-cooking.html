Lesson 2: Core Concepts: heat transfer & cooking methods

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on heat transfer & cooking methods. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Conduction: heat transfer through direct contact — e.g. pan on hob transferring heat to food through the metal. Best for: frying, sautéing, griddling.
Key Fact: Convection: heat transfer through fluid (liquid or air) movement — hot fluid rises, cool fluid sinks, creating currents. Best for: baking (hot air), boiling/simmering (hot water), roasting.
Key Fact: Radiation: heat transfer through electromagnetic waves without needing a medium — e.g. grilling, toasting. The heat source emits infrared radiation that is absorbed by the food surface.
Key Fact: Microwave cooking: microwaves (electromagnetic waves) cause water molecules in food to vibrate rapidly, generating heat through friction. Microwaves penetrate about 4cm, so thick foods cook from outside in. Not suitable for pastry or crispy textures.
Key Fact: Baking: convection (hot air) + conduction (through baking tray). Temperature typically 180-220°C. Produces browning (Maillard reaction), rising (gas expansion, steam), and setting (protein coagulation, starch gelatinisation).
Key Fact: Boiling: convection (hot water) at 100°C. Nutrients (especially water-soluble vitamins) leach into water. Use minimal water and cook for shortest time to preserve nutrients.

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Compare two different cooking methods, explaining how heat is transferred and the effects on nutritional and sensory properties. [12 marks] <div class="

Boiling and steaming are both moist-heat cooking methods but differ significantly in heat transfer and effects on food. Boiling uses convection — heat transfers through circulating hot water at 100°C. The food is fully immersed, and water-soluble vitamins (B and C) leach into the cooking water. If the water is discarded, these nutrients are lost. Boiling also causes cell walls to break down, potentially making vegetables soft and losing colour (especially green vegetables, where acid released during cooking degrades chlorophyll). Steaming also uses convection — heat transfers through circulating steam. However, the food sits above the water and never touches it, so water-soluble vitamins are retained in the food. The gentle heat preserves cell structure, maintaining firmer texture, brighter colour, and more flavour. Steaming requires no added fat. Both methods cook at approximately 100°C, so high-temperature reactions like the Maillard reaction don't occur. Neither produces browning or the complex flavours associated with dry-heat cooking. From a nutritional perspective, steaming is clearly superior — studies show steamed broccoli retains up to 80% of vitamin C compared to only 40%

Common Errors: ✗ Microwaves cook food from the inside out. ✓ Microwaves penetrate only about 4cm and heat the outer layers. Heat then conducts inward to cook the centre. It has nothing to do with nuclear radiation.
