Lesson 2: Core Concepts: food production & sustainability

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on food production & sustainability. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Food provenance means knowing where food comes from — how, where, and by whom it was produced, processed, and transported.
Key Fact: Intensive farming maximises yield using fertilisers, pesticides, antibiotics, and high-density housing; it produces cheap food but raises welfare and environmental concerns.
Key Fact: Organic farming avoids artificial chemicals, uses crop rotation and natural fertilisers, and provides higher animal welfare; it is more expensive and yields are lower.
Key Fact: Free-range and barn-raised systems give animals more space and outdoor access compared to intensive caged systems; UK and EU law defines minimum standards.
Key Fact: Sustainable food production meets current needs without compromising future generations: it conserves resources, minimises pollution, and supports fair livelihoods.
Key Fact: Carbon footprint of food includes: farming (methane from livestock, fertiliser production), processing, transport, packaging, retail, and consumer cooking/refrigeration.

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Compare intensive and organic farming methods and evaluate their impact on sustainability. (8 marks) <div class="

Intensive farming uses artificial fertilisers, pesticides, antibiotics, and high-density animal housing to maximise food production at low cost. It yields large quantities of affordable food, which helps feed growing populations and keeps consumer prices down. However, it is less sustainable: artificial fertilisers and pesticides degrade soil health and pollute waterways, high-density housing raises serious animal welfare concerns, and antibiotic overuse contributes to antimicrobial resistance. Organic farming avoids artificial chemicals, uses crop rotation and natural fertilisers (compost, manure), and provides higher animal welfare with outdoor access. It supports biodiversity, improves soil structure, and avoids pesticide residues. However, organic yields are significantly lower (typically 20–40% less), requiring more land to produce the same amount of food, which can drive deforestation. Organic food is also more expensive, limiting access for lower-income consumers. In terms of sustainability, organic farming is better for local ecosystems and animal welfare, but its lower yields mean a larger land footprint per unit of food. Exam Tips: When comparing farming methods, always give both advantages AND disadvantages — one-sided answers lose marks. | Use specific terms: 'intensive farming' not 'factory farming', 'organic farming' not 'natural farming'. | For sustainability questions, mention the three pillars: environmental, economic, and social sustainability. | Explain carbon footprint as the full lifecycle, not just transport — most emissions come from production, not food miles.
Common Errors: ✗ Organic food is always more nutritious than non-organic food. ✓ The nutritional difference between organic and non-organic food is small and inconsistent. The main benefits of organic farming are environmental (no pesticides, better soil health, higher biodiversity) and animal welfare. ✗ Food miles are the biggest contributor to food's carbon footprint. ✓ Transport typically accounts for a small proportion of food's carbon footprint. Production (especially of meat and dairy) is usually the largest contributor. ✗ Free-range means animals live completely natural lives outdoors. ✓ Free-range standards set minimum access to outdoor areas, but the amount of space, time outdoors, and conditions
