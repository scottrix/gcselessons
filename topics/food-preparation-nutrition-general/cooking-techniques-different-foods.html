Lesson 2: Core Concepts: cooking techniques for different foods

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on cooking techniques for different foods. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Moist heat methods (use water or steam): boiling, simmering, poaching, steaming, braising, stewing. These are suitable for tougher cuts of meat and preserve moisture in foods.
Key Fact: Dry heat methods (use fat, oil, or air): baking, roasting, grilling, frying (shallow, deep, stir). These create browning (Maillard reaction) and crispy textures.
Key Fact: Boiling: cooking in water at 100°C. Quick but can leach water-soluble vitamins (B and C) into the cooking water. Suitable for pasta, potatoes, rice.
Key Fact: Steaming: cooking over boiling water in a covered pan. Preserves nutrients (no contact with water), colour, and texture. The healthiest cooking method for vegetables and fish.
Key Fact: Baking: cooking in an oven using dry heat (typically 160–220°C). Suitable for bread, cakes, pastry, and some vegetable dishes. Gives a crisp exterior and even cooking.
Key Fact: Roasting: cooking in an oven with added fat/oil at higher temperatures (180–220°C). Used for meat, poultry, and root vegetables. Creates browning and flavour through the Maillard reaction.

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Compare steaming, grilling, and deep frying as cooking methods. Explain the effect of each method on nutritional value and health. (8 marks) <div class="

Steaming cooks food over boiling water in a covered pan, without the food touching the water. This is the healthiest method: water-soluble vitamins (B and C) are retained because there is no leaching into cooking water, no fat is added, and the food's natural flavour, colour, and texture are preserved. Steaming is ideal for vegetables, fish, and dumplings. However, steaming does not produce browning or crispness, as the temperature does not exceed 100°C and no Maillard reaction occurs. Grilling cooks food under or over direct heat. Fat drains away from the food as it cooks, reducing the overall fat content compared to frying, and no additional oil is needed. Grilling creates browning and flavour through the Maillard reaction and gives a charred, smoky taste. It is well-suited to meat, fish, and some vegetables. However, very high temperatures can produce potentially harmful compounds (heterocyclic amines) in charred meat, so grilling should be done carefully and not to excess. Deep frying submerges food in hot oil (160–190°C), cooking it quickly and creating a crisp, golden exterior through the Maillard reaction.
