WC4: Sentence Structures

Foundation Higher All Boards

Develop precise, effective word choices and sentence structures that create specific effects in both creative and transactional writing.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Choose words precisely for their connotations and register
  • Vary sentence structures for different effects
  • Use punctuation deliberately for emphasis and rhythm
  • Write in different registers for different audiences and purposes
  • Edit writing to improve word choice and sentence variety

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of connotation vs denotation
  • Basic understanding of sentence types (simple, compound, complex)
  • Confidence with writing sentences of varied length

Materials & Equipment

  • Thesauruses (physical or online)
  • Word choice comparison activities
  • Register switching exercises
  • Model texts showing varied sentence structures

Lesson 1: Precise Word Choice

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Stronger Verbs

Display: "She walked to the door." Students suggest alternatives: strode, crept, stumbled, ambled, marched. Each changes the character's mood and intention. Introduce: precise verbs do more work than adverbs.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach three principles of effective word choice:
  • Precision over generality: "aftershave" not "smell"; "cobblestones" not "stones"; "iced" not "cold"
  • Strong verbs over verb + adverb: "sprinted" not "ran quickly"; "whispered" not "said quietly"
  • Register: the level of formality in language. Academic, formal, informal, slang — each appropriate for different contexts
Teach students to audit their writing: find weak verbs (was, had, went, got, said) and replace with specific ones. Find vague nouns (thing, stuff, place) and replace with precise ones. Show the "ADVERB TEST": if you've used an adverb, can you find a stronger verb instead?
Worked Example

Before: "The man walked slowly and carefully across the wet road. He was looking at things in the shop windows."
After: "The man picked his way across the rain-slicked road, pausing at each shop window to linger over the displays."

Practice Task

Rewrite these sentences using more precise vocabulary. Replace at least two weak words in each.

1. "She went into the room and looked at everything."
2. "The food was very nice and the place was really pretty."
3. "He said no in a loud voice and then walked away."

1. "She swept into the room, her gaze sweeping over every detail."
2. "The food was exquisite and the restaurant was elegantly decorated."
(or: "The food was hearty and the café was cosily cluttered" — different register, different effect)
3. "He refused flatly, then strode away without looking back."

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a "weak word hitlist" of words to avoid (nice, good, big, small, went, got) with alternatives.
Stretch:
Write the same paragraph in three different registers: formal, informal, and slang. Discuss how register changes the reader's perception.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Verb Upgrade

Display 10 sentences with weak verbs. Students have 2 minutes to upgrade as many as possible.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Uses precise, varied vocabulary with awareness of connotation and register
  • Getting there: Uses some precise vocabulary but relies on generic words in places
  • Not yet: Uses predominantly vague, generic vocabulary

Lesson 2: Sentence Variety and Punctuation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Rhythm Reader

Read a passage from a novel aloud. Students tap the desk for each pause (commas, full stops). Notice how punctuation creates rhythm in writing.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach how punctuation creates effects:
  • Full stop: creates finality, emphasis, or a break in thought
  • Comma: creates a pause, adds detail, or separates items in a list
  • Semicolon: links two related independent clauses; creates a sophisticated rhythm
  • Colon: introduces an explanation, list, or quotation; creates anticipation
  • Dash: creates an interruption, emphasis, or afterthought
  • Ellipsis: creates trailing off, hesitation, or mystery
Teach sentence types:
  • Simple sentence: one clause. "She ran." (Punchy, fast)
  • Compound sentence: two clauses joined by and/but/so/yet. "She ran, but he was faster." (Balanced)
  • Complex sentence: main clause + subordinate clause. "Although she ran, he was faster." (Nuanced)
Key exam skill: varied sentence structures improve the writing mark at both Foundation and Higher tiers.
Worked Example

Monotonous: "He walked to the shop. He bought some milk. He walked home. He made a cup of tea."
Varied: "He walked to the shop — a ten-minute detour he could have avoided, had he remembered this morning. Milk. That was all he needed, and all he could think about. By the time he got home, the kettle was already boiling. Funny, that: sometimes the small rituals are the only things that hold a day together."

Practice Task

Rewrite this passage to include: one semicolon, one colon, one dash, and varied sentence lengths.

Original: "The concert was very loud. The crowd was excited. The singer performed well. Everyone had a good time."

"The concert was deafening — a wall of sound that hit you in the chest the moment you walked through the doors. The crowd surged forward, voices raised, hands in the air; it was less an audience than a single, living organism. The singer commanded the stage with an energy that bordered on supernatural: every gesture precise, every note held a beat longer than expected. By the end, nobody wanted to leave."

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a punctuation effects reference card. Students practise replacing full stops with commas, semicolons, or dashes to see the effect.
Stretch:
Write a paragraph using every punctuation mark at least once, each for a deliberate effect.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Punctuation Swap

Display a sentence. Students rewrite it using a semicolon, a dash, and an ellipsis. Discuss how each changes the rhythm and meaning.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Uses a range of sentence structures and punctuation marks deliberately for effect
  • Getting there: Shows some variety but relies on similar sentence patterns
  • Not yet: Sentences are all the same length and structure

Homework Suggestions

  • Find five weak verbs in a piece of your writing and replace them with precise alternatives
  • Write a paragraph about your day using at least three different sentence types
  • Practise using semicolons, colons, and dashes in five original sentences
  • Write the same paragraph in formal and informal registers — which feels more natural?
