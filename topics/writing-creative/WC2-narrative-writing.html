WC2: Narrative Writing

Foundation Higher All Boards

Write compelling stories with well-developed characters, engaging plots, effective dialogue, and purposeful structural choices.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 4
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Plan a story with a clear beginning, middle, and end
  • Develop characters through action, dialogue, and description
  • Write effective dialogue that reveals character and advances plot
  • Create tension and suspense through pacing and structural choices
  • Write an engaging opening that hooks the reader

Prerequisites

  • Descriptive writing skills (WC1)
  • Understanding of narrative structure
  • Confidence with varied sentence structures

Materials & Equipment

  • Story planning templates
  • Model narrative openings from published authors
  • Dialogue punctuation guide
  • Character profile worksheets

Lesson 1: Story Openings

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Hook the Reader

Display five famous opening lines. Students rank them from most to least engaging. Discuss: what makes you want to read on? (Mystery, drama, unusual voice, a question that needs answering.)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The opening must hook the reader immediately. Teach these techniques:
  • Start with action: "The door burst open and there he was — soaking wet, empty-handed, and grinning."
  • Start with mystery: "It was the last time I ever saw her smile. I just did not know it then."
  • Start with a striking image: "The house looked like a mouth missing its teeth — gaps where windows should have been, the front door hanging open like a tongue."
  • Start with dialogue: "'Don't go in there.' Her voice was barely a whisper, but it stopped me cold."
Avoid: "I'm going to tell you a story about…" or "My name is…" — these are slow and generic. Key principle: the reader should have a question after the first sentence. What happened? What will happen? Who is this person? The opening creates a debt the story must pay.
Worked Example

Weak opening: "I am going to tell you about the time I went to the beach with my family. It was a sunny day in July."
Strong opening: "The sea had swallowed the boat. One moment it was there — white hull, blue stripe, my brother waving from the stern — and then a wave rose up like a fist and the sea closed over it. By the time I reached the water, there was nothing but foam."

The strong opening works because it drops the reader into a crisis, uses powerful imagery ("swallowed", "like a fist"), and creates immediate questions: Is the brother safe? What happened next?

Practice Task

Write three different openings (2-3 sentences each) for a story about "a journey". Each must use a different technique: action, mystery, and striking image.

Action: "She grabbed the bag, slammed the door, and ran — not towards the station but away from it, towards the hills, towards anywhere that was not here."
Mystery: "The ticket was for a place I had never heard of. It was tucked inside an envelope with no name, no address — just the date, today, and a single word: 'Go.'"
Striking image: "The road unspooled ahead like a grey ribbon, disappearing into a sky so heavy with cloud it looked like it was pressing down on the earth."

Differentiation

Support:
Provide sentence starters for each technique. Show three model openings and ask students to identify which technique each uses.
Stretch:
Write an opening that uses TWO techniques simultaneously (e.g. mystery + striking image).

Plenary (5 minutes)

First Line Feedback

Students write their favourite opening on a mini-whiteboard. Partners read and say whether they would want to read on, and why.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Writes an engaging opening that creates questions or immediate interest
  • Getting there: Writes an opening but it is generic or slow
  • Not yet: Starts with exposition or summary rather than engaging the reader

Lesson 2: Characterisation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Show, Don't Tell

Display: "He was nervous." vs "He checked his phone for the fourth time in a minute, then pressed his thumbnail into the soft flesh of his palm until it left a white crescent." Which is more vivid? Introduce: show character through action, not labels.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach the three methods of characterisation:
  • Action: what the character does reveals who they are. A character who picks up litter shows care; one who kicks a bin shows frustration.
  • Dialogue: what and how characters speak. Formal language? Slang? Pauses? Interruptions? These reveal education, emotion, and relationship.
  • Description: appearance and body language. Clothing, posture, facial expressions — but always tied to character, not just appearance.
Key rule: "Show, don't tell." Never write "She was kind" — show her being kind. Never write "He was angry" — show him slamming doors, clenching his jaw, speaking through gritted teeth. Teach students to create a "character card" before writing: name, age, appearance, personality (3 adjectives), one key trait revealed through action, one line of typical dialogue.
Worked Example

Telling: "Mrs Okonkwo was a strict teacher who scared the students."
Showing: "Mrs Okonkwo walked into the room and the whispering stopped like someone had switched it off. She set her bag on the desk without looking up, then placed a ruler beside it — perfectly parallel to the edge, as if she had measured. When she finally spoke, her voice was quiet, which was somehow worse than shouting."

Practice Task

Create a character and write a 100-word paragraph that reveals their personality WITHOUT using any adjectives to describe their character (no "she was brave", "he was rude", etc.).

"He arrived twenty minutes early, as always, and arranged his things on the desk — pen aligned with the edge of his notebook, calculator centred, rubber placed in the top-right corner. When another student sat down beside him, he shifted his chair an inch to the left. He did not say hello. He checked his watch — a habit, since it was always the right time — and began working through the questions he had already completed at home."

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a character profile template. Use a "character auction" — students bid on personality traits and must create a character who embodies them.
Stretch:
Create a character who appears one way but is revealed to be different. Show the gap between surface and depth.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Guess the Character

Students write a 50-word character sketch. Partners guess: Are they rich/poor? Happy/sad? Young/old? Confident/shy?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Creates characters revealed through action, dialogue, and description — never told with adjectives
  • Getting there: Uses some "showing" but reverts to "telling" occasionally
  • Not yet: Describes characters using only labels and adjectives

Lesson 3: Dialogue

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Talk Like a Character

Write three identical statements ("I didn't do it") attributed to three different characters: a scared child, a furious teenager, a calm lawyer. Students discuss how the delivery changes the meaning.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach dialogue skills:
  • Punctuation: new speaker = new line. Commas inside speech marks. Question marks and exclamation marks inside speech marks.
  • Speech tags: "said" is invisible — readers skim past it. Avoid "exclaimed", "shouted", "gasped" — let the dialogue itself convey the emotion. Use action beats instead: "'I didn't do it.' He folded his arms."
  • Subtext: characters rarely say exactly what they mean. A character who says "I'm fine" is usually not fine.
  • Purpose: every line of dialogue should reveal character, advance plot, or both. If it does neither, cut it.
Model dialogue with action beats vs dialogue with speech tags. Show how action beats are more vivid.
Worked Example

Weak: "'Where are you going?' she asked angrily. 'Out,' he said coldly.
She exclaimed, 'You can't go out!'"

Strong: "'Where are you going?' She blocked the doorway, arms folded.
He picked up his jacket. 'Out.'
'You can't go out. Not after what happened.' Her voice cracked on the last word."

Practice Task

Write a dialogue between two characters who are arguing about something unstated. The reader should be able to guess the topic from subtext alone. 8-10 lines.

"You forgot."
"I didn't forget."
"Then what do you call it?"
"I call it being busy."
"Busy." She stared at the untouched cake on the table. Two candles. One already blown out.
"I said I was sorry."
"No. You said you were busy. There's a difference."
He reached for her hand. She pulled away.
"Happy birthday," he said, quietly.
She turned to the window. "It's tomorrow."

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a dialogue punctuation checklist. Use role-play: students act out a scene before writing it.
Stretch:
Write a dialogue where the subtext contradicts the literal meaning of every line.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Tag vs Beat

Display five lines of dialogue with speech tags. Students rewrite each using action beats instead. Compare results.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Writes dialogue with correct punctuation, purposeful speech tags or action beats, and effective subtext
  • Getting there: Punctuation mostly correct; dialogue is functional but lacks subtext
  • Not yet: Incorrect punctuation; dialogue does not reveal character or advance plot

Lesson 4: Plot and Tension

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Story Arc

Display a simple story arc: hook → build-up → complication → climax → resolution. Students identify each stage in a familiar fairy tale.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach plot structure:
  • Hook: the opening that grabs attention
  • Build-up: developing characters, setting, and stakes
  • Complication/problem: something goes wrong or a challenge arises
  • Climax: the moment of highest tension — the turning point
  • Resolution: how the story ends (not necessarily happily)
For GCSE, students typically write 45-60 minutes (500-800 words). Teach them to plan:
  • Beginning (100-150 words): Hook + introduce character + establish situation
  • Middle (250-400 words): Build tension + complication + rising stakes
  • End (100-150 words): Climax + resolution (can be ambiguous or open-ended)
Key exam technique: practise planning in 5 minutes before writing. A planned story will always outperform an unplanned one.
Worked Example — Creating Tension

Tension is created through: withholding information, time pressure, short sentences, repetition, and the gap between what the reader knows and what the character knows. Example:

"She checked the time. 11:47. Thirteen minutes. Her fingers found the handle of the door. Cold. She turned it. It did not move. From somewhere inside, she heard footsteps."

The short sentences, the ticking clock, and the unanswered question (Who is inside?) create tension.

Practice Task

Plan a story using this structure: Beginning (hook + character + situation), Middle (complication + rising tension), End (climax + resolution). Then write the story in 400-500 words.

Plan:
Beginning: A girl finds a key in a second-hand bookshop. No label, no owner. She puts it in her pocket.
Middle: She notices the key matches a lock on a door in her street she has never seen before. She tries it. It fits. Inside: a room frozen in time — photos, letters, a clock stopped at 3:15. She reads a letter addressed to someone called "Eleanor." It is signed with her grandmother's name.
End: She realises her grandmother once lived here. The clock stopped at the moment she left. She closes the door and takes the key with her — some doors are meant to stay closed.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a story planning template with prompts for each section. Reduce word count to 300 words.
Stretch:
Write a story with a non-linear structure (flashback, reverse chronology). Plan the events in chronological order first, then reorder.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Tension Techniques Checklist

Students read their own story and tick: ✓ Engaging opening? ✓ Character shown through action? ✓ Tension created? ✓ Clear climax? ✓ Satisfying ending?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Writes a complete narrative with clear structure, effective characterisation, and controlled tension
  • Getting there: Story has structure but some elements are underdeveloped
  • Not yet: Story lacks structure or resolution

Homework Suggestions

  • Write a complete short story (500-700 words) in response to a GCSE-style picture prompt
  • Write a story that begins with dialogue and ends with silence
  • Practise writing three different endings for the same story opening
  • Read the opening chapter of a novel and annotate the techniques the author uses to hook the reader
