WC1: Descriptive Writing

Foundation Higher All Boards

Create vivid, immersive descriptions of people, places, and events using sensory detail, figurative language, and varied sentence structures.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 4
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Write vivid descriptions using sensory detail (sight, sound, smell, touch, taste)
  • Use figurative language effectively (simile, metaphor, personification)
  • Create atmosphere and mood through word choice and sentence structure
  • Control pacing through paragraphing and sentence length
  • Describe people, places, and events from a clear viewpoint

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of figurative language techniques (R3)
  • Ability to plan before writing
  • Basic spelling, punctuation, and grammar

Materials & Equipment

  • Photographs or paintings of evocative scenes
  • Sensory vocabulary bank
  • Model descriptive writing pieces
  • Planning templates (mind maps, lists)
  • Writing frames for structure

Lesson 1: Sensory Detail

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Close Your Eyes

Ask students to close their eyes. Describe a busy market: the smell of spices, the sound of vendors calling, the heat of the sun, the colours of fruit stalls, the texture of rough wooden counters. Open eyes. Most people default to visual description — teach them to go beyond sight.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Effective descriptive writing engages all five senses:
  • Sight — colour, light, shadow, shape, movement (most common, but overused)
  • Sound — volume, pitch, rhythm, silence (often the most atmospheric sense)
  • Smell — the most memory-linked sense; creates immediate atmosphere
  • Touch — texture, temperature, pressure (makes description physical and immediate)
  • Taste — the most intimate sense; use sparingly for impact
Teach the "5-senses challenge": for every description, include at least three different senses. This transforms flat writing into immersive writing.
Worked Example

Weak: "The beach was nice. The water was blue and the sand was soft."

Strong: "The sand was warm beneath her bare feet, fine as sugar and impossibly white. The sea stretched out in bands of colour — turquoise near the shore, deepening to navy at the horizon. Somewhere behind her, a seagull screamed. The air smelled of salt and sunscreen, and when she licked her lips she could taste the sea — briny and sharp. A wave curled and broke with a sound like a long, satisfied sigh."

Practice Task

Write a one-paragraph description of a rainy street scene including sight, sound, and at least one other sense (80-100 words).

"The rain hammered the pavement in sheets, turning the street into a river of reflected neon. Puddles trembled with each drop, distorting the glow of traffic lights into smeared halos of red and amber. A bus hissed past, sending a wave of dirty water over the kerb. The air was thick with the smell of wet tarmac and exhaust fumes. She pulled her coat tighter, feeling the cold seep through the fabric, and tasted the metallic tang of rain on her lips."

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a senses checklist card. Students tick each sense as they include it.
Stretch:
Challenge students to include all five senses in one paragraph.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Sense Swap

Display a paragraph that only uses sight. Students rewrite it replacing one visual detail with a different sense.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Includes at least three senses with specific, precise details
  • Getting there: Includes two senses but details are generic
  • Not yet: Relies only on visual description with vague language

Lesson 2: Creating Atmosphere

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Two Rooms

Display two descriptions of the same room — one creates comfort, the other unease. Students identify three differences in language that create each atmosphere.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Atmosphere is created through consistent, controlled choices:
  • Word choice: positive words create warmth (golden, soft, gentle); negative words create unease (creeping, cold, shadowed)
  • Sentence length: short sentences create tension; long sentences create calm or build-up
  • Figurative language: metaphors and similes create vivid images that set the mood
  • Pathetic fallacy: weather and light reflecting the mood
  • Pacing: fast pacing creates excitement; slow pacing creates tension or contemplation
Teach students to decide the atmosphere BEFORE writing and maintain it consistently throughout.
Worked Example — Atmosphere: tension/unease

"The corridor stretched ahead, impossibly long. Every fluorescent light buzzed and flickered, casting the walls in a sickly, intermittent glow. Her footsteps echoed — too loud, too solitary — and she found herself holding her breath, listening. At the far end, a door stood ajar. Beyond it: silence. But it was not the silence of emptiness. It was the silence of something waiting."

Practice Task

Write a description (100-120 words) that creates an atmosphere of calm and peace.

"The garden lay still in the late afternoon light, every leaf and petal rendered in gold. A bee drifted lazily between the lavender bushes, its hum barely audible above the whisper of the breeze. The grass was soft and dry beneath her bare feet, warm from a day of sun. She settled into the old wooden chair and closed her eyes, feeling the heat on her face like a gentle hand. Somewhere in the distance, a blackbird sang — a single, clear note that hung in the air and slowly faded. Time seemed to slow, to stretch, to stop altogether."

Differentiation

Support:
Provide an atmosphere word bank. Students select from the bank rather than generating from scratch.
Stretch:
Challenge students to shift the atmosphere mid-paragraph — from calm to unease — using a single sentence as the turning point.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Atmosphere Detective

Read an anonymised paragraph. Students identify the intended atmosphere and list three techniques used.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Creates a consistent, clear atmosphere through controlled word choice and imagery
  • Getting there: Creates some atmosphere but it is inconsistent
  • Not yet: Writes descriptively without controlling mood or atmosphere

Lesson 3: Pacing and Structure

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Speed Reading

Display two paragraphs about a chase scene: one with long sentences, one with short, punchy sentences. Which feels faster?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach how sentence structure controls pacing:
  • Short sentences — create tension, drama, emphasis
  • Long sentences — create calm, build-up, or flowing movement
  • Lists (tricolons) — build rhythm and momentum
  • One-sentence paragraphs — create dramatic emphasis (use sparingly)
  • Paragraph breaks — signal shifts in focus, time, or intensity
The best descriptive writing varies pacing: building up with longer sentences, then hitting hard with a short one.
Worked Example

"The market hummed with life. Vendors called out prices, children chased each other between the stalls, and the smell of fried onions filled the air. It was ordinary. It was everyday. It was the last time any of them would see it like this."

The long second sentence builds a picture. Then three short sentences disrupt the flow, creating emphasis and signalling something significant is about to change.

Practice Task

Write a description of a storm approaching (120-150 words). Start with calm (long sentences), build tension (medium), climax with the storm hitting (short sentences).

"The afternoon had settled into a hazy, golden stillness, the kind that makes you forget anything could ever change. The trees stood motionless, their leaves hanging limp in the heavy air. Even the birds had gone quiet. Then, at the edge of the sky, a darkness gathered — slow at first, then faster, rolling towards them like a great grey wave. The temperature dropped. The first gust of wind rattled the windows. She looked up. The sky was black. The rain came. Not gently. Not gradually. It arrived all at once, hammering the roof, flooding the gutters, turning the world into a wall of water. She slammed the window shut. The storm had begun."

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a sentence-length guide with examples of each length.
Stretch:
Ask students to experiment with sentence fragments for dramatic effect: "Silence. Nothing but silence."

Plenary (5 minutes)

Pacing Map

Students draw a simple graph showing the pacing of their paragraph (slow/fast, calm/tense). Compare shapes as a class.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Uses varied sentence lengths deliberately to control pacing and create impact
  • Getting there: Some variation in sentence length but not always purposeful
  • Not yet: Sentences are all similar length with no pacing control

Lesson 4: Viewpoint and Descriptive Writing

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Whose Eyes?

Display a description of a city. Ask: who is describing this? A tourist? A resident? A child? An alien? Show how viewpoint changes what details are noticed.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Every description has a viewpoint — a character or persona through whose eyes the reader sees. Teach:
  • First person ("I") — immediate, personal, subjective
  • Third person limited ("she/he") — close but slightly more objective
  • Close third person — follows one character's thoughts closely
Viewpoint determines what details are included: a child notices different things than an adult; a scared person notices different things than a happy one. Teach students to choose a viewpoint BEFORE writing and let it filter every detail.
Worked Example — Same scene, two viewpoints

Child: "The library was huge, bigger than their bedroom, bigger than the garden. Books went all the way up to the ceiling — how did anyone reach them? A lady sat behind a desk and whispered hello, and her voice was the loudest sound in the whole building."

Adult: "The library was a cathedral of silence. Sunlight fell through tall windows in dusty columns, illuminating rows of books that stood like sentinels along the walls. The librarian looked up from her desk, her expression measured — the look of someone who had seen too many overdue fines."

Practice Task

Write a description of a busy train station from two different viewpoints: a nervous first-time traveller and a tired commuter. 80 words each.

Nervous first-timer: "The station was enormous and terrifying. People rushed past in every direction, all of them looking like they knew exactly where they were going. Boards flashed with names she did not recognise. Somewhere, a voice announced a departure — too fast, too muffled. She clutched her ticket and checked the time. Her train was in four minutes. She was already late.

Tired commuter: "The 7:52 was three minutes late again. He did not bother checking the board — Platform 4, same as every weekday for the past eleven years. The coffee in his hand was already lukewarm. Around him, the morning crowd shuffled and coughed and stared at their phones, all performing the same ritual of not-quite-waking-up. He stepped onto the train, found his seat, and closed his eyes."

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a viewpoint frame: "Through [character]'s eyes, the [place] looks/feels/sounds like…"
Stretch:
Write the same scene from an omniscient viewpoint that shifts between characters mid-paragraph.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Whose Perspective?

Display a paragraph. Students write one sentence identifying whose viewpoint it is and what they would notice next.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Maintains a consistent viewpoint that filters all descriptive details
  • Getting there: Establishes a viewpoint but it sometimes drops
  • Not yet: Writes without a clear viewpoint, listing details without a character's perspective

Homework Suggestions

  • Write a 200-word description of your favourite place, including at least four senses
  • Describe the same scene from two different characters' viewpoints
  • Find a descriptive passage in a novel and annotate the sensory details, figurative language, and pacing techniques
  • Practise writing one-sentence paragraphs for dramatic effect in a piece of descriptive writing
