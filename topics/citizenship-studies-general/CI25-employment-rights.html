Lesson 2: Core Concepts: employment rights

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on employment rights. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: The National Minimum Wage sets legal pay floors based on age: lower rates for under-18s and 18-20 year olds, a higher rate for 21-22 year olds, and the National Living Wage for workers aged 23+.
Key Fact: Workers have the right to a written statement of employment terms from day one, including pay, hours, holiday entitlement, and notice periods.
Key Fact: The maximum working week is 48 hours (averaged over 17 weeks) unless the worker voluntarily opts out. Young workers (under 18) are limited to 40 hours per week and 8 hours per day.
Key Fact: Workers are entitled to at least 5.6 weeks (28 days for full-time) of paid holiday per year, which can include bank holidays.
Key Fact: The Health and Safety at Work Act 1974 requires employers to provide a safe working environment, and employees have a duty to follow safety procedures.
Key Fact: Unfair dismissal protection applies after 2 years of continuous employment (1 year in Northern Ireland). Employers must follow fair procedures and have a valid reason for dismissal.

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Evaluate the extent to which employment law protects workers from exploitation. [12 marks] <div class="

UK employment law provides significant protections. The National Minimum Wage ensures baseline income, and the Employment Rights Act 1996 gives unfair dismissal protection after two years. Health and safety legislation requires safe conditions, and the Equality Act prohibits workplace discrimination. These protections have real impact. The minimum wage has lifted millions from poverty wages since 1999. Employment tribunals provide a mechanism to challenge unfair treatment, and trade unions negotiate better pay and conditions. However, significant gaps remain. The two-year qualifying period for unfair dismissal means new employees have limited protection. Zero-hours contracts create income insecurity. The gig economy has exposed how companies can avoid employee rights by classifying workers as self-employed. Enforcement is problematic. Minimum wage enforcement relies on workers reporting employers, which many fear to do. The Supreme Court's Uber ruling improved gig worker rights, but enforcement is inconsistent. Exam Tips: Distinguish between 'employees' (full rights), 'workers' (some rights), and 'self-employed' (few employment rights) | Use current National Minimum Wage figures in your answers if possible | When evaluating, consider both the legal framework and real-world enforcement
Common Errors: ✗ All workers have the same employment rights. ✓ Rights depend on employment status. Employees have full rights (unfair dismissal, redundancy pay). Workers have some rights (minimum wage, holiday). The self-employed have few employment rights. ✗ Zero-hours contracts are illegal. ✓ Zero-hours contracts are legal but controversial. Workers on them still have some rights including the National Minimum Wage and holiday pay, but have no guaranteed income. ✗ Trade unions can force employers to agree to their demands. ✓ Trade unions negotiate with employers but cannot force agreements. Industrial action requires a ballot and is a last resort after negotiation fails.
