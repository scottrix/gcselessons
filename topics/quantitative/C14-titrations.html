C14: Titrations

Higher All Boards

Carry out and calculate acid-alkali titrations to find unknown concentrations of solutions.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the titration method and calculate unknown concentrations from titration results
  • Carry out titration calculations involving dilution and multi-step problems

Prerequisites

  • Relative Masses and Moles (C10)
  • Concentration (C12)
  • Burette, pipette, conical flask, indicator
  • White tile
  • Safety goggles

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Burette, pipette, conical flask, indicator
  • White tile
  • Safety goggles and gloves

Lesson 1: Titration Method and Calculations

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Exact Measurements

How would you find out exactly how much acid neutralises exactly 25 cm³ of alkali? You can't just pour and guess — you need a precise method. That method is titration.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Titration is a technique used to find the exact volume of one solution that reacts completely with a known volume of another solution.

Apparatus:
• Burette (graduated to 0.1 cm³, holds up to 50 cm³)
• Pipette (accurately measures 25.0 cm³)
• Conical flask
• Indicator (e.g. phenolphthalein — colourless in acid, pink in alkali)
• White tile (to see colour change clearly)

Method:
1. Fill burette with acid (or base). Record initial reading.
2. Use pipette to transfer 25.0 cm³ of alkali into conical flask. Add indicator.
3. Slowly add acid from burette while swirling flask.
4. Near the endpoint, add acid drop by drop.
5. Stop when indicator changes colour permanently. Record final reading.
6. Repeat until you get two concordant results (within 0.1 cm³).
7. Calculate the average titre from concordant results only.

Calculation:
Use: moles = concentration × volume
Then use the mole ratio from the balanced equation.
Then: concentration = moles / volume

Important: Always convert volume to dm³ by dividing by 1000.
Finding concentration of HCl

25.0 cm³ of 0.1 mol/dm³ NaOH neutralises 23.2 cm³ of HCl.
NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O
Moles of NaOH = 0.1 × 0.025 = 0.0025 mol
Mole ratio NaOH:HCl = 1:1, so moles of HCl = 0.0025
Concentration of HCl = 0.0025 / 0.0232 = 0.108 mol/dm³

Concordant results

Titration results: 23.1, 23.4, 23.2, 23.2 cm³
23.1 and 23.4 differ by 0.3 cm³ — not concordant.
23.2 and 23.2 differ by 0 cm³ — concordant!
Average titre = (23.2 + 23.2) / 2 = 23.2 cm³
Only use concordant results for calculations.

Titration calculation steps:
1. moles = C × V (for the known solution)
2. Use mole ratio from equation
3. C = n / V (for the unknown solution)

Always: V must be in dm³ (÷ 1000)

Concordant results: within 0.1 cm³ of each other
Average titre = mean of concordant results only

Common Misconception

You should use all titration results to calculate the average, even if one is very different.

Only use concordant results (within 0.1 cm³). An outlier may indicate a mistake (overshot endpoint, air bubble in burette). Include all concordant results, not just the two closest to each other.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a step-by-step titration calculation template. Use the analogy of a recipe: the titre tells you exactly how much acid 'fits' with the alkali.
Stretch:
Design an experiment to determine the concentration of vinegar (ethanoic acid) by titrating with sodium hydroxide. Include safety precautions and expected results.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Complete a titration calculation from given data: 25 cm³ of 0.5 mol/dm³ KOH neutralises 20 cm³ of H₂SO₄. Find the concentration of H₂SO₄. (Hint: the mole ratio is 2:1)

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Carries out titration accurately and performs calculations step by step, using concordant results.
  • Getting there: Can perform titration but makes errors in unit conversions or mole ratio.
  • Not yet: Cannot use the titration method. Reteach with simplified calculations first, then practical demonstration.

Lesson 2: Advanced Titration Problems

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Dilution Puzzle

If you dilute 10 cm³ of 1 mol/dm³ HCl to 100 cm³, what is the new concentration? (Answer: 0.1 mol/dm³ — same moles, 10× more volume.)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
This lesson covers more complex titration scenarios:

1. Dilution calculations:
When you dilute a solution, the moles of solute stay the same, but the volume increases.
C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ (concentration × volume before = concentration × volume after)

2. Titrations with different mole ratios:
Not all acid-alkali reactions are 1:1.
H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O (ratio 1:2)
H₃PO₄ + 3NaOH → Na₃PO₄ + 3H₂O (ratio 1:3)

3. Finding the formula of an unknown acid:
Titrate a known volume of acid with known concentration alkali. Use the titre to find how many moles of alkali react with the acid. This tells you the ratio and therefore the formula.

4. Titrations with polyprotic acids:
Sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) has 2 H⁺ ions → reacts with 2 NaOH
Phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) has 3 H⁺ ions → reacts with 3 NaOH
Dilution calculation

25 cm³ of 0.5 mol/dm³ NaOH is diluted to 250 cm³. What is the new concentration?
C₁V₁ = C₂V₂
0.5 × 25 = C₂ × 250
C₂ = 12.5 / 250 = 0.05 mol/dm³

Identifying an unknown acid

25.0 cm³ of an unknown acid is titrated with 0.1 mol/dm³ NaOH. The average titre is 25.0 cm³.
Moles of NaOH = 0.1 × 0.025 = 0.0025 mol
If the acid is monoprotic (1 H⁺), moles of acid = 0.0025, so concentration = 0.0025/0.025 = 0.1 mol/dm³
If the acid is diprotic (2 H⁺), moles of acid = 0.00125, so concentration = 0.05 mol/dm³
The titre matching the pipette volume suggests a 1:1 ratio → monoprotic acid.

Dilution: C₁V₁ = C₂V₂

H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
Mole ratio H₂SO₄ : NaOH = 1 : 2

For diprotic acids:
moles of acid = moles of alkali / 2
C₁V₁ = n (acid) = (C₂V₂) / 2

Common Misconception

When you dilute a solution, the number of moles of solute changes.

Dilution only changes the volume and concentration — the number of moles stays the same. You're adding more solvent (water), not more solute. C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ because moles are constant.

Differentiation

Support:
Use the dilution formula explicitly: C₁V₁ = C₂V₂. Label each value clearly before substituting. Check: does the answer make sense? (More dilute = lower concentration.)
Stretch:
A 0.1 mol/dm³ solution of H₃PO₄ is titrated with 0.1 mol/dm³ NaOH. What volume of NaOH is needed to neutralise 25 cm³ of the acid?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Three titration calculations: one straightforward, one with dilution, one with a diprotic acid. Self-mark.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Handles dilution and different mole ratios confidently in titration calculations.
  • Getting there: Can do straightforward titrations but makes errors with dilution or polyprotic acids.
  • Not yet: Cannot use the dilution formula. Reteach C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ with simple examples.

Homework Suggestions

  • Practise titration calculations — 15 problems
  • If possible, perform a real titration at home (vinegar and baking soda solution)
  • Research: how are titrations used in the food industry to test the acidity of products?
