C11: Reacting Masses

Foundation All Boards

Use balanced equations and the mole concept to calculate masses of reactants and products in chemical reactions.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Write balanced symbol equations and use them to calculate reacting masses using moles
  • Apply mole calculations to multi-step reacting mass problems

Prerequisites

  • Relative Masses and Moles (C10) — mole concept, Mr calculations
  • Scientific calculator

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Whiteboard for equation writing

Lesson 1: Balancing Equations and Mole Ratios

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Recipe Maths

A cake recipe uses 2 eggs for every 1 bag of flour. If you have 6 eggs, how much flour do you need? Chemistry works the same way — balanced equations tell us the 'recipe' ratio.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Balanced equations show the exact ratio in which substances react. The numbers in front of formulae (coefficients) tell us how many moles of each substance are involved.

Steps to calculate reacting masses:
1. Write the balanced symbol equation.
2. Write the Mr of each substance below its formula.
3. Write the mole ratio from the equation.
4. Convert moles to mass: mass = moles × Mr

How to balance equations:
• Count atoms of each element on both sides
• Add coefficients to make numbers equal
• Never change the formula (subscripts) — only change coefficients
• Balance metals first, then non-metals, then hydrogen and oxygen last
Mass of CO₂ from burning methane

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
Mr: 16 + 64 → 44 + 36

Mass of methane = 32g
Moles of CH₄ = 32/16 = 2 mol
Moles of CO₂ = 2 mol (1:1 ratio)
Mass of CO₂ = 2 × 44 = 88g

Mass of acid needed

What mass of hydrochloric acid is needed to react completely with 10g of calcium carbonate?
CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂
Mr: 100 + 73 → 111 + 18 + 44

Moles of CaCO₃ = 10/100 = 0.1 mol
Moles of HCl = 0.1 × 2 = 0.2 mol (1:2 ratio)
Mass of HCl = 0.2 × 36.5 = 7.3g

Step 1: Balance the equation
Step 2: Calculate Mr for each substance
Step 3: Use mole ratio
Step 4: mass = moles × Mr

Key: moles of A / coefficient A = moles of B / coefficient B

e.g. 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
moles of Mg / 2 = moles of MgO / 2

Common Misconception

You can just use the masses directly without converting to moles.

Masses are not in the same ratio as the equation coefficients because different substances have different Mr values. You must convert to moles first, use the mole ratio, then convert back to mass.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a template with steps clearly numbered. Always start with the mole ratio from the balanced equation.
Stretch:
Calculate: if 20g of calcium carbonate reacts with excess hydrochloric acid, what volume of CO₂ is produced at RTP? (Hint: 1 mol of gas = 24 dm³ at RTP)

Plenary (5 minutes)

Three quick calculations: given a balanced equation and mass of one reactant, find the mass of a product. Self-mark.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Follows the 4-step method accurately for any balanced equation.
  • Getting there: Can balance equations but makes errors in mole ratio or Mr calculations.
  • Not yet: Cannot balance equations or use mole ratios. Reteach with the cake recipe analogy.

Lesson 2: Advanced Reacting Mass Problems

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Equation Balancing Race

Balance these equations as quickly as possible: Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃, Al + HCl → AlCl₃ + H₂, C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
This lesson tackles more complex reacting mass problems, including:
• Finding the limiting reactant
• Calculating percentage yield from reacting masses
• Multi-step calculations involving several reactions

Limiting reactant:
In a reaction, one reactant is used up first — this is the limiting reactant. It determines the maximum amount of product that can be formed. The other reactant is in excess.

How to find the limiting reactant:
1. Convert mass of each reactant to moles
2. Divide each by its coefficient in the balanced equation
3. The smallest value identifies the limiting reactant

Percentage yield:
% yield = (actual mass / theoretical mass) × 100%
Theoretical mass = what the balanced equation predicts
Actual mass = what you actually get (usually less due to losses)
Finding the limiting reactant

10g of magnesium reacts with 10g of hydrochloric acid. Which is in excess?
Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂
Moles of Mg = 10/24 = 0.417 mol
Moles of HCl = 10/36.5 = 0.274 mol
Divide by coefficient: Mg = 0.417/1 = 0.417, HCl = 0.274/2 = 0.137
HCl is the limiting reactant (smaller value). Mg is in excess.

Percentage yield

Theoretical yield of product = 15g. Actual yield = 12.3g.
% yield = (12.3/15) × 100 = 82%
18% was lost — possibly left in the flask, or some product evaporated.

Limiting reactant test:
moles / coefficient → smallest = limiting reactant

Percentage yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100%

Theoretical mass = moles of limiting reactant × (coefficient product / coefficient limiting) × Mr product

Common Misconception

The reactant with the smallest mass is always the limiting reactant.

The limiting reactant is not determined by mass alone — it depends on both the mass AND the mole ratio. You must convert to moles and compare using the balanced equation coefficients.

Differentiation

Support:
Always use the mole ratio — don't try to shortcut by comparing masses directly. Write Mr and moles clearly under each substance.
Stretch:
In the Haber process: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. If 28g of N₂ reacts with 8g of H₂, which is in excess? What mass of NH₃ is produced?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Two quick limiting reactant problems. Identify the limiting reactant and calculate the mass of product formed.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Identifies limiting reactants and calculates theoretical/actual yields accurately.
  • Getting there: Can calculate theoretical yield but struggles with limiting reactant identification.
  • Not yet: Cannot apply mole ratio to multi-step problems. Reteach with simpler whole-number examples first.

Homework Suggestions

  • Complete 20 reacting mass calculations from balanced equations
  • Write and balance 10 equations then calculate reacting masses
  • Research: why is the Haber process (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃) so important for making fertiliser?
