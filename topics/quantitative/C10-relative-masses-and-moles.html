C10: Relative Masses and Moles

Foundation All Boards

Understand the concept of the mole, molar mass, and how to convert between moles, mass and number of particles.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define the mole and Avogadro's constant, and calculate the number of particles
  • Calculate the relative formula mass (Mr) and molar mass of compounds
  • Convert between moles, mass and number of particles in calculations

Prerequisites

  • Atoms and Elements (C1) — atomic mass, periodic table
  • Calculator

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator

Lesson 1: The Mole Concept

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

How Do We Count Atoms?

If you had to count 1 million grains of rice, how long would it take? Atoms are far too small and numerous to count individually. So how do chemists count them? They use a special number: the mole.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The mole is the standard unit for the amount of a substance. 1 mole = 6.02 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's constant, L).

Why 6.02 × 10²³? Because 1 mole of carbon-12 has a mass of exactly 12 grams. This number makes the maths work out neatly.

What counts as a 'particle'?
• Atoms (for elements like Fe, He, Cu)
• Molecules (for molecular elements like O₂, N₂, H₂O)
• Formula units (for ionic compounds like NaCl, MgO)

Key relationship:
Number of moles (n) = mass (m) ÷ molar mass (M)

Molar mass (M) = mass of 1 mole of a substance, in g/mol. It is the same number as the relative atomic/formula/molecular mass, but in grams.
• Ar of carbon = 12 → M of carbon = 12 g/mol
• Mr of water (H₂O) = (1×2) + 16 = 18 → M = 18 g/mol

Counting particles:
Number of particles = number of moles × Avogadro's constant
How many moles in 36g of water?

Mr(H₂O) = 18, so M = 18 g/mol
n = m/M = 36/18 = 2 moles
Number of molecules = 2 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.204 × 10²⁴ molecules

How many atoms in 2 moles of helium?

He is monatomic (single atoms).
Number of atoms = 2 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.204 × 10²⁴ atoms

n = m / M

n = number of moles (mol)
m = mass (g)
M = molar mass (g/mol)

Number of particles = n × 6.02 × 10²³

M(H₂O) = 18 g/mol
M(NaCl) = 58.5 g/mol
M(CO₂) = 44 g/mol

Common Misconception

A mole is always 6.02 × 10²³ grams.

A mole is 6.02 × 10²³ PARTICLES, not grams. The mass of 1 mole depends on the substance: 1 mol of water = 18g, 1 mol of iron = 56g, 1 mol of CO₂ = 44g.

Differentiation

Support:
Use a balance to weigh out 1 mole of different substances. Children can see that 1 mole of different substances has different masses but always contains the same number of particles.
Stretch:
Calculate the number of molecules in a drop of water (about 0.05 g). How does this compare to the number of people on Earth?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick calculations: How many moles in 80g of NaOH? How many particles in 3 moles of O₂?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Confidently converts between mass, moles, and number of particles using n = m/M and N = n × L.
  • Getting there: Can use n = m/M but makes errors with large numbers in particle calculations.
  • Not yet: Cannot use the mole equation. Reteach using physical analogy: 1 dozen = 12 items, 1 mole = 6.02 × 10²³ items.

Lesson 2: Molar Mass from Formulae

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Mr Calculation Race

Write these formulae on the board: H₂O, NaCl, Ca(OH)₂, H₂SO₄, Mg(NO₃)₂. Calculate Mr for each. First to finish all five correctly wins.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
To calculate Mr (relative formula mass):
1. Write down the number of atoms of each element in the formula.
2. Multiply by the relative atomic mass (Ar) of each element (from periodic table).
3. Add them all together.

Brackets in formulae:
Ca(OH)₂ means: 1 Ca, 2 O, 2 H — the 2 outside the bracket multiplies everything inside.
Mg(NO₃)₂ means: 1 Mg, 2 N, 6 O — the 2 multiplies both N and 3×O.

Molar mass = Mr expressed in g/mol. So if Mr(H₂O) = 18, the molar mass of water is 18 g/mol.

Common Mr values to learn:
H₂O = 18, CO₂ = 44, NaCl = 58.5, NaOH = 40, H₂SO₄ = 98, CaCO₃ = 100, Fe₂O₃ = 160
Mr of calcium hydroxide Ca(OH)₂

Ca = 40 (×1 = 40)
O = 16 (×2 = 32)
H = 1 (×2 = 2)
Mr = 40 + 32 + 2 = 74

Mr of sulfuric acid H₂SO₄

H = 1 (×2 = 2)
S = 32 (×1 = 32)
O = 16 (×4 = 64)
Mr = 2 + 32 + 64 = 98

Mr = Σ(Ar × number of atoms of each element)

Ca(OH)₂: Ca×1 + O×2 + H×2 = 40 + 32 + 2 = 74
H₂SO₄: H×2 + S×1 + O×4 = 2 + 32 + 64 = 98
CaCO₃: Ca×1 + C×1 + O×3 = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100

Common Misconception

Ca(OH)₂ has 1 O and 2 H because the bracket only applies to H.

The subscript 2 outside the bracket multiplies EVERYTHING inside: 2 × O and 2 × H. So Ca(OH)₂ has 1 Ca, 2 O, and 2 H.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a periodic table with Ar values highlighted. Show the step-by-step method written out clearly. Use colour to show which atoms come from which part of the formula.
Stretch:
Calculate the Mr of complicated formulae: Cu₂(OH)₂CO₃ (malachite), Al₂(SO₄)₃, Ca₃(PO₄)₂.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Rapid fire: calculate Mr for MgO, KCl, Na₂SO₄, Al₂O₃, FeCl₃. Check answers together.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Calculates Mr accurately for any formula, including those with brackets.
  • Getting there: Can do simple formulae but makes errors with brackets and subscripts.
  • Not yet: Cannot calculate Mr. Reteach with explicit step-by-step method and physical model using coloured beads for each element.

Lesson 3: Combined Calculations

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Fire Conversions

Convert: 36g water to moles. 2.5 mol CO₂ to grams. 3.01 × 10²³ atoms of He to moles. Race to finish first.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
This lesson consolidates all mole calculations. Children practise converting in all directions: mass ↔ moles ↔ number of particles.

Three key equations:
1. n = m / M (moles from mass)
2. m = n × M (mass from moles)
3. Number of particles = n × 6.02 × 10²³

Problem-solving strategy:
• What are you given?
• What do you need to find?
• Which equation links them?
• Are the units correct? (mass in grams, volume in dm³, not cm³)

Common exam-style questions:
• Calculate the number of moles in a given mass
• Calculate the mass of a given number of moles
• Calculate the number of particles in a given mass
• Calculate the mass of a single atom or molecule (divide by Avogadro's constant)
Mass of one atom of iron

M(Fe) = 56 g/mol
Mass of one atom = 56 / (6.02 × 10²³) = 9.3 × 10⁻²³ g
This is incredibly small — atoms really are tiny!

Which has more atoms: 12g of carbon or 24g of magnesium?

Carbon: n = 12/12 = 1 mol → 6.02 × 10²³ atoms
Magnesium: n = 24/24 = 1 mol → 6.02 × 10²³ atoms
They have the SAME number of atoms! Both are 1 mole. Different mass, same number of particles.

The three conversions:

mass (g) ←→ moles (mol) ←→ number of particles
    ÷M                  ×L
    ×M                  ÷L

Where L = 6.02 × 10²³ (Avogadro's constant)

Common Misconception

12g of carbon has more atoms than 24g of magnesium because carbon has a smaller atomic mass.

Both contain exactly 1 mole = 6.02 × 10²³ atoms. The key is the RATIO of mass to molar mass, not the absolute mass. 12g/12 = 1 mol. 24g/24 = 1 mol. Same number of atoms.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a conversion flowchart: mass → (÷M) → moles → (×L) → particles. Children can follow the arrows.
Stretch:
A sample contains 3.01 × 10²³ molecules of water. What mass is this? How many hydrogen atoms? How many oxygen atoms?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Five rapid calculations covering all three conversion types. Self-mark and discuss.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Confidently performs any mole calculation, converting between mass, moles and particles.
  • Getting there: Can do straightforward calculations but makes errors with multi-step problems.
  • Not yet: Cannot link the three equations. Reteach with the flowchart method.

Homework Suggestions

  • Calculate Mr for 20 compounds
  • Convert between moles, mass and number of particles for 15 different problems
  • Weigh out 1 mole of salt and 1 mole of sugar — compare masses
