C12: Concentration and Yield

Foundation All Boards

Calculate the concentration of solutions in g/dm³ and mol/dm³, and understand percentage yield in chemical reactions.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Calculate the concentration of a solution in g/dm³ and mol/dm³, and convert between them
  • Calculate percentage yield and explain why actual yield is usually less than theoretical yield

Prerequisites

  • Relative Masses and Moles (C10) — mole concept, Mr calculations
  • Scientific calculator

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Volumetric flask for solution preparation demo

Lesson 1: Concentration of Solutions

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Strong or Weak?

Taste (but don't swallow!) a very dilute salt solution. Now taste a strong one. What's the difference? Concentration tells us how much solute is dissolved in a given volume of solvent.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Concentration = amount of solute per unit volume of solution.

Units of concentration:
• g/dm³ = grams of solute per cubic decimetre of solution
• mol/dm³ = moles of solute per cubic decimetre of solution (more useful in calculations)

Conversions:
1 dm³ = 1000 cm³ = 1 litre
1 cm³ = 1 mL

Equation:
Concentration (mol/dm³) = moles of solute (mol) / volume of solution (dm³)

To convert g/dm³ to mol/dm³: divide by Mr
To convert mol/dm³ to g/dm³: multiply by Mr

Making a solution of known concentration:
1. Calculate mass needed: mass = concentration × volume × Mr
2. Weigh the solute
3. Dissolve in some water in a beaker
4. Transfer to a volumetric flask
5. Rinse beaker and add washings to flask
6. Top up to the mark with distilled water
7. Stopper and shake
Calculate concentration of salt solution

5.85g of NaCl dissolved in water to make 500cm³ of solution.
Mr(NaCl) = 58.5
Moles = 5.85 / 58.5 = 0.1 mol
Volume = 500 cm³ = 0.5 dm³
Concentration = 0.1 / 0.5 = 0.2 mol/dm³

Making a solution

Make 250cm³ of 0.1 mol/dm³ NaOH solution.
Mr(NaOH) = 40
Mass = concentration × volume × Mr = 0.1 × 0.25 × 40 = 1g
Weigh 1g NaOH, dissolve, transfer to 250cm³ volumetric flask, top up to mark.

Concentration (mol/dm³) = n / V

n = moles (mol)
V = volume (dm³)

To make a solution:
mass (g) = C (mol/dm³) × V (dm³) × Mr

1 dm³ = 1000 cm³
Volume in dm³ = volume in cm³ ÷ 1000

Common Misconception

cm³ and dm³ are the same thing.

1 dm³ = 1000 cm³. When using the concentration formula, volume must be in dm³. A common error is using cm³ directly — always convert first by dividing by 1000.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a conversion table: cm³ → dm³ by dividing by 1000. Use colour-coded formula cards. Always check: does the answer make sense?
Stretch:
A solution has concentration 2 g/dm³ and Mr = 50. What is the concentration in mol/dm³? How many grams of solute are in 250 cm³?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick calculations: find concentration given moles and volume; find mass needed to make a solution of known concentration.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Confidently converts between g/dm³ and mol/dm³ and calculates mass needed to make solutions.
  • Getting there: Can calculate concentration but forgets to convert cm³ to dm³.
  • Not yet: Cannot use the concentration formula. Reteach with physical demonstrations using food colouring in water.

Lesson 2: Percentage Yield

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Theoretical vs Real

If a recipe makes 12 cookies but you only get 10 out of the oven (2 burned/stuck), what percentage of the recipe did you actually get? Chemistry is the same — reactions rarely give 100% of the theoretical yield.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Theoretical yield = the maximum amount of product that could be formed from the given amounts of reactants (calculated from the balanced equation).

Actual yield = the amount of product actually obtained from the reaction (measured experimentally).

Percentage yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100%

Why is actual yield usually less than theoretical?
• Some product is lost during separation and purification
• The reaction may not go to completion (reversible reactions reach equilibrium)
• Some product may remain dissolved in solution
• Side reactions may produce unwanted products

Percentage yield is important in industry because it tells companies how efficient a process is. Higher yield = less waste = more profitable and more sustainable.
Calculating percentage yield

Theoretical yield of aspirin = 10g. Actual yield = 7.6g.
Percentage yield = (7.6 / 10) × 100 = 76%.
24% was lost — maybe some stayed in the flask, or some decomposed.

Why yield matters industrially

In the Haber process for making ammonia, only about 15% of the N₂ and H₂ convert to NH₃ in one pass. The unreacted gases are recycled. Even 15% yield makes billions of tonnes of ammonia per year because of the scale.

Percentage yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100%

Theoretical yield: calculate from balanced equation (reacting masses)
Actual yield: measured from experiment

Typical yield: 50-90% for most reactions

Common Misconception

A low percentage yield means the experiment was done wrong.

Low yields are normal and expected. Even in industry, yields below 100% are common. Factors like equilibrium position, product loss during purification, and side reactions all reduce yield. The goal is to maximise yield, not necessarily reach 100%.

Differentiation

Support:
Start with the cookie recipe analogy. Then use simple whole-number examples before introducing realistic data.
Stretch:
In the Haber process: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g). If 28g of N₂ reacts and 30g of NH₃ is collected, calculate the percentage yield.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Calculate percentage yield for three scenarios. Discuss: what could have caused the yield to be less than 100%?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Calculates percentage yield correctly and can explain reasons for yields below 100%.
  • Getting there: Can calculate percentage yield but struggles with calculating theoretical yield from equations.
  • Not yet: Cannot calculate percentage yield. Reteach with simple whole-number examples first.

Homework Suggestions

  • Calculate concentrations for 10 different solutions
  • Design an experiment to find the concentration of an unknown salt solution
  • Research: what is atom economy and how does it differ from percentage yield?
