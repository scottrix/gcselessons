C9: Nanoscience and Nanoparticles

Higher All Boards

Explore the properties of materials at the nanoscale and their applications in medicine, technology and everyday products.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define nanoparticles, explain their scale, and describe why they have different properties from bulk materials
  • Evaluate the benefits and risks of using nanoparticles in products and medicine

Prerequisites

  • Bonding (C5-C7) — ionic, covalent and metallic bonding
  • States of Matter (C8) — particle model, surface area

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Magnifying glass for examining nanoparticles (if available)
  • Computer/tablet for nanoparticle simulations
  • Printed pro/con table template

Lesson 1: What Are Nanoparticles?

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

How Small Is Nano?

A human hair is about 80,000 nm wide. A gold nanoparticle might be 10 nm wide. How many gold nanoparticles could fit across a human hair? (Answer: 8,000)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Nanoparticles are particles between 1 and 100 nanometres (nm) in size. 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m = one billionth of a metre.

At the nanoscale, materials behave differently from their bulk (normal-sized) forms:

Why nanoparticles are different:
• High surface area to volume ratio: As particles get smaller, the proportion of atoms on the surface increases dramatically. Surface atoms are more reactive because they are not fully surrounded by other atoms.
• Different optical properties: Gold nanoparticles appear red or purple (not gold) due to how they interact with light.
• Increased reactivity: More surface area → more contact → faster reactions.

Applications of nanoparticles:
• Sun cream: Titanium dioxide (TiO₂) nanoparticles — transparent (don't leave white residue) but still absorb UV light
• Medicine: Targeted drug delivery — nanoparticles can carry drugs directly to cancer cells
• Catalysts: High surface area → more effective catalysts
• Electronics: Smaller, faster computer chips
• Clothing: Nano-coatings make fabrics water-repellent and stain-resistant
Surface area to volume ratio

A 1 cm³ cube has surface area 6 cm². Cut it into 1mm cubes (1000 pieces): total surface area = 60 cm². Cut into 100nm particles: surface area is now 60,000 m² (a football pitch!). Same mass, vastly more surface exposed.

Gold nanoparticles in medicine

Gold nanoparticles can be coated with molecules that bind to cancer cells. They are then injected into the body. When exposed to infrared light, the gold nanoparticles heat up and destroy the cancer cells, without harming surrounding healthy tissue.

1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m

Surface area to volume ratio:
Cube side = s
Surface area = 6s², Volume = s³
SA:V = 6/s

As s → 0 (smaller particles), SA:V → ∞ (ratio increases dramatically)

Common Misconception

Nanoparticles are just very small versions of the bulk material with the same properties.

At the nanoscale, materials can have completely different properties. Gold nanoparticles are red/purple, not gold-coloured. Nano-TiO₂ is transparent, not white. The high surface area to volume ratio and quantum effects cause these differences.

Differentiation

Support:
Use the cube-cutting demonstration: draw a cube on the board, cut it in half, then quarters. Show how total surface area increases while total volume stays the same.
Stretch:
Research the ethical concerns around nanoparticles: could they be harmful to health or the environment? What safety testing is needed?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Give three examples of nanoparticle applications. For each, explain WHY nanoparticles are better than the bulk material.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains why nanoparticles have different properties and gives relevant applications with reasoning.
  • Getting there: Knows some applications but cannot fully explain why nano-scale changes properties.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain surface area to volume ratio. Reteach with the cube-cutting demonstration.

Lesson 2: Nanoparticles in Society

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Friend or Foe?

Nanoparticles in sun cream protect against skin cancer. But could they also be absorbed through the skin and cause harm? How do we decide if a technology is safe?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Benefits of nanotechnology:
• Medicine: targeted drug delivery reduces side effects, nano-bandages promote faster wound healing
• Environment: nano-catalysts reduce pollution, nano-filters clean water more efficiently
• Electronics: smaller, faster, more energy-efficient devices
• Energy: nano-solar cells are cheaper and more efficient

Potential risks:
• Health: nanoparticles are small enough to pass through cell membranes and enter the bloodstream. Long-term effects are not yet fully known.
• Environment: nanoparticles released into water or soil could harm organisms. They may accumulate in the food chain.

Key principle: New technologies should be tested thoroughly before widespread use. The precautionary principle suggests we should be cautious when the effects are unknown.

Regulation: In the EU, nanomaterials must be labelled on product ingredient lists. Research is ongoing into their safety.
Nano-sun cream debate

Benefit: transparent, effective UV protection, less white residue encourages use → fewer skin cancers. Risk: nanoparticles may penetrate skin and enter blood. Current evidence suggests they don't penetrate healthy skin, but damaged skin may allow entry. More research needed.

Silver nanoparticles in clothing

Benefit: silver nanoparticles kill bacteria → anti-odour, anti-bacterial clothing. Risk: when washed, silver nanoparticles may enter waterways and harm aquatic organisms. Research into washing filters is ongoing.

Evaluation framework:
1. What is the benefit?
2. What are the risks?
3. Who is affected?
4. What safeguards exist?
5. Is the benefit worth the risk?

Common Misconception

Nanoparticles are dangerous and should be banned.

Like many technologies, nanoparticles have both benefits and risks. The key is thorough testing, regulation, and ongoing research. Many nanoparticle products (like sun cream) are considered safe based on current evidence, but research continues.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a pro/con table template. Fill in together as a class for one example, then children complete another independently.
Stretch:
Write a balanced argument: 'Should all products containing nanoparticles be required to carry a warning label?'

Plenary (5 minutes)

Debate: 'Nanotechnology will do more good than harm for society.' Children vote and justify their position.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Evaluates nanoparticle use with balanced consideration of benefits and risks, using scientific evidence.
  • Getting there: Lists some benefits and risks but cannot evaluate or weigh them against each other.
  • Not yet: Cannot identify benefits or risks. Reteach with guided examples.

Homework Suggestions

  • Research three real-world applications of nanoparticles and write a report
  • Design an experiment to test the surface area to volume ratio of cubes
  • Debate: write a balanced argument for or against nanoparticle use in food
