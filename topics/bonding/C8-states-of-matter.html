C8: States of Matter

Foundation All Boards

Describe the three states of matter and the changes between them in terms of particle arrangement, motion and energy.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the arrangement, motion and spacing of particles in solids, liquids and gases
  • Explain evaporation and condensation and their applications in everyday life

Prerequisites

  • Atoms and Elements (C1) — states of matter at particle level

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Bags of rice and water for particle models
  • Syringe for compressing gas
  • Bunsen burner for heating demonstration
  • Beakers and ice for condensation demo

Lesson 1: Particle Model and States

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

State Detective

Show three sealed bags: one with rice packed tight (solid), one with rice and water sloshing (liquid), one with air inside (gas). What are the similarities and differences?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
All matter is made of particles (atoms, molecules or ions) that are in constant random motion.

Solid:
• Particles packed tightly in a regular arrangement
• Strong forces of attraction between particles
• Particles vibrate in fixed positions — cannot move around
• Fixed shape and fixed volume
• Cannot be compressed

Liquid:
• Particles close together in a random arrangement
• Weaker forces of attraction than solids
• Particles can move around each other (slide past)
• Takes the shape of its container — no fixed shape
• Fixed volume — cannot be compressed

Gas:
• Particles far apart in a random arrangement
• Negligible forces of attraction between particles
• Particles move rapidly in all directions, filling the container
• No fixed shape, no fixed volume
• Can be compressed

Changes of state:
• Melting: solid → liquid (energy increases, particles overcome forces and start to slide)
• Freezing: liquid → solid (energy decreases, particles lose movement and lock into position)
• Boiling/evaporation: liquid → gas (energy increases, particles overcome all forces and escape)
• Condensing: gas → liquid (energy decreases, particles slow and forces attract them together)
• Sublimation: solid → gas (e.g. dry ice, iodine)
• Deposition: gas → solid (e.g. frost forming)

Heating curves: Temperature stays constant during a change of state (energy used to overcome forces, not increase temperature).
Boiling water

At 100°C, water boils. The temperature stays at 100°C until ALL the water has turned to steam. The energy is being used to overcome the intermolecular forces between water molecules, not to raise the temperature.

Why evaporation happens below boiling point

Evaporation occurs at any temperature. The fastest-moving particles at the surface have enough energy to escape. This is why puddles dry up on a warm day even though it's not 100°C.

Melting point of water = 0°C
Boiling point of water = 100°C

During state change: temperature is CONSTANT
Energy input = overcoming intermolecular forces

Heating curve shape:
Temperature ↑ → flat (state change) → Temperature ↑ → flat (state change) → Temperature ↑

Common Misconception

When water boils, the water molecules break apart into hydrogen and oxygen.

Boiling is a physical change, not chemical. Water molecules (H₂O) stay intact — they just move apart from each other. Breaking water into hydrogen and oxygen requires electrolysis (a chemical reaction).

Differentiation

Support:
Use animations showing particle arrangements in each state. Colour-code: solid (red, vibrating), liquid (orange, sliding), gas (yellow, flying).
Stretch:
Research: what is the critical point? What happens to water above 374°C and 218 atm? (Supercritical water)

Plenary (5 minutes)

Draw particle diagrams for solid, liquid and gas. Label: arrangement, spacing, movement, forces. Then explain what happens during melting.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Draws accurate particle diagrams for all three states and explains changes of state in terms of particles.
  • Getting there: Can draw particle diagrams but struggles to explain changes of state using particle theory.
  • Not yet: Cannot draw particle diagrams. Reteach using physical models.

Lesson 2: Evaporation, Condensation and Applications

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Drying Clothes

Why do clothes dry faster on a warm, windy day than on a cold, still day? Think about what evaporation requires and what speeds it up.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Factors affecting rate of evaporation:
• Temperature: higher temp → more particles have enough energy to escape
• Surface area: larger surface → more particles at the surface can escape
• Wind/air flow: moving air carries away evaporated particles, allowing more to escape
• Humidity: less humid air → more room for evaporated particles → faster evaporation

Condensation is the reverse: gas → liquid. It happens when gas particles lose energy and come together.

Everyday applications:
• Refrigerators/freezers: refrigerant evaporates inside (absorbs heat from food) → compressor → condenses outside (releases heat)
• Distillation: evaporation then condensation to purify liquids
• Water cycle: evaporation from oceans → condensation into clouds → precipitation → collection

Sublimation in practice:
• Dry ice (solid CO₂) sublimes at room temperature → used for stage effects
• Iodine crystals sublime when heated → used in demonstrations
Why a puddle disappears

Water molecules at the surface gain enough kinetic energy from the surroundings to overcome intermolecular forces and escape as gas. Wind carries them away. Temperature is not necessarily 100°C — evaporation occurs below boiling point.

Refrigerator cycle

Liquid refrigerant evaporates inside the fridge (absorbs heat → cools food). The vapour is compressed and condensed outside the fridge (releases heat to the kitchen). The cycle repeats continuously.

Evaporation rate depends on:
↑ Temperature → faster
↑ Surface area → faster
↑ Air flow → faster
↓ Humidity → faster

Condensation: gas particles lose energy → attract together → liquid

Common Misconception

Evaporation and boiling are the same process at different temperatures.

Both involve liquid → gas, but they are different. Boiling happens at a specific temperature (100°C for water), throughout the liquid, with bubbles forming. Evaporation happens at any temperature, only at the surface, and is a slower process.

Differentiation

Support:
Use the analogy of a crowded room: people at the door (surface) leave more easily. More people near the door (larger surface area) → more leave. A fan blowing (wind) → helps people leave faster.
Stretch:
Research: how does a cooling tower at a power station use evaporation? Why is water sprayed into the air?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Explain why food dries faster in a dehydrator than on an open plate. Use at least three factors from today's lesson.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains evaporation and condensation clearly and can apply knowledge to real-world situations.
  • Getting there: Understands evaporation but struggles to explain factors affecting rate.
  • Not yet: Cannot link evaporation to particle theory. Reteach with the puddle experiment.

Homework Suggestions

  • Draw particle diagrams for all 6 changes of state
  • Design an experiment to investigate factors affecting evaporation rate
  • Research: how does the water cycle work? Draw a labelled diagram.
