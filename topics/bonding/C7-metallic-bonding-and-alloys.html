C7: Metallic Bonding and Alloys

Foundation All Boards

Explain metallic bonding in terms of a sea of delocalised electrons, and describe how alloys are made and why they are harder than pure metals.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe metallic bonding in terms of a lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons
  • Explain what alloys are and why they are harder than pure metals

Prerequisites

  • Atoms and Elements (C1) — electron arrangements, metals vs non-metals

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Different metal samples or photos (iron, copper, aluminium, gold)
  • Coins of different sizes for alloy model
  • Samples of brass and steel if available

Lesson 1: Metallic Bonding

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Why Are Metals Shiny?

Show different metals: iron, copper, gold, aluminium. What properties do they all share? Why can metals conduct electricity but ionic compounds cannot (as solids)?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Metallic bonding:
Metals consist of a regular lattice (arrangement) of positive metal ions surrounded by a sea of delocalised electrons.

When metal atoms bond, their outer electrons become delocalised — they are no longer associated with any one atom. These free electrons move freely throughout the structure.

The bond is the electrostatic attraction between the positive metal ions and the negative delocalised electrons.

Properties explained:

1. High melting points (variable): Strong metallic bonds need lots of energy to break. Varies: sodium (98°C) to tungsten (3422°C).

2. Good conductors of electricity and heat: Delocalised electrons are free to move and carry charge (electricity) or kinetic energy (heat).

3. Malleable and ductile: Layers of metal ions can slide over each other without breaking the bond — the sea of electrons holds them together. Malleable = can be hammered into shape. Ductile = can be drawn into wires.

4. Sonorous (make a ringing sound): Layers vibrate when struck.

5. Lustrous (shiny): Free electrons reflect light at the surface.
Why copper conducts electricity

In copper metal, each Cu atom loses 2 electrons to become Cu²⁺. These 2 electrons per atom join the delocalised sea. When a voltage is applied, these free electrons flow, carrying electric current.

Why iron is strong but sodium is soft

Iron has more delocalised electrons per atom and smaller ions → stronger metallic bonds → hard. Sodium has only 1 delocalised electron per atom → weaker bonds → soft, can cut with a knife.

Metallic bond = electrostatic attraction between:
Positive metal ions (cations) + Sea of delocalised electrons

More delocalised electrons → stronger bond → higher melting point
Smaller ionic radius → stronger bond → higher melting point

e.g. Na: 1 delocalised e⁻, low m.p. (98°C)
Mg: 2 delocalised e⁻, higher m.p. (650°C)
Al: 3 delocalised e⁻, even higher m.p. (660°C)

Common Misconception

Metals are solid because the atoms are stuck together by covalent bonds.

Metals are held together by metallic bonds — the attraction between positive ions and delocalised electrons. This is different from covalent bonds (shared pairs) or ionic bonds (transfer of electrons).

Differentiation

Support:
Use the 'plum pudding' analogy: positive ions are plums embedded in a 'pudding' of delocalised electrons. Or use ball pit analogy: balls (ions) floating in a sea of marbles (electrons).
Stretch:
Research: why do transition metals like iron have higher melting points than Group 1 metals? How does the number of d-electrons affect metallic bonding?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Explain in your own words why metals conduct electricity, are malleable, and have high melting points. Use the words 'delocalised electrons' and 'electrostatic attraction'.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains metallic bonding clearly and links it to all key properties of metals.
  • Getting there: Understands the basic idea of delocalised electrons but cannot fully explain all properties.
  • Not yet: Cannot describe metallic bonding. Reteach using the ball pit model and physical demonstrations.

Lesson 2: Alloys and Their Uses

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Pure Isn't Always Best

Gold is too soft for jewellery on its own. Pure iron is too soft for tools. How do we make metals harder? Hint: think about what happens when you put different sized balls in a box.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Alloys are mixtures of a metal with one or more other elements (usually other metals or carbon).

In a pure metal, all atoms are the same size → layers slide easily → soft.

In an alloy, different sized atoms disrupt the regular layer structure → layers cannot slide easily → harder.

Important alloys:
• Steel = iron + carbon (up to 2.5%). Harder than pure iron. Used in buildings, bridges, cars, tools.
• Stainless steel = iron + carbon + chromium (+ nickel). Resists corrosion. Used in cutlery, surgical instruments, sinks.
• Brass = copper + zinc. Harder than copper, attractive golden colour. Used in musical instruments, door handles.
• Bronze = copper + tin. Hard, resists corrosion. Used in statues, bells, coins.
• Solder = tin + lead (or tin + silver in lead-free solder). Low melting point. Used to join electrical wires.
• Duralumin = aluminium + copper + magnesium. Lightweight and strong. Used in aircraft.

Pure metals are rarely used because they are usually too soft, too reactive, or too expensive.
Steel vs pure iron

Pure iron is soft and corrodes easily. Adding ~1% carbon makes steel — much harder because carbon atoms (smaller) sit between iron atoms, disrupting the layers. Adding chromium and nickel makes stainless steel — resists rust.

Brass door handles

Pure copper is soft and tarnishes. Brass (copper + zinc) is harder, more durable, and has an attractive gold-like colour. Perfect for door handles that are used frequently.

Alloy = metal + other element(s)

Why alloys are harder:
Pure metal: same-sized atoms → regular layers → slide easily
Alloy: different-sized atoms → disrupted layers → cannot slide

Steel = Fe + C (up to 2.5%)
Stainless steel = Fe + C + Cr + Ni
Brass = Cu + Zn
Bronze = Cu + Sn

Common Misconception

Steel is an element because it is listed on some periodic tables in industrial contexts.

Steel is an alloy — a mixture of iron and carbon (and sometimes other elements). It is not an element and does not appear on the periodic table. Only pure iron (Fe) is an element.

Differentiation

Support:
Use a physical model: arrange identical coins in layers (pure metal) — they slide easily. Now intersperse with different-sized coins (alloy) — layers won't slide.
Stretch:
Research: what is a shape-memory alloy (e.g. nitinol)? How does it 'remember' its original shape?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Match each alloy to its components and a use: steel, brass, bronze, solder, duralumin. Explain why each use suits that alloy's properties.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains why alloys are harder than pure metals and can name at least 5 alloys with their components and uses.
  • Getting there: Knows some alloys but cannot always explain why they are harder.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain why alloys are harder. Reteach using the coin-layer model.

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a table of 8 alloys: name, components, properties, uses
  • Research: how is recycling of metals more energy-efficient than extracting from ores?
  • Visit: find examples of alloys in your home — list them with their compositions
