C6: Covalent Bonding

Foundation All Boards

Understand how covalent bonds form by sharing electrons, draw dot-and-cross diagrams for simple molecules, and describe giant covalent structures.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Explain how covalent bonds form by sharing pairs of electrons and draw dot-and-cross diagrams
  • Describe the structures and properties of diamond, graphite, silicon dioxide and simple polymers
  • Explain the differences between giant covalent structures and simple molecular structures

Prerequisites

  • Atoms and Elements (C1) — electron arrangements, outer electrons

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Ball-and-stick molecular model kits
  • Whiteboard for dot-and-cross diagrams
  • Samples of diamond (cubic zirconia) and graphite pencil for comparison

Lesson 1: Simple Covalent Molecules

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Sharing is Caring

Two children each have 1 sweet. They both want 2 sweets. If they put their sweets together and share, they both have 2. This models covalent bonding — sharing, not transferring.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Covalent bonds form when non-metal atoms share pairs of electrons to achieve full outer shells.

Each shared pair of electrons counts as one covalent bond.

Simple molecules:

Hydrogen (H₂): Each H has 1 electron. They share 1 pair → both have 2 (full outer shell). H–H

Water (H₂O): O has 6 outer electrons, needs 2 more. Each H has 1 electron. O shares 1 pair with each H → O has 8, each H has 2. Two single bonds, two lone pairs on O.

Carbon dioxide (CO₂): C has 4 outer electrons, needs 4 more. O has 6, needs 2 more. C shares 2 pairs with each O → double bonds. O=C=O

Methane (CH₄): C has 4 outer electrons. Each H has 1. C shares 1 pair with each of 4 H atoms → 4 single bonds. Tetrahedral shape.

Dot-and-cross rules:
• Draw the central atom's electrons as dots, outer atoms' as crosses
• Show shared pairs between atoms
• Show lone pairs (non-bonding pairs) on the central atom
Water (H₂O)

O: 2,6 → needs 2 more. Each H: 1 → needs 1 more.
O shares one pair with each H.
O now has 2+2+4 = 8 (full outer shell). Each H has 2 (full shell).
Two single covalent bonds. Two lone pairs on O.

Carbon dioxide (CO₂)

C: 2,4 → needs 4 more. Each O: 2,6 → needs 2 more.
C shares 2 pairs (double bond) with each O.
C has 8 total. Each O has 8 total.
Two double bonds. No lone pairs on C.

Covalent bond = shared pair of electrons

H₂ : H–H (1 bond)
H₂O : H–O–H (2 bonds, 2 lone pairs on O)
CO₂ : O=C=O (2 double bonds)
CH₄ : 4 single bonds from C to H
N₂ : N≡N (triple bond)

Common Misconception

Covalent bonds are weak because the atoms are just sharing.

Covalent bonds are strong — it takes a lot of energy to break them (e.g. 413 kJ/mol to break one C–H bond). What's weak are the intermolecular forces between molecules, not the covalent bonds within them.

Differentiation

Support:
Use a physical model: two children hold hands (shared pair). Four children in a ring (4 bonds in CH₄). Show lone pairs as non-held hands.
Stretch:
Research: why does nitrogen (N₂) have a triple bond? What does this mean for its reactivity? Why is N₂ used in food packaging?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Draw dot-and-cross diagrams for NH₃, HCl, and H₂S. Count bonds and lone pairs.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Draws accurate dot-and-cross diagrams for simple covalent molecules and identifies single/double/triple bonds.
  • Getting there: Can draw simple molecules (H₂, H₂O) but struggles with double bonds (CO₂).
  • Not yet: Cannot draw covalent dot-and-cross diagrams. Reteach using the hand-holding analogy with physical models.

Lesson 2: Giant Covalent Structures

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Stronger Than Steel?

Diamond is the hardest natural substance known. Graphite (also pure carbon) is soft enough to write with. How can two forms of the same element be so different?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Not all covalent substances exist as small molecules. Some form giant covalent structures — enormous lattices of atoms joined by covalent bonds throughout.

Diamond:
• Each carbon forms 4 covalent bonds (tetrahedral arrangement)
• Giant 3D lattice — extremely hard
• Very high melting point (~3550°C) — many strong bonds to break
• Does NOT conduct electricity — no free electrons (all 4 are used in bonding)
• Used in cutting tools, drill bits, jewellery

Graphite:
• Each carbon forms 3 covalent bonds (trigonal planar — hexagonal layers)
• Layers held by weak intermolecular forces → slides easily → soft, used as a lubricant and in pencils
• High melting point (sublimes ~3600°C)
• DOES conduct electricity — each carbon has 1 delocalised electron not used in bonding
• Used in electrodes, lubricant, pencils

Silicon dioxide (SiO₂) — sand/quartz:
• Each Si bonded to 4 O atoms, each O bonded to 2 Si atoms
• Giant covalent structure → very high melting point
• Used in glass-making, construction
Why diamond doesn't conduct but graphite does

Diamond: all 4 outer electrons of each carbon are used in covalent bonds → no free electrons → no conductivity.
Graphite: only 3 outer electrons used → 1 delocalised electron per carbon → can carry electric current.

Why graphite is soft

Graphite layers are held together by weak intermolecular forces (not covalent bonds). These forces are easy to overcome → layers slide over each other → soft, writes on paper.

Diamond: C — 4 bonds per atom, tetrahedral, 3D giant structure
Graphite: C — 3 bonds per atom, hexagonal layers, 1 delocalised electron per atom

SiO₂: Si bonded to 4 O, O bonded to 2 Si

Common Misconception

Diamond and graphite have different properties because they are different elements.

They are both pure carbon — allotropes of carbon. The difference is in how the carbon atoms are bonded and arranged. Diamond: 4 bonds, 3D lattice. Graphite: 3 bonds, layered hexagonal sheets.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide diagrams of diamond and graphite structures side by side. Highlight the 4th bond in diamond and the free electron in graphite with different colours.
Stretch:
Research graphene and carbon nanotubes — what properties do they have? Why are they called 'wonder materials'?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Complete a comparison table: diamond vs graphite — number of bonds, shape, conductivity, hardness, uses.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains structure-property relationships for diamond and graphite clearly.
  • Getting there: Knows the structures but cannot always link them to properties.
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish diamond and graphite structures. Reteach with physical models: ball-and-stick kits.

Lesson 3: Polymers and Fullerenes

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Plastic Everywhere

List all the plastic items in the room. How many can you find? Polymers are everywhere — but what exactly are they?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Polymers are very large molecules made from many small repeating units called monomers.

How polymers form:
Many monomer molecules join together by covalent bonds in a process called polymerisation.

Key polymers to know:

Poly(ethene) — polythene:
Monomer: ethene (CH₂=CH₂)
The double bond opens up and forms single bonds to the next monomer.
Repeating unit: –CH₂–CH₂–
Used for plastic bags, bottles, food wrap.

Poly(chloroethene) — PVC:
Monomer: chloroethene (CH₂=CHCl)
Repeating unit: –CH₂–CHCl–
Used for pipes, window frames, electrical insulation.

Poly(phenylethene) — polystyrene:
Monomer: phenylethene (styrene, CH₂=CHC₆H₅)
Used for packaging, disposable cups, insulation.

Fullerenes:
• C₆₀ (buckminsterfullerene): 60 carbon atoms arranged in a hollow sphere of pentagons and hexagons (like a football)
• Carbon nanotubes: cylinders of carbon atoms with extraordinary strength and electrical properties
• Graphene: single layer of carbon atoms in a hexagonal arrangement — stronger than steel, excellent conductor

Uses of fullerenes:
• Drug delivery (hollow cage can carry medicine)
• Catalysts (large surface area)
• Reinforcing composite materials
Making poly(ethene)

Ethene: CH₂=CH₂. The C=C double bond opens up. Each carbon forms single bonds to the next monomer. The result is a long chain: –CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂– (repeating for thousands of units).

C₆₀ fullerene

60 carbon atoms in a cage of 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons. Named after Buckminster Fuller (architect of geodesic domes). Each carbon forms 3 covalent bonds. The remaining electron is delocalised → conducts electricity.

Polymerisation: n(CH₂=CH₂) → (–CH₂–CH₂–)ₙ

Monomer → Polymer
Ethene → Poly(ethene)
Chloroethene → Poly(chloroethene) [PVC]
Tetrafluoroethene → PTFE (Teflon)

Fullerenes: C₆₀, nanotubes, graphene

Common Misconception

Polymers are all the same — just 'plastic'.

Different monomers give polymers with very different properties. Poly(ethene) is flexible (plastic bags), PVC is rigid (pipes), PTFE is non-stick (Teflon). The atoms in the repeating unit determine the properties.

Differentiation

Support:
Use the paper chain analogy: each paper clip = monomer, the chain = polymer. Break one link = the polymer breaks down.
Stretch:
Research: what is biodegradable plastic? How does it differ from conventional polymers? What are the environmental implications?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Draw the repeating unit for poly(chloroethene). What atoms are in each unit? How does this differ from poly(ethene)?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains polymerisation, draws repeating units, and describes uses of key polymers and fullerenes.
  • Getting there: Can describe polymerisation but makes errors drawing repeating units.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain how monomers form polymers. Reteach with the paper chain model.

Homework Suggestions

  • Draw dot-and-cross diagrams for 8 molecules: H₂, O₂, N₂, H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, NH₃, HCl
  • Compare diamond and graphite in a detailed table (structure, properties, uses)
  • Research: what are fullerene molecules (C₆₀) and how are they used?
