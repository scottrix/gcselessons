C5: Ionic Bonding

Foundation All Boards

Understand how ionic bonds form between metals and non-metals through electron transfer, and describe the properties of ionic compounds.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Explain how ions are formed by the gain or loss of electrons, and describe ionic bonding
  • Explain the properties of ionic compounds in terms of their structure and bonding
  • Write formulae for ionic compounds using ion charges, and name compounds from their formulae

Prerequisites

  • Atoms and Elements (C1) — electron arrangements, groups and outer electrons

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Whiteboard and pens for dot-and-cross diagrams
  • Coloured counters/pennies for electrons
  • Magnet set for lattice demonstration

Lesson 1: How Ionic Bonds Form

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Giant Ball Game

Give one child 8 pennies (electrons) and another child an empty cup. The pennies represent outer electrons. What happens if the child with 8 gives away some to fill the other child's cup? This models electron transfer.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Ions are atoms that have gained or lost electrons, giving them a charge.

• A metal atom loses electrons to form a positive ion (cation).
• A non-metal atom gains electrons to form a negative ion (anion).

Why do atoms form ions?
Atoms want a full outer shell (like a noble gas). Metals with 1-3 outer electrons tend to lose them. Non-metals with 5-7 outer electrons tend to gain electrons to fill their outer shell.

Ionic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. It forms a giant ionic lattice (regular repeating 3D structure).

Dot and cross diagrams:
Show one element's electrons as dots, the other's as crosses. Draw the original atoms, then show electron transfer, then draw the resulting ions with square brackets and charges.
Sodium chloride (NaCl)

Na: 2,8,1 → loses 1 electron → Na⁺: 2,8 (full outer shell like neon)
Cl: 2,8,7 → gains 1 electron → Cl⁻: 2,8,8 (full outer shell like argon)
One Na⁺ pairs with one Cl⁻ → 1:1 ratio → NaCl

Magnesium oxide (MgO)

Mg: 2,8,2 → loses 2 electrons → Mg²⁺: 2,8
O: 2,6 → gains 2 electrons → O²⁻: 2,8
One Mg²⁺ pairs with one O²⁻ → 1:1 ratio → MgO

Electron transfer:
Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Ionic bond = electrostatic attraction between cation (+) and anion (−)

Formula unit: smallest ratio of ions in the lattice
e.g. NaCl (1:1), MgO (1:1), CaF₂ (1:2)

Common Misconception

Ionic bonds are a type of chemical bond where atoms stick together like glue.

Ionic bonds are not physical links. They are electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions arranged in a giant lattice structure. There are no individual 'ionic molecules' — the formula NaCl represents a ratio, not a molecule.

Differentiation

Support:
Use physical counters (pennies or counters) for electrons. Children physically move counters from metal to non-metal atom diagram. Use different coloured counters for each element.
Stretch:
Research: why does sodium chloride have a melting point of 801°C? How does the lattice structure explain its brittleness?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Draw dot-and-cross diagrams for LiBr, K₂O, and MgCl₂. Check electron arrangements of all ions.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Draws accurate dot-and-cross diagrams and explains why ionic compounds have high melting points.
  • Getting there: Can do simple cases (1:1 ratio) but struggles with 1:2 ratios like MgCl₂.
  • Not yet: Cannot draw dot-and-cross diagrams. Reteach with physical electron counters before attempting diagrams.

Lesson 2: Properties of Ionic Compounds

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Crystal Cube

Show a large crystal of salt. Ask: why is it a cube? Why is it hard? Why does it dissolve in water but not in oil? All these relate to ionic bonding.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Ionic compounds form a giant ionic lattice — a regular, repeating 3D arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions.

Properties explained by the giant lattice:

1. High melting and boiling points:
Strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions require lots of energy to overcome. NaCl melts at 801°C.

2. Brittle:
If you hit an ionic crystal, layers shift. Like charges end up next to each other → repulsion → crystal shatters.

3. Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water (but NOT as solids):
In the solid state, ions are fixed in position and cannot move. When molten or dissolved, ions are free to move and carry electric current.

4. Dissolve in water (but not in organic solvents):
Water is polar — the δ+ and δ− ends of water molecules attract the ions, pulling them away from the lattice. This is called hydration.
Why does salt dissolve in water?

Water molecules are polar: O is δ−, H is δ+. δ− oxygens surround Na⁺, δ+ hydrogens surround Cl⁻. Ions are pulled away from the lattice and surrounded by water molecules (hydrated ions).

Why is salt brittle?

Hit the crystal hard → layers of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ shift → Na⁺ ends up next to Na⁺ → strong repulsion → crystal shatters. Metals don't do this because the 'sea of delocalised electrons' holds layers together.

Giant ionic lattice: alternating + and − ions in 3D

Strong electrostatic forces = high melting point
Fixed ions in solid = no conductivity
Free ions when molten/dissolved = conducts electricity
Polar water molecules = hydration = dissolves

Common Misconception

Ionic compounds conduct electricity as solids because they contain charged particles.

The ions are charged, but in the solid state they are fixed in position within the lattice and cannot move. Only when molten or dissolved do the ions become free to move and carry current.

Differentiation

Support:
Use a physical model: arrange magnets in alternating poles on a board. Show how they are stuck (solid) vs sliding apart when heated (molten).
Stretch:
Research: why does magnesium oxide have a much higher melting point (2852°C) than sodium chloride (801°C)? Hint: think about ion charge and size.

Plenary (5 minutes)

True or false: ionic compounds conduct electricity as solids / dissolve in oil / have high melting points / are brittle. Justify each answer.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains all properties of ionic compounds in terms of the giant lattice structure and electrostatic forces.
  • Getting there: Knows the properties but cannot always explain them in terms of structure.
  • Not yet: Cannot link structure to properties. Reteach using the magnet model and simple analogies.

Lesson 3: Writing Formulae and Naming Ionic Compounds

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Ion Charge Game

Give cards with ion charges: Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Cl⁻, O²⁻. Can the children pair them up to make neutral compounds? How many of each ion do they need?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
To write the formula of an ionic compound:
1. Write the symbols and charges of the ions involved.
2. Balance the charges so the overall compound is neutral (zero charge).
3. Use the lowest whole number ratio.

Common ions to know:
• Group 1: +1 (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺)
• Group 2: +2 (Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺)
• Al: +3 (Al³⁺)
• Transition metals: variable (Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺, Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺)
• Group 7: −1 (F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻)
• Group 6: −2 (O²⁻, S²⁻)
• Group 5: −3 (N³⁻)
• Hydroxide: OH⁻
• Carbonate: CO₃²⁻
• Nitrate: NO₃⁻
• Sulfate: SO₄²⁻
• Ammonium: NH₄⁺

Naming: Name the metal first, then the non-metal with "-ide" ending.
NaCl = sodium chloride, MgO = magnesium oxide, CaCl₂ = calcium chloride
Aluminium oxide

Al³⁺ and O²⁻. Charges: +3 and −2. LCM of 3 and 2 is 6. Need 2 Al³⁺ (= +6) and 3 O²⁻ (= −6). Formula: Al₂O₃.

Iron(III) chloride

Fe³⁺ and Cl⁻. Charges: +3 and −1. Need 1 Fe³⁺ and 3 Cl⁻. Formula: FeCl₃. The (III) tells us iron's charge — iron can also form Fe²⁺ (iron(II) chloride = FeCl₂).

Step 1: Write ions with charges: Al³⁺, O²⁻
Step 2: Balance charges: 2×(+3) + 3×(−2) = 0
Step 3: Write formula: Al₂O₃

Common polyatomic ions:
OH⁻ hydroxide, CO₃²⁻ carbonate, NO₃⁻ nitrate, SO₄²⁻ sulfate, NH₄⁺ ammonium

Common Misconception

You can write any ratio of ions — e.g. NaCl₂ is fine because chlorine sometimes has different charges.

Ionic compounds must be neutral. The charges must balance exactly. NaCl₂ would have an overall negative charge — it cannot exist. You must use the correct charge for each ion and balance them.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a reference card of common ion charges. Use a charge balance table: columns for cation charge, anion charge, and multiplier needed.
Stretch:
Research: what is the formula of ammonium sulfate? Aluminium hydroxide? Calcium nitrate? These use polyatomic ions.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Write formulae for: potassium bromide, calcium oxide, aluminium sulfide, iron(II) oxide, zinc chloride.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Writes correct formulae for any ionic compound, including those with polyatomic ions and variable-charge metals.
  • Getting there: Can do simple 1:1 compounds but struggles with balancing charges like Al₂O₃.
  • Not yet: Cannot balance charges. Reteach with physical counters: use + tokens and − tokens to find the right combination.

Homework Suggestions

  • Draw dot-and-cross diagrams for 6 ionic compounds
  • Practise writing formulae from ion charges — 20 questions
  • Research: why is magnesium oxide used as a lining in furnaces?
