Lesson 2: Core Concepts: stage types and performance spaces

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on stage types and performance spaces. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Proscenium arch: audience on one side, framed stage — creates a 'fourth wall', common in traditional theatres.
Key Fact: Thrust stage: audience on three sides — intimate, breaks fourth wall, used in Shakespeare's Globe.
Key Fact: In-the-round: audience on all sides — most intimate, actors must keep moving to avoid backs to audience.
Key Fact: Traverse: audience on two opposite sides — creates a catwalk/battle effect, highly dynamic.
Key Fact: Promenade: audience moves through the space — immersive, site-specific, breaks traditional boundaries.
Drama Exam Tips: When writing about stage types, always state the name, describe the audience-performer relationship, and explain WHY a director might choose it. Never just list features — always link to dramatic effect.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Name three stage types and explain how each affects the audience-performer relationship.

Answer: Three named types with clear explanation of audience positioning and the performer relationship — e.g. proscenium creates distance/thrust creates intimacy/in-the-round creates total immersion.

Lesson 3: Application: stage types and performance spaces

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Drama Exam Tips. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Name three stage types and explain how each affects the audience-performer relationship.

Answer: Three named types with clear explanation of audience positioning and the performer relationship — e.g. proscenium creates distance/thrust creates intimacy/in-the-round creates total immersion.

Q2: A director wants to create an intimate, immersive experience. Which stage type would you recommend and why?

Answer: A justified recommendation (e.g. promenade or in-the-round) with specific reasons about audience proximity, sight lines and the immersive qualities the stage type enables.

Q3: Compare the advantages and disadvantages of performing in-the-round versus a proscenium arch stage.

Answer: A balanced comparison: in-the-round gives intimacy and 360-degree audience engagement but requires constant movement management; proscenium allows controlled focus and set design but creates distance.

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response A director is staging a play about imprisonment. Which stage type would best communicate this theme to the audience? Justify your choice with detailed reference to how the space would be used. <div class="

A grade 9 response will: name a specific stage type; explain how the space creates feelings of confinement or surveillance; describe how the audience's positioning reinforces the theme; use precise theatrical vocabulary (sight lines, fourth wall, proxemics); consider practical staging choices (entrances, exits, levels). Mark scheme: GCSE Drama has three components: Understanding Drama (40% written exam), Devising Drama (30% NEA — devised performance +...

Exam Tips: Always name the stage type precisely — never just say 'a normal stage' | Link stage choice to the EFFECT on the audience | Consider practical implications: sight lines, exits, set placement | Use diagrams in your notes to remember configurations
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often make mistakes here. Wrong: It doesn't matter what stage type you use — the audience can always see the action. Correct: Stage type fundamentally changes the audience-performer relationship and what the audience can see. In-the-round requires constant actor repositioning; proscenium allows controlled focus. Directors choose stage types deliberately to create specific effects.
