Lesson 2: Core Concepts: ethical, legal & environmental impacts

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on ethical, legal & environmental impacts. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Concept: Technology has wide-ranging impacts beyond just technical considerations. As computing professionals and informed citizens, we need to understand the ethical, legal, environmental, and cultural effects of technology on individuals and society.
The Debate: Should governments and organisations be able to collect and monitor personal data to improve security? This creates a conflict between individual privacy and collective security.
The Issue: As technology advances, many jobs are being automated (performed by machines or software instead of people). This raises ethical questions about unemployment, retraining, and economic inequality.
The Issue: AI systems can reflect and amplify human biases. If training data contains biases, the AI will produce biased results, potentially discriminating against certain groups.
Purpose: The Data Protection Act (DPA) 2018 and the UK GDPR protect individuals' personal data. They give people rights over their own data and impose obligations on organisations that process it.
Purpose: The Computer Misuse Act makes certain activities involving computers illegal. It was created to address hacking and unauthorised access to computer systems.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Explain the ethical concerns around AI bias. Give an example.

Answer: AI systems can reflect and amplify biases present in their training data. If historical data contains biases (e.g. favouring certain demographics in hiring), the AI will produce biased results. Example: a facial recognition system trained mostly on lighter-skinned faces was found to be significantly less accurate at identifying darker-skinned faces, leading to potential misidentification.

Lesson 3: Application: ethical, legal & environmental impacts

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Concept, The Debate, The Issue. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain the ethical concerns around AI bias. Give an example.

Answer: AI systems can reflect and amplify biases present in their training data. If historical data contains biases (e.g. favouring certain demographics in hiring), the AI will produce biased results. Example: a facial recognition system trained mostly on lighter-skinned faces was found to be significantly less accurate at identifying darker-skinned faces, leading to potential misidentification.

Q2: What is the purpose of the Data Protection Act 2018? State three rights it gives to individuals.

Answer: The DPA 2018 protects individuals' personal data by setting rules for how organisations collect, store, and process it. Three rights: right to access their personal data, right to have inaccurate data corrected, right to have data deleted (right to be forgotten).

Q3: Describe the three main offences under the Computer Misuse Act 1990.

Answer: Section 1: Unauthorised access to a computer system (hacking). Section 2: Unauthorised access with intent to commit a further crime (e.g. hacking to steal money). Section 3: Unauthorised modification of computer material (e.g. deploying malware that changes or deletes data).

Q4: What is e-waste and why is it an environmental concern?

Answer: E-waste is discarded electronic equipment (computers, phones, TVs). It is an environmental concern because it contains toxic materials (lead, mercury, cadmium) that can leach into soil and water if not properly recycled. Much e-waste is shipped to developing countries where informal recycling causes health problems and environmental contamination.

Q5: What is the digital divide? Explain how it can affect people's opportunities.

Answer: The digital divide is the gap between people who have access to technology and the internet and those who do not. It affects opportunities because those without access cannot use online education, banking, job searching, or government services. During COVID-19 lockdowns, students without home internet fell behind. The divide exists between countries, between urban and rural areas, and between socioeconomic groups.

Exam Tips: Know the three key pieces of legislation: DPA 2018, Computer Misuse Act 1990, CDPA 1988 | Be specific about what each law does and the offences/rights it covers | Creative Commons is NOT the same as "no copyright" - it is a type of licence | For ethical questions, present balanced arguments (e.g. privacy vs security) | Environmental: mention e-waste AND energy consumption of data centres | Cultural: the digital divide affects education, employment, and social inclusion | Always name specific legislation rather than saying "the law says"
Common Errors: ✗ Confusing the Data Protection Act and the Computer Misuse Act ✓ The DPA (now UK GDPR) protects personal data about individuals. The Computer Misuse Act criminalises unauthorised access to computer systems (hacking). They protect different things. ✗ Thinking copyright only applies to music and films ✓ Copyright applies to any original creative work: software, code, databases, images, text, designs. Copying software without a licence is a copyright violation. ✗ Believing technology always has a negative environmental impact ✓ Technology has both negative impacts (e-waste, energy consumption of data centres) and positive impacts (remote working reduces travel, smart grids reduce energy waste,
