C17: Electrolysis

Foundation All Boards

Describe the process of electrolysis, what happens at the electrodes, and the industrial uses of electrolysis.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe what happens during electrolysis and define the key terms: electrolyte, electrode, anode, cathode
  • Write ionic half-equations for reactions at the cathode and anode
  • Describe the industrial uses of electrolysis including purification of copper and electroplating

Prerequisites

  • Ionic Bonding (C5) — ions, electron transfer
  • Reactivity of Metals (C15) — extraction methods

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Safe electrolysis demonstration kit (if available): battery, wires, graphite pencils, beaker, salt water
  • Video: electrolysis of copper sulfate solution

Lesson 1: What Is Electrolysis?

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Electricity Through Liquids

Can electricity pass through water? Through salt water? Through molten salt? Set up a simple circuit with a beaker as the gap. Which liquids allow the circuit to complete?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Electrolysis is the decomposition of an ionic compound (when molten or dissolved in water) by passing an electric current through it.

Key terms:
• Electrolyte: a liquid that conducts electricity because it contains free-moving ions (molten or dissolved ionic compound)
• Electrodes: conductors (usually graphite or metal) that dip into the electrolyte
• Anode: positive electrode (connected to + terminal of power supply)
• Cathode: negative electrode (connected to − terminal of power supply)

What happens at each electrode:
• Cathode (−): positive ions (cations) are attracted here. They gain electrons (reduction).
  e.g. Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (copper metal deposits on the cathode)
• Anode (+): negative ions (anions) are attracted here. They lose electrons (oxidation).
  e.g. 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ (chlorine gas produced at the anode)

Electrolysis of molten ionic compounds:
NaCl (molten) → Na (at cathode) + Cl₂ (at anode)
Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na (at cathode)
2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ (at anode)

Electrolysis of aqueous solutions (dissolved in water):
The products depend on the reactivity of the ions and the concentration of the solution.
At the cathode: if the metal is more reactive than hydrogen → H₂ produced. If less reactive → metal produced.
At the anode: if halide ions present → halogen produced. Otherwise → O₂ produced.
Electrolysis of brine (NaCl solution)

NaCl dissolved in water. At cathode: Na⁺ ions are attracted but sodium is more reactive than hydrogen → hydrogen gas produced: 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻
At anode: Cl⁻ ions are attracted → chlorine gas produced: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
Solution left behind contains NaOH (sodium hydroxide) — this is the chlor-alkali industry.

Electrolysis of copper sulfate solution

At cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (brown copper deposits)
At anode: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ (oxygen gas produced)
Solution turns blue → colourless as Cu²⁺ ions are removed and H⁺ ions remain.

Electrolysis: electrical energy → chemical change

Cathode (−): cations attracted → reduction (gain electrons)
Anode (+): anions attracted → oxidation (lose electrons)

Mnemonic: PANIC — Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode

At cathode: metal ions → metal (if less reactive than H) or H₂ (if more reactive)
At anode: halide ions → halogen, or OH⁻/H₂O → O₂

Common Misconception

Electricity is used up in electrolysis — that's why the bulb goes out.

Electricity is not used up — electrical energy is converted into chemical energy. The current continues to flow as long as the electrolyte contains ions. When all the ions are used up, the current stops.

Differentiation

Support:
Draw a clear diagram of the electrolysis setup with arrows showing ion movement. Use colour: red for positive ions moving to cathode, blue for negative ions moving to anode.
Stretch:
Research: what is electroplating? How is it used to coat cheap metals with expensive ones (e.g. silver-plating cutlery)?

Plenary (5 minutes)

For each electrolysis setup, predict the products: molten PbBr₂, aqueous CuCl₂, molten Al₂O₃.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains electrolysis clearly, identifies products at each electrode, and writes half-equations.
  • Getting there: Knows cathode = negative, anode = positive, but struggles with half-equations.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain what electrolysis does. Reteach with the PANIC mnemonic and simple examples.

Lesson 2: Half-Equations and Predicting Products

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Half-Equations

Write the half-equation for: Cu²⁺ gaining 2 electrons, Cl⁻ losing 1 electron, Al³⁺ gaining 3 electrons.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Writing half-equations:
A half-equation shows either the reduction (gain of electrons) or oxidation (loss of electrons) at one electrode.

At the cathode (reduction):
Metal ion + electrons → metal
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag
Fe³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Fe
2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻ (when H₂ is produced instead of metal)

At the anode (oxidation):
2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻
4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ (when O₂ is produced instead of halogen)
2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻

How to write half-equations:
1. Write the ion and what it becomes
2. Balance the atoms
3. Balance the charge by adding electrons to the more positive side
4. Check: total charge is the same on both sides
Half-equation for zinc electrolysis

At cathode: Zn²⁺ → Zn
Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn
Check: charge on left = +2 + (−2) = 0. Charge on right = 0. Balanced!

Predicting products from reactivity

Electrolysis of aqueous ZnCl₂:
At cathode: Zn is more reactive than H → hydrogen produced: 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻
At anode: Cl⁻ present → chlorine produced: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
Products: hydrogen gas (cathode), chlorine gas (anode), zinc hydroxide left in solution.

Half-equation writing:
1. Write ion → product
2. Balance atoms
3. Balance charge by adding electrons

Reduction (cathode): ion + e⁻ → product
Oxidation (anode): ion → product + e⁻

Check: charge and atoms must balance on both sides

Common Misconception

At the cathode, anions are attracted because they are negative and the cathode is negative.

Opposite charges attract. The cathode is negative, so it attracts positive ions (cations). The anode is positive, so it attracts negative ions (anions). Remember: PANIC — Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a half-equation template with blanks: ___ + ___e⁻ → ___. Children fill in the ion, number of electrons, and product.
Stretch:
Write the overall equation for electrolysis of molten Al₂O₃ by combining the two half-equations. Include the role of cryolite.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Write half-equations for electrolysis of: molten MgCl₂, aqueous KBr, molten PbBr₂.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Writes balanced half-equations for any electrolysis scenario and predicts products correctly.
  • Getting there: Can write half-equations for simple cases but struggles with aqueous solutions.
  • Not yet: Cannot write half-equations. Reteach with the step-by-step method and simpler examples.

Lesson 3: Industrial Electrolysis

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Copper Purification

Why is purified copper needed for electrical wiring? What happens if impure copper is used? (Impurities increase resistance → wires overheat → fire risk.)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
1. Purification of copper by electrolysis:
• Impure copper (from smelting) is used as the anode
• Pure copper is used as the cathode
• Electrolyte: copper sulfate solution
• At anode: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ (impure copper dissolves)
• At cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (pure copper deposits)
• Impurities: more reactive impurities dissolve and stay in solution. Less reactive impurities (e.g. gold, silver) fall to the bottom as 'anode slime' — recovered later!

2. Electroplating:
Coating a cheaper metal with a more expensive or more attractive metal.
• Object to be plated = cathode (negative)
• Metal to plate with = anode (positive)
• Electrolyte: solution containing ions of the plating metal
• e.g. Silver-plating: silver anode, object as cathode, silver nitrate solution as electrolyte
• Uses: jewellery, cutlery, car bumpers, tap fittings

3. Chlor-alkali industry:
Electrolysis of brine (NaCl solution) produces three useful products:
• Chlorine (Cl₂) at anode — used in water treatment, PVC production, bleach
• Hydrogen (H₂) at cathode — used in margarine production, fuel cells
• Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) in solution — used in soap, paper, drain cleaners
Copper purification

Impure copper anode → dissolves → Cu²⁺ ions in solution → deposit as pure copper on cathode. After electrolysis, the anode has become thinner and the cathode thicker. The electrolyte concentration stays roughly constant. Gold and silver impurities fall as anode slime — this is how precious metals are recovered!

Silver-plating a spoon

Silver anode (+) connected to power supply. Spoon (cathode, −) dipped in silver nitrate solution. Current passes → silver dissolves from anode → silver ions migrate to cathode → deposit as shiny silver layer on spoon.

Copper purification:
Anode (impure Cu): Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻
Cathode (pure Cu): Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu

Electroplating:
Anode (plating metal): M → Mⁿ⁺ + ne⁻
Cathode (object): Mⁿ⁺ + ne⁻ → M

Chlor-alkali:
2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g) + Cl₂(g)

Common Misconception

In electroplating, the metal to be plated is the anode.

The object to be plated is the cathode (negative), because positive metal ions are attracted to it and deposit there. The metal being used for plating is the anode (positive), because it dissolves to provide the metal ions.

Differentiation

Support:
Use a table: column 1 = process, column 2 = cathode reaction, column 3 = anode reaction, column 4 = uses.
Stretch:
Research: why is electrolysis used to produce aluminium instead of carbon reduction? What are the economic and environmental factors?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Summarise: what are the three products of chlor-alkali? What is each used for?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Describes copper purification, electroplating and chlor-alkali processes with correct electrode reactions.
  • Getting there: Knows the processes but cannot write the electrode reactions accurately.
  • Not yet: Cannot describe industrial electrolysis. Reteach with the copper purification demonstration.

Homework Suggestions

  • Write half-equations for 10 different electrolysis scenarios
  • Draw and label a diagram of the copper purification cell
  • Research: how is electrolysis used to produce hydrogen for fuel cells?
