C16: Reactions of Acids

Foundation All Boards

Describe the reactions of acids with metals, bases, metal oxides and metal carbonates, and write balanced equations for each.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define acids and bases, and describe neutralisation reactions
  • Write equations for the reactions of acids with metals, metal oxides and metal carbonates
  • Explain the uses of bases in controlling pH and in everyday products

Prerequisites

  • Reactivity of Metals (C15) — reactivity series
  • Basic understanding of pH

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Litmus paper or universal indicator
  • Test tubes and racks
  • Marble chips (calcium carbonate) and hydrochloric acid for CO₂ demo
  • Safety goggles and gloves

Lesson 1: Acids, Bases and Neutralisation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Sour Taste Test

Lemons taste sour — they contain citric acid. Vinegar tastes sour — it contains ethanoic acid. What do all acids have in common? (Hint: think about the taste, the feel on skin, and what they turn litmus paper.)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Acids are substances with a pH less than 7. Common acids:
• Hydrochloric acid (HCl) — in stomach acid
• Sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) — in car batteries
• Ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH) — in vinegar
• Citric acid — in citrus fruits

Bases are substances with a pH greater than 7. They neutralise acids. Common bases:
• Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) — drain cleaner
• Calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂) — lime water
• Metal oxides (CuO, MgO, Fe₂O₃)
• Metal hydroxides

Alkalis are bases that dissolve in water.

Neutralisation:
Acid + Base → Salt + Water
HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O

The H⁺ ions from the acid react with the OH⁻ ions from the base to form water:
H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

Specific neutralisation reactions:
• Acid + metal oxide → salt + water
• Acid + metal hydroxide → salt + water
• Acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide
• Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen
Neutralising stomach acid

Antacids contain bases like Mg(OH)₂ or CaCO₃. They neutralise excess stomach acid (HCl):
Mg(OH)₂(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l)
This relieves heartburn and indigestion.

Removing rust with acid

Rust is Fe₂O₃ (iron(III) oxide). It reacts with acid:
Fe₂O₃(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2FeCl₃(aq) + 3H₂O(l)
The acid dissolves the rust, leaving clean metal underneath. This is called 'pickling'.

Neutralisation: Acid + Base → Salt + Water

General ionic equation: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen
Acid + metal oxide → salt + water
Acid + metal hydroxide → salt + water
Acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + CO₂

Common Misconception

All acids are dangerous and corrosive.

Some acids are very weak and found in food (citric acid in lemons, tartaric acid in grapes, acetic acid in vinegar). The strength of an acid depends on how completely it dissociates in water, not on its concentration.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide pH scale diagram. Match acids and bases to everyday items. Use litmus paper or universal indicator if available.
Stretch:
Research the difference between strong and weak acids. Is hydrochloric acid always stronger than ethanoic acid?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Match each substance to its pH: lemon juice, bleach, pure water, stomach acid, baking soda solution.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Defines acids, bases and alkalis, and writes neutralisation equations confidently.
  • Getting there: Knows that acid + base = salt + water but cannot write specific equations.
  • Not yet: Cannot define acids and bases. Reteach with pH paper/litmus testing of common substances.

Lesson 2: Reactions of Acids with Metals and Carbonates

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Fizzy Reaction

Add acid to a carbonate (baking soda or marble chips). What do you observe? What gas is produced? How can you test for it?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Acid + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen
Only metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series react with dilute acids.

Key reactions:
• Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂
• Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂
• Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂ (iron forms Fe²⁺, iron(II))
• Cu + HCl → no reaction (copper is below hydrogen)

Acid + Metal carbonate → Salt + Water + Carbon dioxide
All metal carbonates react with acids (even those below hydrogen in the reactivity series).

Key reactions:
• CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂
• Na₂CO₃ + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + H₂O + CO₂
• CuCO₃ + 2HCl → CuCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

Test for CO₂: Bubble the gas through limewater (calcium hydroxide solution). If it turns milky/cloudy, the gas is CO₂.
CO₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O (white precipitate of calcium carbonate)

Acid + Metal oxide → Salt + Water
• CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O
• Fe₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2FeCl₃ + 3H₂O
• MgO + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O
Making hydrogen from acid and metal

Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂
Observations: fizzing, magnesium dissolves, gas produced (test: squeaky pop with a lit splint). The solution remains colourless (MgCl₂ is colourless).

Lime water test for CO₂

Bubble gas through clear limewater: CO₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O
The limewater turns milky/cloudy because insoluble CaCO₃ forms. This is a specific test for carbon dioxide.

Acid + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen
HCl + Mg → MgCl₂ + H₂
H₂SO₄ + Zn → ZnSO₄ + H₂

Acid + Metal carbonate → Salt + Water + CO₂
2HCl + CaCO₃ → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

Test for CO₂: limewater turns milky
CO₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O

Test for H₂: squeaky pop with lit splint

Common Misconception

All metals react with acids.

Only metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series react with dilute acids. Copper, silver, gold, and platinum do not react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid. This is a useful way to test if a metal is above or below hydrogen.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a flowchart: Does the substance react with acid? Metal → check reactivity series. Metal oxide → yes. Metal carbonate → yes. What gas is produced? Metal → hydrogen. Carbonate → CO₂.
Stretch:
Calculate the volume of CO₂ produced when 10g of CaCO₃ reacts with excess HCl. (Use Mr and molar volume.)

Plenary (5 minutes)

Write equations for: iron + sulfuric acid, sodium carbonate + hydrochloric acid, copper oxide + sulfuric acid. Predict observations for each.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Writes balanced equations for acid reactions and can predict products and observations.
  • Getting there: Knows the pattern (acid + metal → salt + hydrogen) but cannot always balance equations.
  • Not yet: Cannot write acid reactions. Reteach with physical demonstrations and the general patterns.

Lesson 3: Uses of Bases and pH

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Household Chemistry

List household products that are acidic (vinegar, lemon juice) and basic (baking soda, bleach, soap). Why do we use them? What would happen if we mixed them?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Strong and weak acids:
• Strong acid: completely dissociates (ionises) in water → releases all H⁺ ions. E.g. HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃
• Weak acid: only partially dissociates → most remain as molecules, few H⁺ ions. E.g. ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH), citric acid, carbonic acid (H₂CO₃)

Uses of alkalis:
• Sodium hydroxide (NaOH): drain cleaner (dissolves hair and grease), manufacture of soap, paper
• Calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂): neutralising acidic soils in agriculture, lime mortar in building
• Ammonia (NH₃): cleaning products (window cleaners), fertiliser production
• Antacids: Mg(OH)₂ or CaCO₃ neutralise stomach acid

pH scale:
• 0-6: acidic (lower = more acidic)
• 7: neutral (pure water)
• 8-14: alkaline (higher = more alkaline)

Indicators:
• Litmus: red in acid, blue in alkali
• Universal indicator: full colour range (red = strong acid, orange = weak acid, green = neutral, blue = weak alkali, purple = strong alkali)
• Phenolphthalein: colourless in acid, pink in alkali
Strong vs weak acid

0.1 mol/dm³ HCl and 0.1 mol/dm³ ethanoic acid (vinegar) both have the same concentration. But HCl has a much lower pH (~1) than ethanoic acid (~3) because HCl releases all its H⁺ ions while ethanoic acid releases only a few.

Neutralising acidic soil

Some soils are too acidic for plants to grow well. Farmers add calcium hydroxide (lime) to neutralise the acid. Ca(OH)₂ + 2H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + 2H₂O. This raises the pH to a level suitable for crops.

Strong acid: HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻ (100% dissociation)
Weak acid: CH₃COOH ⇌ H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻ (partial dissociation)

pH scale: 0 (strong acid) ← 7 (neutral) → 14 (strong alkali)

Neutralisation: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

Common Misconception

A strong acid is always more concentrated than a weak acid.

'Strong' and 'weak' refer to how completely the acid dissociates, not its concentration. You can have a dilute strong acid (low concentration, but fully dissociated) and a concentrated weak acid (high concentration, but barely dissociated). A dilute strong acid can have a lower pH than a concentrated weak acid.

Differentiation

Support:
Use the 'open door' analogy: strong acid = all doors open (all H⁺ ions released). Weak acid = mostly closed doors (few H⁺ ions released).
Stretch:
Research: what is the pH of gastric acid in the stomach? Why doesn't it damage the stomach lining? What is the role of mucus?

Plenary (5 minutes)

True or false: strong acids completely dissociate / weak acids don't dissociate at all / pH 7 is always neutral / antacids are bases. Justify each answer.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Distinguishes strong and weak acids, describes uses of alkalis, and explains the pH scale.
  • Getting there: Knows the pH scale but confuses strong/concentrated and weak/dilute.
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish strong and weak acids. Reteach with the open door analogy and pH testing.

Homework Suggestions

  • Write 10 balanced equations for acid reactions
  • Classify each reaction: acid + metal, acid + oxide, acid + hydroxide, acid + carbonate
  • Research: what are the environmental effects of acid rain?
