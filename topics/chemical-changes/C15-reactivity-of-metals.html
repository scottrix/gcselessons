C15: Reactivity of Metals

Foundation All Boards

Order metals by reactivity, explain the reactivity series in terms of electron loss, and describe displacement and extraction reactions.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • State the reactivity series and explain reactivity in terms of tendency to lose electrons
  • Predict displacement reactions and describe how metals are extracted from their ores
  • Explain the conditions used in the extraction of aluminium by electrolysis

Prerequisites

  • Atoms and Elements (C1) — electron arrangements, reactivity
  • The Periodic Table (C2) — groups, metals

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Printed reactivity series
  • Video clips of metal reactions with water/steam
  • Iron nails and copper sulfate solution for displacement demo (if available)

Lesson 1: The Reactivity Series

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Which Metal Reacts Most?

Show (or describe) videos of lithium, sodium, potassium, magnesium, zinc, iron and copper being added to cold water or steam. Which reacts most violently? Can you put them in order?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The reactivity series orders metals from most to least reactive:

Most reactive → least reactive:
K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > H > Cu > Ag > Au

Why the order?
Reactivity depends on how easily a metal atom loses its outer electron(s) to form a positive ion.

Factors affecting reactivity of metals:
• Atomic radius: larger atoms → outer electron further from nucleus → easier to remove → more reactive
• Number of outer electrons: metals with fewer outer electrons (e.g. 1 in Group 1) tend to lose them more easily
• Nuclear charge: more protons → stronger attraction on outer electrons → harder to remove → less reactive (but atomic radius is usually more important)

Reactions with water and acids:
• K, Na: react vigorously with cold water
• Ca: reacts with cold water (less vigorously)
• Mg: reacts with steam (not cold water)
• Zn, Fe: react with steam
• Cu, Ag, Au: do not react with water or steam
• Metals above hydrogen in the series react with dilute acids
Potassium in water

2K(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq) + H₂(g). Potassium reacts so violently the hydrogen catches fire with a lilac flame. This shows potassium is very reactive — it loses its outer electron very easily.

Why gold doesn't react

Gold (Au) has a very high ionisation energy — its outer electron is very strongly held by the nucleus. It doesn't lose electrons easily, so it doesn't react with water, oxygen, or most acids. This is why gold jewellery stays shiny.

Reactivity series (mnemonic):
"Kevin Nelly Cries Much About Zapping Iron's Feelings And Can't Get Any Silver Or Gold"
K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, H, Cu, Ag, Au

Common Misconception

Reactivity is about how much energy a reaction produces.

Reactivity is about how easily a metal loses electrons. A vigorous reaction (like potassium in water) is not just about energy — it's about the ease of electron loss. Some exothermic reactions involve unreactive substances.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide the reactivity series as a poster. Use colour-coding: red for very reactive, amber for moderate, green for low.
Stretch:
Research: why is aluminium apparently unreactive despite being high in the reactivity series? What is the oxide layer?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Place 6 unfamiliar metals in the reactivity series based on given descriptions of their reactions.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: States the reactivity series and explains reactivity in terms of electron loss and atomic structure.
  • Getting there: Knows the order but cannot explain why metals have different reactivities.
  • Not yet: Cannot remember or use the reactivity series. Reteach with videos of metal reactions and the mnemonic.

Lesson 2: Displacement and Extraction

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Displacement Challenge

If you put iron nails in copper sulfate solution, what do you think will happen? Give a prediction based on the reactivity series.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Displacement reactions:
A more reactive metal will displace a less reactive metal from its salt solution.
If metal A is above metal B in the reactivity series, A will displace B from a solution of B's salt.

Examples:
• Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s) — iron displaces copper
• Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s) — zinc displaces copper
• Cu(s) + ZnSO₄(aq) → no reaction — copper cannot displace zinc (it's less reactive)

Extraction of metals:
How a metal is extracted depends on its reactivity:

Very reactive metals (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al): extracted by electrolysis of the molten ore
• Aluminum: electrolysis of molten aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃) dissolved in cryolite

Moderately reactive metals (Zn, Fe): extracted by reduction with carbon in a blast furnace
• Iron: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ (in blast furnace)

Less reactive metals (Cu, Ag): found native (uncombined) or extracted by heating with carbon

Unreactive metals (Au, Pt): found native in the ground
Iron nail in copper sulfate

Iron is more reactive than copper. Iron displaces copper from the solution.
Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
Blue copper sulfate solution turns green (iron(II) sulfate). Brown copper metal deposits on the nail.

Aluminium extraction

Al₂O₃ is electrolysed (molten). At the cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al (molten aluminium collected). At the anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻. Cryolite is added to lower the melting point from 2050°C to about 960°C.

Displacement: A + BC → AC + B (if A is more reactive than B)

Reduction with carbon: Metal oxide + Carbon → Metal + CO₂
Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂

Electrolysis of Al₂O₃:
Cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al
Anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻

Common Misconception

All metals are extracted by heating with carbon.

Only moderately reactive metals are extracted with carbon. Very reactive metals (like aluminium) require electrolysis because carbon cannot reduce their oxides (carbon is not reactive enough). Unreactive metals are found native.

Differentiation

Support:
Use the reactivity series poster as a reference. For displacement, use the 'musical chairs' analogy: the more reactive metal takes the place of the less reactive one in the salt solution.
Stretch:
Research: why is aluminium extraction so energy-intensive? What environmental impact does this have?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Predict whether displacement will occur: Zn + FeCl₂, Cu + AgNO₃, Mg + ZnSO₄. Explain using the reactivity series.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Predicts displacement reactions and describes extraction methods based on metal reactivity.
  • Getting there: Can predict displacement but cannot always explain extraction methods.
  • Not yet: Cannot use the reactivity series for predictions. Reteach with physical demonstrations of displacement.

Lesson 3: Extraction of Aluminium

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Aluminium Facts

Aluminium is the most abundant metal in the Earth's crust. Yet it was once more expensive than gold! Why? Because extracting it requires huge amounts of electricity.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Why aluminium is extracted by electrolysis (not carbon reduction):
Aluminium is more reactive than carbon. Carbon cannot displace aluminium from aluminium oxide. Electrolysis is the only way to extract it.

The process:
1. Purification: Bauxite ore (Al₂O₃) is purified by dissolving in NaOH (Bayer process) to remove impurities (iron oxide = red mud)
2. Dissolving: Pure Al₂O₃ is dissolved in cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) to lower the melting point from 2050°C to about 960°C (saves energy)
3. Electrolysis: Molten mixture is electrolysed

Electrode reactions:
• Cathode (negative): Al³⁺ ions gain electrons → molten aluminium metal
  Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al
• Anode (positive): O²⁻ ions lose electrons → oxygen gas
  2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻

Why the anodes need replacing:
The oxygen produced reacts with the carbon anodes to form CO₂, gradually burning them away. The anodes must be replaced regularly (cost and environmental concern).

Properties of aluminium:
• Low density (lightweight)
• Resistant to corrosion (forms protective oxide layer)
• Good conductor of electricity
• Malleable and ductile
• Silvery-white, lustrous
• Recyclable (recycling uses only 5% of the energy needed for extraction)
Why cryolite is added

Pure Al₂O₃ melts at 2050°C — this requires enormous energy. Adding cryolite lowers the melting point to about 960°C, making the process much more energy-efficient and cheaper. Cryolite is not used up in the reaction.

Anode consumption

The carbon anodes react with oxygen: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g). This means the anodes gradually burn away and must be replaced every few weeks. This is both a cost and an environmental issue (CO₂ emissions).

Purification: Bauxite (Al₂O₃ + impurities) → dissolve in NaOH → pure Al₂O₃

Electrolysis setup:
Electrolyte: Al₂O₃ dissolved in cryolite (molten)
Cathode: steel vessel (lined with carbon) — molten Al collects here
Anodes: carbon rods dipped into melt — O₂ produced here

Cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al
Anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻

Common Misconception

Cryolite is a reactant in the extraction of aluminium.

Cryolite is not used up — it is a solvent that lowers the melting point of aluminium oxide. It acts like a catalyst in that it is not consumed, but unlike a catalyst it does not speed up a reaction — it changes the physical conditions (melting point).

Differentiation

Support:
Draw a clear diagram of the electrolysis cell. Label cathode, anode, electrolyte, and where each product is formed. Use arrows to show ion movement.
Stretch:
Research: why is recycling aluminium so much more energy-efficient than extracting it from ore? Calculate the energy saving as a percentage.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Answer: Why is aluminium extracted by electrolysis? Why is cryolite added? What happens to the anodes? Why is aluminium recycling important?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains the entire aluminium extraction process, including why electrolysis is used, the role of cryolite, and what happens at each electrode.
  • Getting there: Knows electrolysis is used but cannot explain the role of cryolite or the electrode reactions.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain why aluminium is extracted by electrolysis. Reteach using the reactivity series and the carbon reduction limitation.

Homework Suggestions

  • Write up observations from a displacement reaction
  • Create a table showing each metal's position in the series, reaction with water, reaction with acid, extraction method
  • Research: why is the extraction of aluminium so important for the modern world?
