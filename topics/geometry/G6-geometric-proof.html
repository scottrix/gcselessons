G6: Geometric Proof

Higher Only All Boards

Higher tier: Construct formal geometric proofs using congruence, circle theorems, parallel line properties, and vector methods. Develop logical reasoning and clear mathematical communication.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Higher only
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Construct formal geometric proofs with clear statements and reasons
  • Use congruence criteria (SSS, SAS, ASA, RHS) and CPCTC in proofs
  • Apply circle theorems (G10) and parallel line angle facts (G3) in proofs
  • Use vectors to prove geometric results (collinearity, parallelism, ratios)
  • Prove properties of quadrilaterals, triangles, and circles

Prerequisites

  • Congruence criteria (G5)
  • Circle theorems (G10)
  • Vector basics (G24)
  • Angle facts and parallel lines (G3)

Materials & Equipment

  • Proof template worksheets
  • Reason banks (angle facts, circle theorems, congruence criteria)
  • Geometry software for exploration

Lesson 1: Proof Structure and Congruence Proofs

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

What is a Proof?

Discuss: A proof is a logical argument where each step follows from previous steps or accepted facts. Unlike "show that" (verify a given result), "prove that" requires building the argument from known facts.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Proof structure:
1. Diagram with clear labels
2. "Given:" — state known information
3. "To prove:" — state conclusion
4. "Proof:" — numbered steps with reasons
5. "QED" or "□" to conclude
Reasons (justifications):
- Given / Given in question
- Definition (e.g., "definition of parallelogram")
Angle facts (G3): straight line, triangle sum, alternate, corresponding, co-interior
- Circle theorems (G10): angle at centre, angles in same segment, cyclic quadrilateral, tangent-radius, etc.
- Congruence criteria (G5): SSS, SAS, ASA, RHS
- CPCTC: Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are Congruent
- Properties of shapes (G4): parallelogram, rhombus, etc.
- Algebraic manipulation
Common proof types:
- Prove triangles congruent → deduce sides/angles equal
- Prove lines parallel (equal alternate/corresponding angles)
- Prove points collinear (angles sum to 180°)
- Prove quadrilateral is specific type (parallelogram, cyclic, etc.)
Worked Example

"Given: ABCD is a parallelogram. E is midpoint of AD. F is midpoint of BC. Prove: EBFD is a parallelogram."
Proof:
1. AD = BC (opposite sides of parallelogram)
2. AE = ½AD, CF = ½BC (midpoints)
3. AE = CF (halves of equals)
4. AD ∥ BC (opposite sides of parallelogram) → AE ∥ CF
5. AE = CF and AE ∥ CF → AECF is a parallelogram (one pair opposite sides equal and parallel)
6. EB ∥ FD (opposite sides of parallelogram AECF)
7. AE = CF and AD = BC → DE = BF (subtracting equals)
8. DE ∥ BF (AD ∥ BC)
9. DE = BF and DE ∥ BF → EBFD is a parallelogram □

Common Misconception

"A diagram is a proof"

A diagram illustrates but does not prove. Measurements from diagrams are not exact. Proofs require logical reasoning from stated facts, not visual estimation.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide proof skeletons with missing reasons. Use two-column format (Statement | Reason). Focus on congruence proofs first. Colour-code: Given (blue), Deductions (green), Circle theorems (orange), Congruence (purple).
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Prove: "The angle bisectors of a triangle are concurrent (incentre)." "The perpendicular bisectors of a triangle are concurrent (circumcentre)." Explore proof by contradiction. Investigate projective geometry theorems (Ceva, Menelaus).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Proof Critique

Show a flawed proof (e.g., assumes what it's proving, uses unmarked parallel lines, skips steps). Students identify errors.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Writes complete formal proofs with clear reasons, uses multiple fact types
  • Getting there: Identifies correct approach but omits reasons or logical steps
  • Not yet: Cannot structure a proof or identify relevant facts

Lesson 2: Circle Theorem and Vector Proofs

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Circle Theorem Recall

Quick fire: "Angle at centre is?" "Angles in same segment?" "Cyclic quadrilateral opposite angles?" "Tangent-radius angle?" "Alternate segment?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Circle theorem proofs: Combine circle theorems with triangle congruence, isosceles triangles (radii), and angle facts.
Example: Prove alternate segment theorem. Given: Tangent at A, chord AB, point C on major arc. Prove ∠BAT = ∠ACB.
Proof uses: Angle in semicircle (90°), angle sum of triangle, tangent-radius (90°).
Vector proofs (G24):
- Vectors for sides: AB = b − a
- Parallel: AB = k·CD (scalar multiple)
- Collinear: A, B, C collinear if AB = k·AC
- Midpoints: M = (a+b)/2
- Ratios: AP:PB = m:n → p = (na + mb)/(m+n)
Proving with vectors: Express key vectors in terms of basis vectors. Show parallelism (scalar multiple) or collinearity. Use for: midpoints of quadrilateral form parallelogram (Varignon), centroid divides median 2:1, diagonals of parallelogram bisect.
Worked Example (Vector)

"OABC is a parallelogram. M is midpoint of OA. N is midpoint of BC. Prove MN passes through intersection of diagonals."
Let OA = a, OC = c. OB = a + c. Diagonals intersect at D (midpoint of both). OD = ½(a + c).
OM = ½a. CN = ½CB = ½a. ON = OC + CN = c + ½a.
MN = ON − OM = (c + ½a) − ½a = c. OD = ½(a + c).
MD = OD − OM = ½(a + c) − ½a = ½c. So MD = ½ MN → M, D, N collinear □

Worked Example (Circle Theorem)

"Prove: The tangent at the midpoint of an arc is parallel to the chord joining the arc endpoints."
Let arc AB have midpoint M. Tangent at M. Chord AB. ∠AMB = ∠ACB (angles in same segment, C on arc). Tangent-chord theorem: ∠(tangent, AM) = ∠ABM. But MA = MB (equal arcs → equal chords) → ∠MAB = ∠MBA. So tangent ∥ AB (alternate angles equal) □

Common Misconception

"In vector proofs, you can just add vectors without defining them first"

Always define basis vectors clearly (e.g., "Let OA = a, OB = b"). Every vector must be expressed in terms of these. Undefined vectors make the proof meaningless.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide vector proof templates with basis vectors given. Focus on proving parallel (show one is scalar multiple of other). Use grid paper for visual vector addition.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Prove Apollonius circle theorem using vectors. Use vectors to prove Ceva's theorem. Explore complex numbers as alternative to vectors for geometric proofs.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Choose Your Method

Given a geometric statement, students decide: congruence proof, circle theorem proof, vector proof, or coordinate proof? Discuss advantages of each.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Selects appropriate method, executes vector/circle theorem proofs correctly
  • Getting there: Can do congruence proofs but struggles with vector notation or circle theorem application
  • Not yet: Cannot apply circle theorems or vectors in proof context

Lesson 3: Multi-Step Problems and Exam Technique

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Prove or Disprove

"The diagonals of a kite are equal." False (only in rhombus/square). "The line joining midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side." True (Midpoint theorem).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Exam technique for proofs:
- Read question carefully: "Prove that..." vs "Show that..." vs "Explain why..."
- Annotate diagram with given info
- Plan: what facts do I have? what do I need?
- Write reasons for EVERY statement
- Use standard notation: ∠ for angle, ≅ for congruent, ∥ for parallel
- Don't assume facts not given (e.g., lines parallel unless stated/proven)
- Check: does each step follow logically?
Common exam proof questions:
- Prove triangle congruence in complex diagrams
- Prove quadrilateral is cyclic / parallelogram / etc.
- Prove lines are parallel / points collinear
- Vector proofs: midpoints, ratios, intersections
- Circle theorem proofs: alternate segment, tangent properties
Worked Example

"A, B, C, D are points on a circle. AB = BC. AC and BD intersect at E. Prove: AE = EC."
Proof:
1. AB = BC (given) → arc AB = arc BC (equal chords subtend equal arcs)
2. ∠ADB = ∠BDC (angles subtended by equal arcs)
3. ∠BAC = ∠BCA (base angles of isosceles triangle ABC, AB = BC)
4. ∠AEB = ∠CED (vertically opposite)
5. ∠ABE = ∠DCE (angles in same segment, arc AD)
6. △AEB ≅ △CED (AAS: ∠ABE = ∠DCE, AB = CD? Wait... AB = BC given, not AB = CD)
Alternative: Use equal arcs → ∠ADB = ∠BDC. ∠ABD = ∠ACD (same segment). ∠AEB = ∠CED. △AEB ≅ △CED (ASA)? Need side...
Better: Arc AB = arc BC → chord AB = BC. ∠ADB = ∠BDC. ∠ABD = ∠DCA (same segment). AD = DC (equal arcs). △ABD ≅ △CBD (ASA). AE = EC (CPCTC) □

Common Misconception

"If the diagram looks like it, I can state it as fact"

Never assume from diagram: right angles, parallel lines, equal lengths, collinearity. Only use what is given or proven. "Looks like a right angle" = 0 marks.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Practise "prove triangle congruent" questions with clear given information. Provide checklist: 1) Label diagram, 2) List known equalities, 3) Find 3rd equality, 4) State criterion.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Open-ended: "Given a cyclic quadrilateral, prove as many properties as you can." Explore proofs using inversion or complex numbers. Past paper proof questions (AQA, Edexcel, OCR Higher).

Plenary (5 minutes)

One-Minute Proof Plan

Show a proof question. Students write a bullet-point plan in 1 minute (no full sentences). Share and compare approaches.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Plans and executes multi-step proofs under time pressure, adapts to unfamiliar configurations
  • Getting there: Can do standard proofs but struggles with novel configurations or vector methods
  • Not yet: Cannot plan a proof from scratch

Homework Suggestions

  • Complete past paper geometric proof questions (Higher tier)
  • Prove: "The perpendicular from the centre of a circle to a chord bisects the chord" (using congruence)
  • Prove: "The line joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and half its length" (using vectors)
  • Create a "Proof Toolkit" card: structure, common reasons, vector templates, circle theorem list
