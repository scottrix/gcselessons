Lesson 2: Core Concepts: predestination and free will

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on predestination and free will. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Muslims believe in qadr (predestination) — that Allah has foreknowledge and control over all things. However, most Muslims also believe humans have free will to choose their actions. The relationship between God's decree and human freedom is one of the most debated topics in Islamic theology.
Exam Tip: The key tension: if Allah has already decreed everything, how can humans be responsible for their actions? Most Muslims resolve this by saying Allah's foreknowledge is not the same as forcing — he knows what you will freely choose. You are still responsible.
RS Exam Tips — Predestination and Free Will: 1. For Predestination and Free Will questions, always include specific scriptural or source references where relevant 2. Explain both religious and non-religious viewpoints 3. Use specialist vocabulary precisely (e.g. terms from the specification) 4. Evaluate by analysing strengths and weaknesses of different views before reaching your conclusion 5. Link your discussion of Predestination and Free Will to its real-world impact on believers and society
UK Context: When a British Muslim says ‘insha’Allah’ (‘if Allah wills’) when discussing future plans, they are acknowledging al-qadr — that nothing happens except by Allah’s permission. This does not mean they believe their efforts are pointless; rather, it expresses trust in Allah’s wisdom while still taking responsibility for their actions.
UK Context: The Shi’a emphasis on free will and justice is reflected in their political and social engagement in Britain. Shi’a community organisations such as the Al-Khoei Foundation are active in promoting human rights, social justice and interfaith dialogue, reflecting the belief that Allah requires humans to actively pursue justice.
UK Context: During the COVID-19 pandemic, British Muslim leaders emphasised both qadr and free will: the virus was part of Allah’s decree, but Muslims had a responsibility to follow public health guidelines (social distancing, vaccination) as acts of free will that protected themselves and others.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Explain two Muslim beliefs about predestination and free will.

Answer: 1) Qadr — Muslims believe Allah has decreed all things and has complete knowledge of everything that will happen. Surah 9:51 states "Nothing will happen to us except what Allah has decreed for us." 2) Free will — most Muslims also believe humans have genuine freedom to choose their actions. Without free will, the Day of Judgement would be unjust, as people cannot be held responsible for actions they were forced to take. The balance: Allah knows what we will choose, but we still choose freely.

Lesson 3: Application: predestination and free will

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Exam Tip, UK Context, UK Context. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain two Muslim beliefs about predestination and free will.

Answer: 1) Qadr — Muslims believe Allah has decreed all things and has complete knowledge of everything that will happen. Surah 9:51 states "Nothing will happen to us except what Allah has decreed for us." 2) Free will — most Muslims also believe humans have genuine freedom to choose their actions. Without free will, the Day of Judgement would be unjust, as people cannot be held responsible for actions they were forced to take. The balance: Allah knows what we will choose, but we still choose freely.

Q2: "If God knows everything, humans do not have free will." Evaluate this statement.

Answer: Some argue that if Allah has complete foreknowledge, human choices are already determined. However, most Muslim scholars distinguish between God's knowledge and God's compulsion — Allah knowing what you will choose does not mean he forces you to choose it. An analogy: a teacher may know a student will fail an exam, but the student still freely chooses not to revise. For Muslims, belief in both qadr and free will is essential — qadr gives trust in Allah's plan, while free will gives moral responsibility.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: predestination and free will

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on predestination and free will from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Always refer to specific sources of authority (scripture, sacred texts, religious leaders) | Explain contrasting beliefs — different views within and between traditions | Use key terminology precisely (e.g. tawhid, atonement, sanctity of life) | For 12-mark evaluation questions, consider both sides before reaching a justified conclusion | Link beliefs to their impact on individuals, communities and society | Acknowledge diversity — no religious tradition has a single, uniform view
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often confuse Sunni and Shi'a beliefs about predestination. Wrong: "All Muslims believe everything is predestined and humans have no free will." Correct: "Most Muslims believe God has foreknowledge but humans have free will to choose; the balance between predestination and free will is understood differently across traditions."
