Lesson 2: Core Concepts: angels

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on angels. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Muslims believe angels (mala'ikah) are created from light and serve as God's messengers. They have no free will — they always obey Allah. Belief in angels is one of the Six Articles of Faith in Sunni Islam.
RS Exam Tips — Angels: 1. For Angels questions, always include specific scriptural or source references where relevant 2. Explain both religious and non-religious viewpoints 3. Use specialist vocabulary precisely (e.g. terms from the specification) 4. Evaluate by analysing strengths and weaknesses of different views before reaching your conclusion 5. Link your discussion of Angels to its real-world impact on believers and society
UK Context: In the UK, the concept of guardian angels provides comfort to many British Muslims. The belief that two angels record all deeds motivates ethical behaviour: a Muslim knows that even secret actions are witnessed and will be accounted for on the Day of Judgement.
UK Context: The distinction between angels (who obey perfectly) and humans (who can choose to obey or disobey) is central to the Islamic understanding of morality. Humans are morally superior to angels when they choose good, because their choice involves overcoming temptation — an option angels do not face.
UK Context: During the COVID-19 pandemic, many British Muslims found comfort in the belief that Allah’s angels were watching over them. Imams reminded congregations that the recording angels witnessed both their patience in hardship and their kindness to others during the crisis.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Explain two Muslim beliefs about angels.

Answer: 1) Jibril is the chief angel who brought revelation from Allah to the prophets, most importantly delivering the Qur'an to Muhammad over 23 years. Without Jibril, Muslims would not have the Qur'an. 2) Raqib and Atid are recording angels who write down every good and bad deed a person does. These records will be used on the Day of Judgement, which motivates Muslims to live morally because they know every action is recorded.

Lesson 3: Application: angels

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: RS Exam Tips — Angels, UK Context, UK Context. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain two Muslim beliefs about angels.

Answer: 1) Jibril is the chief angel who brought revelation from Allah to the prophets, most importantly delivering the Qur'an to Muhammad over 23 years. Without Jibril, Muslims would not have the Qur'an. 2) Raqib and Atid are recording angels who write down every good and bad deed a person does. These records will be used on the Day of Judgement, which motivates Muslims to live morally because they know every action is recorded.

Q2: "Belief in angels is not important for Muslims today." Evaluate this statement.

Answer: Some might argue angels are irrelevant because they are invisible and cannot be proven scientifically. However, for Muslims, belief in angels is essential — it is one of the Six Articles of Faith. Angels demonstrate that Allah is actively involved in the world, they record human deeds for Judgement Day, and Jibril delivered the Qur'an itself. Without angels, the connection between Allah and humanity would be broken. Belief in angels strengthens faith and encourages moral behaviour.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: angels

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on angels from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Always refer to specific sources of authority (scripture, sacred texts, religious leaders) | Explain contrasting beliefs — different views within and between traditions | Use key terminology precisely (e.g. tawhid, atonement, sanctity of life) | For 12-mark evaluation questions, consider both sides before reaching a justified conclusion | Link beliefs to their impact on individuals, communities and society | Acknowledge diversity — no religious tradition has a single, uniform view
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often confuse Sunni and Shi'a beliefs about predestination. Wrong: "All Muslims believe everything is predestined and humans have no free will." Correct: "Most Muslims believe God has foreknowledge but humans have free will to choose; the balance between predestination and free will is understood differently across traditions."
