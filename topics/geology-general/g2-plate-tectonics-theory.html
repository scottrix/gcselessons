Lesson 2: Core Concepts: plate tectonics theory

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on plate tectonics theory. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Plate tectonics states the lithosphere is divided into large plates that move on the asthenosphere, driven by convection currents in the mantle
Key Fact: Divergent boundaries: plates move apart (e.g. Mid-Atlantic Ridge), creating new oceanic crust
Key Fact: Convergent boundaries: plates collide; oceanic-continental produces subduction zones and volcanoes; continental-continental produces fold mountains
Key Fact: Transform boundaries: plates slide past each other (e.g. San Andreas Fault), causing earthquakes
Key Fact: Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912, citing fossil, rock and coastline evidence, but the mechanism was unknown
Key Fact: Seafloor spreading was confirmed by paleomagnetic striping in oceanic crust and age dating of ocean floor rocks

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on plate tectonics theory from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Three boundary types: divergent, convergent, transform | Wegener's evidence is commonly tested, know at least 3 lines of evidence | Paleomagnetic striping is key evidence for seafloor spreading, symmetrical about the ridge | Subduction: denser oceanic ALWAYS sinks beneath lighter continental
Common Errors: ✗ Continental drift and plate tectonics are the same theory ✓ Continental drift (Wegener, 1912) proposed continents moved through ocean crust; plate tectonics (1960s) explains that the lithosphere is divided into plates that move on the asthenosphere ✗ Mantle convection currents drag plates from above ✓ Convection currents push plates from below and also create ridge push and slab pull; slab pull is the dominant driving force ✗ Only oceanic crust can subduct ✓ While oceanic crust typically subducts due to higher density, continental crust can also be forced down, though it is more buoyant and resists subduction
