Lesson 2: Core Concepts: themes in prose literature

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on themes in prose literature. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Leadership is a central theme: Caesar presents himself as the ideal Roman general — decisive, brave, and always in control. Contrast this with Tacitus's portrait of weak or tyrannical emperors.
Key Fact: Roman imperialism: Caesar justifies conquest as bringing civilisation and order to barbarians; Tacitus questions whether empire brings freedom or oppression.
Key Fact: Virtus (manly courage/excellence) is a key Roman value: in Caesar it means military bravery; in Cicero it means moral courage to defend the Republic.
Key Fact: Pietas (duty to gods, country, and family) drives Roman heroes but can also create conflict: duty to the state vs personal loyalty.
Key Fact: Freedom (libertas) vs tyranny: Cicero defends the Republic's libertas against Catiline; Tacitus mourns its loss under the emperors.
Key Fact: Barbarians vs Romans: Caesar portrays Gauls as fierce, unpredictable, and inferior to Roman discipline; but he also admires individual Gallic leaders like Vercingetorix.

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Discuss how the themes of leadership and imperialism are presented in Latin prose literature, referring to specific set text authors. (8 marks) <div class="

Leadership and imperialism are central themes in Latin prose literature, but different authors present them very differently. Caesar presents leadership as the exercise of personal virtus and decisive action. In De Bello Gallico, he portrays himself as the ideal leader: calm in crisis (as in the Nervii battle), resourceful, and always present at the front. His leadership justifies Roman imperialism: the conquest of Gaul is presented as necessary to protect Rome from barbarian threats and to bring civilisation to backward peoples. The Gallic tribes are shown as fierce but disorganised, needing Roman order. However, Caesar's self-portrait is propaganda: his narrative deliberately enhances his dignitas and political standing back in Rome. Cicero presents a different model of leadership: not military but political and moral. In the Catilinarians, his leadership is oratorical and intellectual — he saves the Republic through rhetoric and political wisdom, not swords. His imperialism is defensive: he seeks to protect Roman libertas from internal enemies who would destroy the Republic from within. Tacitus offers the most critical perspective on both themes.
