Lesson 2: Core Concepts: narrative & style in prose

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on narrative & style in prose. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Latin prose style analyses HOW an author writes, not just WHAT they say. Style includes word order, sentence length, rhythm, and rhetorical devices.
Key Fact: Word order in Latin is flexible and significant: placing a word first or last gives it emphasis. The first and last positions in a sentence are the most emphatic.
Key Fact: Hyperbaton is the separation of an adjective from its noun, which draws attention to both words and creates a literary effect (e.g. 'magna cum virtute' rather than 'cum magna virtute').
Key Fact: Reported speech (oratio obliqua) allows authors to present events through characters' perspectives, creating drama and showing different viewpoints without direct speech.
Key Fact: Tricolon (group of three) is a common rhetorical device: three parallel phrases build emphasis and climax (e.g. veni, vidi, vici).
Key Fact: Anaphora is the repetition of a word or phrase at the beginning of successive clauses for emphasis: 'non illam... non illam... non illam' (not this... not this... not this).

Extended question: Full-Mark Response How does an author's prose style contribute to their narrative? Discuss with reference to at least three stylistic features, using examples from set text authors. (8 marks) <div class="

An author's prose style is central to how they tell their story and shape the reader's response. Word order is the most fundamental tool: because Latin allows flexible word order, authors can place key words in emphatic positions. Caesar, for example, often places the enemy tribe name first in a sentence to foreground the threat, or positions a key verb at the end for climactic effect. Tacitus exploits word order more subtly, placing a loaded adjective last in a sentence so it delivers a devastating final judgment, such as his description of Tiberius's reign ending with a damning final word. Rhetorical devices build power and persuasion. Cicero uses anaphora (repetition of words at the start of successive clauses) to create a mounting, overwhelming effect in his orations: 'non ego... non ego... non ego' insists on his point with increasing force. Caesar uses asyndeton (omission of conjunctions) for speed and urgency: his lists of Gallic tribes without 'et' create a sense of overwhelming numbers and rapid action.
