Lesson 2: Core Concepts: elizabeth's background and accession

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on elizabeth's background and accession. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Context: Elizabeth I was born on 7 September 1533 at Greenwich Palace, the daughter of Henry VIII and Anne Boleyn. Her path to the throne was far from straightforward - she was declared illegitimate, excluded from the succession, imprisoned in the Tower of London, and survived the dangerous reigns of her half-brother Edward VI and her half-sister Mary I. These experiences shaped her into a cautious, pragmatic, and deeply intelligent ruler.
How Elizabeth's Early Life Shaped Her Rule: The execution of her mother and her own imprisonment in the Tower taught Elizabeth the dangers of political life and the importance of caution. She learned to keep her thoughts private and never reveal her hand prematurely. Her humanist education gave her the confidence and intelligence to rule. The religious turmoil of Edward VI's Protestant reign and Mary I's Catholic restoration convinced her that religious extremism was dangerous - a lesson that directly influenced her Religious Settlement.
Elizabeth's Approach: Elizabeth wanted a moderate settlement that would satisfy most people. She was not a religious extremist - she famously said she had "no desire to make windows into men's souls." Her goal was political stability, not religious purity. The Settlement was designed to be a compromise: Protestant enough to satisfy most English people, but traditional enough to make Catholicism possible in practice.
The Impact of the 1570 Excommunication: Pope Pius V's bull Regnans in Excelsius (25 February 1570) transformed the Catholic challenge from a religious issue into a political one. By absolving Catholics of their allegiance to Elizabeth, the Pope effectively made being Catholic equivalent to being a potential traitor. This forced Elizabeth's government to treat Catholics as a security threat, leading to increasing persecution, fines for recusancy (refusing to attend Church of England services), and the execution of Catholic priests.
Elizabeth's Response to Puritans: Elizabeth consistently refused to make further reforms. She insisted on conformity to the Settlement and demanded uniformity of worship. She blocked Puritan bills in Parliament, imprisoned Puritan agitators, and insisted on strict enforcement of the Act of Uniformity. She saw Puritanism as a threat to her authority and to the stability of the Church - if everyone could worship as they chose, there would be chaos.
Successes: The Settlement provided a workable compromise that lasted for Elizabeth's entire reign (45 years). Most English people accepted it, and the vast majority of parish churches conformed. It ended the violent religious oscillation of the previous 25 years (Henry VIII's break from Rome, Edward VI's Protestantism, Mary I's Catholic restoration). It established the Church of England as a distinctive "middle way" between Catholicism and Protestantism.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Why was Elizabeth's education unusual for a woman of her time? (2 marks)

Answer: Elizabeth's education was unusual because she received a humanist education normally reserved for boys: she was tutored by leading scholars like Roger Ascham, became fluent in Latin, Greek, French, Italian, and Spanish, studied rhetoric, philosophy, and theology, and was skilled in music. Most women of the era received only basic domestic education.

Lesson 3: Application: elizabeth's background and accession

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Context, Elizabeth&#x27;s Approach, The Impact of the 1570 Excommunication. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Why was Elizabeth's education unusual for a woman of her time? (2 marks)

Answer: Elizabeth's education was unusual because she received a humanist education normally reserved for boys: she was tutored by leading scholars like Roger Ascham, became fluent in Latin, Greek, French, Italian, and Spanish, studied rhetoric, philosophy, and theology, and was skilled in music. Most women of the era received only basic domestic education.

Q2: Describe two challenges Elizabeth faced when she became queen in November 1558. (4 marks)

Answer: Two challenges: (1) Religious division - England had swung from Henry VIII's break with Rome to Edward VI's Protestantism to Mary I's Catholic restoration, leaving the country deeply divided; (2) Financial crisis - Mary I left the Crown approximately £300,000 in debt. Other challenges include war with France, loss of Calais, and questions about Elizabeth's legitimacy.

Q3: Explain why Elizabeth called her Religious Settlement a "middle way." (4 marks)

Answer: The Settlement was a "middle way" because it was designed to be a compromise between Catholicism and Protestantism. Elizabeth became "Supreme Governor" (not the more Protestant "Supreme Head"); the communion service used deliberately ambiguous wording that could be interpreted as either Catholic or Protestant; some traditional vestments and ceremonies were retained while services were in English; clergy could marry (Protestant) but churches retained some decoration (Catholic).

Q4: How did both Catholics and Puritans challenge the Religious Settlement? (8 marks)

Answer: Catholics challenged the Settlement by: refusing the Oath of Supremacy (8 bishops were deprived); continuing to practice Catholicism privately; Pope Pius V excommunicated Elizabeth in 1570, absolving Catholics of their allegiance. Puritans challenged the Settlement by: refusing to wear vestments (37 London ministers suspended 1566); demanding the abolition of bishops; publishing the Admonition to Parliament (1572); holding unauthorized prophesyings. Elizabeth enforced conformity against both groups.

Q5: "The Religious Settlement of 1559 was Elizabeth's greatest achievement." How far do you agree? (16 marks + 4 SPaG)

Answer: A balanced answer should discuss: the Religious Settlement (it provided 45 years of relative stability, most people accepted it, it ended the violent religious oscillation of previous reigns); other achievements such as securing the succession (though she never married), dealing with foreign threats (the Armada), financial recovery, and the cultural flourishing of the Elizabethan age. Argue either that the Settlement was her greatest achievement because it solved the most dangerous problem, or that other achievements were equally important.

Q6: Explain the significance of the 1570 papal excommunication of Elizabeth. (4 marks)

Answer: The 1570 excommunication was significant because it transformed Catholicism from a religious difference into a political threat. By absolving Catholics of their allegiance to Elizabeth, Pope Pius V made being Catholic equivalent to being a potential traitor. This forced the government to treat Catholics as a security threat, leading to increasing persecution, fines for recusancy, and the execution of Catholic priests. It made compromise between Elizabeth and the Catholic Church impossible.
