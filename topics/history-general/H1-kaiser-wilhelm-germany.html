Lesson 2: Core Concepts: kaiser wilhelm and germany

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on kaiser wilhelm and germany. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Kaiser Wilhelm II became German Emperor (Kaiser) in June 1888 aged 29, following the death of his father Frederick III who had ruled for only 99 days. Wilhelm would reign until his abdication in November 1918.
Key Fact: Germany was a federal state with 25 states in 1871 (27 after 1912), each retaining its own ruler, government and laws. The Prussian King was automatically the German Kaiser.
Key Fact: Prussia was the largest and most powerful state in Germany. It covered 60% of German territory and contained 65% of the population. The King of Prussia was always the Kaiser.
Key Fact: Weltpolitik ("world policy") was Wilhelm II's aggressive foreign policy aiming to make Germany a major world power with colonies, a large navy, and international influence. It replaced Bismarck's Realpolitik ("practical politics") which had focused on maintaining peace and stability in Europe.
Key Fact: Admiral von Tirpitz and Wilhelm II pushed through a series of Navy Laws (1898, 1900, 1906, 1908, 1912) to build a fleet capable of challenging the Royal Navy. The aim was to make Germany a world power and force Britain to respect German interests.
Key Fact: The Social Democratic Party (SPD) was the main opposition to the Kaiser's regime. By 1912, the SPD was the largest party in the Reichstag with 110 seats (34.8% of the vote), yet the government continued to ignore its demands for reform.

Answer: The Chancellor was responsible only to the Kaiser, not the elected Reichstag, meaning parliament could not hold the government to account. The Prussian three-class voting system gave the wealthy minority control over elections, and Prussia had 17 of 58 Bundesrat votes, enough to veto any constitutional change.

Lesson 3: Application: kaiser wilhelm and germany

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: key terms from kaiser wilhelm and germany. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain two ways in which the German Constitution was undemocratic. (4 marks)

Answer: The Chancellor was responsible only to the Kaiser, not the elected Reichstag, meaning parliament could not hold the government to account. The Prussian three-class voting system gave the wealthy minority control over elections, and Prussia had 17 of 58 Bundesrat votes, enough to veto any constitutional change.

Q2: How far did Prussian influence dominate Imperial Germany? Explain your answer. (12 marks)

Answer: Prussia dominated through: the Kaiser was always the King of Prussia; Prussia had 17 of 58 Bundesrat votes (enough to block changes); the Junker class controlled the army and civil service; the three-class franchise in Prussia was deeply undemocratic. However, other states retained their own governments and the Reichstag was elected by universal male suffrage across all of Germany, giving some democratic check on Prussian power.

Q3: What was Weltpolitik and why did Wilhelm II pursue it instead of Bismarck's Realpolitik? (8 marks)

Answer: Weltpolitik was Wilhelm's policy of making Germany a world power through colonial expansion and naval build-up. He pursued it because he believed Germany deserved a "place in the sun," wanted to challenge British naval supremacy, and saw Bismarck's careful diplomacy as too cautious. Bismarck's Realpolitik had focused on peace and isolation of France; Wilhelm wanted global prestige.

Q4: Explain why the Navy Laws were significant for Germany's foreign relations. (8 marks)

Answer: The Navy Laws provoked a naval arms race with Britain, pushing Britain towards the Triple Entente with France and Russia (1907). They created financial strain, requiring higher taxes and diverting funds from social reform. The Morocco Crises (1905, 1911) showed Germany's aggressive diplomacy isolated it further. The Laws made Britain see Germany as a direct threat rather than a potential ally.

Q5: "The SPD was the biggest threat to the Kaiser's regime." How far do you agree? (16 marks)

Answer: The SPD was a significant threat as the largest Reichstag party by 1912 (110 seats), representing working-class demands for reform. However, the constitution limited the SPD's effectiveness: the Chancellor was appointed by the Kaiser, the Bundesrat could veto laws, and the army remained loyal to the Kaiser. Other threats included the Centre Party and progressive liberals. The Kaiser's regime survived because the constitution was designed to resist democratic pressure, not because the SPD was weak.
