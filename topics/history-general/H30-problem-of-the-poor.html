Lesson 2: Core Concepts: the problem of the poor

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on the problem of the poor. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Context: Poverty was one of the most serious problems in Elizabethan England. The population grew from approximately 3 million in 1550 to over 4 million by 1600, but the economy could not provide enough work or food for everyone. The dissolution of the monasteries (1536-41) had destroyed the main system of charitable support for the poor, and economic changes left increasing numbers of people destitute. The government was forced to create new legislation to deal with the crisis.
The Key Distinction: Elizabethan people divided the poor into two categories: The "deserving poor": Those who were poor through no fault of their own - the old, the sick, the disabled, widows, orphans, and those temporarily unemployed due to harvest failure. These people deserved sympathy and help. The "undeserving poor": Those who were believed to be poor through their own fault - the idle, the lazy, drunkards, and vagrants who chose not to work. These people deserved punishment, not help. This distinction was central to all Elizabethan poor law legislation.
The Harshness of the 1572 Act: The 1572 Act was deliberately brutal because it treated vagrancy as a criminal problem, not a social one. Burning through the ear permanently marked a person as a vagrant, making it impossible for them to escape the stigma. The threat of execution for a third offence was extreme, though it was rarely carried out in practice. The Act reflected the fear and anger that vagrants inspired in the propertied classes.
How Effective Were the Punishments?: The harsh punishments for vagrants were largely ineffective. Whipping and branding did not solve the underlying causes of poverty - vagrants returned to wandering because they had no alternative. The punishments were also difficult to enforce consistently: JPs were often reluctant to carry out extreme sentences; local communities sometimes sympathised with local vagrants; and the system of returning vagrants to their home parish was impractical. The fundamental problem was that legislation treated poverty as a moral failing rather than an economic problem.
Strengths: The Elizabethan poor laws created the first national system of poor relief in English history. The 1601 Poor Law remained the basis of the system for over 230 years. The distinction between deserving and undeserving poor ensured that the most vulnerable received some help. The compulsory poor rate provided a reliable source of funding. The parish system meant that relief was organised at a local level, making it more responsive to local needs.
Weaknesses: The system was inconsistent - each parish operated independently, leading to enormous variation in the quality and quantity of relief. The harsh treatment of vagrants did not address the causes of poverty. The parish system meant that the poor could not move to find work without losing their right to relief. The system was underfunded and overwhelmed during crisis years (like 1594-97). The distinction between deserving and undeserving was often arbitrary and cruel. The system stigmatised the poor and treated poverty as a personal moral failing.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Explain two causes of poverty in Elizabethan England. (4 marks)

Answer: Two causes: (1) Enclosure - landowners fenced off common land for sheep farming, depriving peasants of the land they needed for grazing and growing food, driving them off the land into towns; (2) Rising population - the population grew from 2.5 million to over 4 million between 1500 and 1600, creating more demand for food, housing, and jobs than the economy could supply. Other causes include bad harvests, debasement of coinage, and the dissolution of the monasteries.

Lesson 3: Application: the problem of the poor

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Context, The Key Distinction, The Harshness of the 1572 Act. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain two causes of poverty in Elizabethan England. (4 marks)

Answer: Two causes: (1) Enclosure - landowners fenced off common land for sheep farming, depriving peasants of the land they needed for grazing and growing food, driving them off the land into towns; (2) Rising population - the population grew from 2.5 million to over 4 million between 1500 and 1600, creating more demand for food, housing, and jobs than the economy could supply. Other causes include bad harvests, debasement of coinage, and the dissolution of the monasteries.

Q2: What was the difference between the "deserving poor" and the "undeserving poor"? (4 marks)

Answer: The "deserving poor" were those who were poor through no fault of their own - the old, sick, disabled, widows, and orphans - and were considered worthy of sympathy and help. The "undeserving poor" were the able-bodied who could work but chose not to - vagrants, beggars, and the idle - who were considered morally responsible for their poverty and deserving of punishment rather than help.

Q3: Describe the punishments that could be given to vagrants under the 1572 Vagabond Act. (4 marks)

Answer: Under the 1572 Act: first offence - public whipping and burning through the right ear with a hot iron; second offence - imprisonment or deportation; third offence - execution as a felon. Vagrants were also to be returned to their home parish. The punishments were deliberately harsh and public to deter others.

Q4: How did the Elizabethan government's approach to poverty change between 1572 and 1601? (8 marks)

Answer: The government's approach changed from primarily punitive to more constructive: the 1572 Act focused on harsh punishment of vagrants (whipping, ear-burning, execution); the 1576 Act shifted towards providing work for the able-bodied poor and establishing Houses of Correction; the 1597 Act appointed parish overseers and organised systematic relief; the 1601 Act consolidated all previous legislation, maintaining punishment for vagrants but also ensuring a comprehensive system of relief for the deserving poor. The overall trend was from punishment towards a more balanced approach combining punishment with relief.

Q5: "The Elizabethan government was more concerned with punishing vagrants than helping the poor." How far do you agree? (16 marks + 4 SPaG)

Answer: A balanced answer should discuss: the punitive approach (the 1572 Act's extreme punishments for vagrants - whipping, ear-burning, execution; the harsh treatment of the "undeserving poor"; the fact that punishments did not address the causes of poverty); the helpful approach (the poor rate providing funding for relief; the 1576 Act providing work; the 1601 Act creating a comprehensive system of parish relief for the deserving poor). Argue that the government was initially more concerned with punishment but gradually developed a more balanced approach - though the underlying assumption that poverty was a moral problem rather than an economic one remained throughout the period.

Q6: Explain why the 1601 Poor Law was significant. (4 marks)

Answer: The 1601 Poor Law was significant because: it consolidated all previous poor law legislation into a single comprehensive statute; it established the principle of parish responsibility for the poor; it created a compulsory poor rate funded by local property owners; it provided relief for the deserving poor, work for the able-bodied, and apprenticeships for poor children; it remained the basis of the English poor law system for over 230 years until the 1834 Poor Law Amendment Act.
