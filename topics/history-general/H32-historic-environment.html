Lesson 2: Core Concepts: the historic environment

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on the historic environment. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Context: The Historic Environment is a compulsory part of the Elizabethan England GCSE paper. Each year, the exam board specifies a particular Elizabethan site that students must study. The site changes every year, so you need to check which site is specified for your exam year. The Historic Environment question tests your ability to connect a physical place to the broader themes of Elizabethan history - understanding not just what a building looked like, but why it was built, what it tells us about Elizabethan society, and how it connects to the wider events of the period.
Prodigy Houses: The Elizabethan era saw the construction of enormous, lavishly decorated houses by wealthy courtiers. These were called "prodigy houses" because of their extraordinary size and magnificence. They were built partly as family homes, but also as venues to entertain the Queen during her annual "progresses" (royal tours around the country). The ability to host the Queen was a tremendous status symbol and a way for courtiers to demonstrate their loyalty and wealth.
Key Principle: Elizabethan buildings were never just functional - they were statements. A great house declared its owner's wealth and status. A church reflected the Religious Settlement. A castle demonstrated the government's commitment to defence. A House of Correction proclaimed the authorities' determination to control vagrancy. When writing about any site, always ask: What message was this building designed to send?
Theme 1: Religion: How does the site reflect the Religious Settlement of 1559? Church buildings show the shift from Catholic to Protestant worship: the removal of rood screens, statues, and wall paintings; the introduction of communion tables instead of altars; the use of English in services; the placement of the pulpit as the focal point (emphasising preaching). A site like a parish church or cathedral can demonstrate both the changes and the continuities in Elizabethan religious life.
Theme 2: Government and Power: How does the site reflect the structure of Elizabethan government? Great houses of courtiers like Cecil (Burghley House) or Walsingham show the wealth and influence of Elizabeth's key advisors. County buildings reflect the role of the gentry as JPs and local administrators. Prisons and Houses of Correction reflect the government's approach to law and order.
Theme 3: Wealth and Social Structure: How does the site reflect the distribution of wealth and the social hierarchy? The contrast between a great prodigy house and a poor labourer's cottage shows the enormous gap between rich and poor. The interior layout of great houses (with servants' quarters below stairs and grand rooms above) reflects the rigid social hierarchy.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: What are the three most important questions to ask about any Elizabethan building? (3 marks)

Answer: The three most important questions to ask about any Elizabethan building are: (1) Who built it and why? - Understanding the patron's identity, wealth, and motivations; (2) What does it tell us about Elizabethan society? - How the building reflects the values, wealth, and priorities of the period; (3) How does it connect to wider historical events? - Linking the building to themes like religion, government, wealth, defence, or culture.

Lesson 3: Application: the historic environment

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Context, Prodigy Houses, Key Principle. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What are the three most important questions to ask about any Elizabethan building? (3 marks)

Answer: The three most important questions to ask about any Elizabethan building are: (1) Who built it and why? - Understanding the patron's identity, wealth, and motivations; (2) What does it tell us about Elizabethan society? - How the building reflects the values, wealth, and priorities of the period; (3) How does it connect to wider historical events? - Linking the building to themes like religion, government, wealth, defence, or culture.

Q2: Explain how a great house like Hardwick Hall reflects the wealth of Elizabethan courtiers. (4 marks)

Answer: Hardwick Hall reflects the wealth of Elizabethan courtiers through: its enormous glass windows, when glass was a luxury material ("Hardwick Hall, more glass than wall"); its vast size and six towers, requiring enormous expenditure (approximately £30,000); its expensive tapestries and furnishings; the fact that it was built by Bess of Hardwick, who accumulated vast wealth through four advantageous marriages and royal favour.

Q3: How does the design of an Elizabethan parish church reflect the Religious Settlement of 1559? (4 marks)

Answer: An Elizabethan parish church reflects the Religious Settlement through: the removal of Catholic features (rood screens, statues, wall paintings); the introduction of a communion table instead of a high altar (reflecting Protestant rejection of transubstantiation); the prominence of the pulpit (emphasising preaching, a Protestant priority); the use of English in services (not Latin); the plain design of the interior (rejecting Catholic ornamentation). However, some churches retained more traditional features, reflecting the Settlement's compromise approach.

Q4: Explain the significance of the Globe Theatre in relation to Elizabethan culture. (8 marks)

Answer: The Globe Theatre is significant in relation to Elizabethan culture because: it was one of the first purpose-built theatres in England, reflecting the cultural flourishing of the Elizabethan era; it hosted the plays of Shakespeare and other leading dramatists, representing the peak of English Renaissance drama; it brought together all social classes (groundlings and gentry), showing the social function of theatre; its location outside the City walls reflects the Puritan opposition to entertainment; its success demonstrated the commercial viability of professional theatre. The Globe is a physical embodiment of Elizabethan cultural achievement.

Q5: "Elizabethan buildings were primarily designed to display wealth and status." How far do you agree? (16 marks + 4 SPaG)

Answer: A balanced answer should discuss: buildings designed to display wealth and status (great houses like Hardwick Hall and Burghley House, with their enormous windows, grand facades, and luxurious interiors); buildings with other primary purposes: churches designed for worship according to the Religious Settlement; defensive structures built for protection against invasion; theatres built for entertainment; Houses of Correction built for social control; markets and exchanges built for trade. Argue that while wealth and status were important motivations for some buildings, others served practical, religious, or defensive purposes. The variety of Elizabethan buildings reflects the complexity and diversity of the society that created them.

Q6: Explain how coastal defences like Pendennis Castle connect to the wider threat from Spain during Elizabeth's reign. (4 marks)

Answer: Coastal defences like Pendennis Castle connect to the Spanish threat because: they were originally built by Henry VIII as part of the "Device Forts" to protect against invasion; Elizabeth maintained and updated them in response to the growing threat from Spain after the 1570 excommunication and the Dutch Revolt; they formed part of the chain of coastal defences that would warn of the Armada's approach in 1588 (beacons were lit on headlands near such forts); they demonstrated the government's commitment to defending the realm against Catholic invasion; they were garrisoned and armed during the Armada crisis. The forts are physical evidence of the real and present danger that Spain posed to Elizabethan England.
