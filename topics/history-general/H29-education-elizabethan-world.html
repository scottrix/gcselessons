Lesson 2: Core Concepts: education and the elizabethan world

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on education and the elizabethan world. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Context: The Elizabethan era saw a significant expansion in education, driven by the Renaissance emphasis on learning and the growing need for educated administrators, lawyers, and clergy. However, education was still limited by class and gender: most children received no formal education, and opportunities for girls were extremely restricted.
The Purpose of Education: Education in Elizabethan England was not about developing the individual - it was about producing trained men who could serve the state as administrators, diplomats, lawyers, and clergy. A knowledge of Latin was essential for all official and academic communication. The emphasis on classical learning meant that students were immersed in the values and ideas of ancient Rome and Greece, which shaped their worldview and political thinking.
The Irony of Elizabeth's Reign: Elizabeth I was the most educated woman in England and one of the most learned monarchs in European history. She ruled successfully for 45 years, proving that women were capable of intellectual and political leadership. Yet she did little to improve educational opportunities for other women. Her reign is often described as a paradox: a female ruler who upheld patriarchal values and did not challenge the subordination of women.
The Gentry and Elizabeth: The gentry were crucial supporters of Elizabeth's government. They served as JPs (unpaid local magistrates who maintained law and order), MPs in Parliament, and commissioners for collecting taxes and enforcing the Religious Settlement. In return, Elizabeth gave them status, influence, and the opportunity to profit from their positions. The partnership between the Crown and the gentry was the foundation of Elizabethan government.
Puritan Campaigns Against Theatre: In 1572, the Puritan-influenced London Common Council banned plays within the City of London, forcing theatres to build outside the City's boundaries (in Southwark and Shoreditch). In 1574, the City banned all plays within its jurisdiction. In 1576, the first theatre was built just outside the City walls. Puritans in Parliament repeatedly tried to ban theatre entirely, though they were unsuccessful during Elizabeth's reign. After the Civil War in 1642, the Puritan government finally closed all theatres.

School hours were long (6am-5pm), discipline was harsh (beatings with a birch rod), and all teaching was in Latin. Holidays were limited, and boys were punished for speaking English in school.

Lesson 3: Application: education and the elizabethan world

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Context, The Purpose of Education, The Irony of Elizabeth&#x27;s Reign. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Describe the education a boy would receive at a grammar school in Elizabethan England. (4 marks)

Answer: At a grammar school, a boy would study Latin grammar (using Lily's Grammar), read classical authors like Cicero and Virgil, study Greek, rhetoric, and logic. School hours were long (6am-5pm), discipline was harsh (beatings with a birch rod), and all teaching was in Latin. Holidays were limited, and boys were punished for speaking English in school.

Q2: Explain why education for girls was so limited in Elizabethan England. (4 marks)

Answer: Education for girls was limited because: Elizabethan society was patriarchal and believed a woman's role was domestic; girls were expected to become wives and mothers, not professionals; the Bible was used to justify female subordination; girls were not admitted to grammar schools or universities; even wealthy girls were taught only reading, writing, music, needlework, and household management; no career required women to be educated.

Q3: Why did the Elizabethan theatre flourish? (8 marks)

Answer: The Elizabethan theatre flourished because: the relative stability of Elizabeth's reign allowed culture to develop; London's population grew rapidly to over 200,000 by 1600, providing a large audience; the Renaissance inspired new forms of drama; patronage by noblemen supported theatrical companies (the Lord Chamberlain's Men, the Lord Admiral's Men); purpose-built theatres like The Theatre (1576) and The Globe (1599) provided permanent venues; talented playwrights like Shakespeare and Marlowe wrote compelling plays; theatre attracted all social classes.

Q4: How far did Puritan opposition threaten the development of Elizabethan theatre? (8 marks)

Answer: Puritan opposition threatened theatre by: persuading the London Common Council to ban plays within the City in 1572 and 1574; repeatedly attempting to ban theatre in Parliament; opposing performances on Sundays; condemning cross-dressing (boys playing women); associating theatres with crime and the plague. However, theatre survived because: Elizabeth and her government did not support the Puritan campaign; theatres were built outside the City's jurisdiction; noble patronage protected theatrical companies; the public demand for entertainment was strong; the Puritans were a minority, however vocal.

Q5: "Education was the most important factor in the rise of the gentry." How far do you agree? (16 marks + 4 SPaG)

Answer: A balanced answer should discuss: education (grammar schools and universities provided the gentry with the skills to serve as JPs, MPs, and administrators); economic factors (rising land values, the dissolution of the monasteries, and trade enriched gentry families); political factors (the Tudor reliance on the gentry for local government gave them power and influence); social mobility (merchants and lawyers could buy land and join the gentry). Argue that education was important but not the only factor - economic and political changes were equally significant.

Q6: Explain the role of the gentry in Elizabethan government. (4 marks)

Answer: The gentry served as: Justices of the Peace (unpaid local magistrates who maintained law and order, handled minor crimes, and enforced the Religious Settlement); Members of Parliament in the House of Commons; commissioners for collecting taxes and mustering troops; sheriffs and other local officials. They were the essential link between Elizabeth's central government and the localities, and without them the Tudor system of government could not have functioned.
