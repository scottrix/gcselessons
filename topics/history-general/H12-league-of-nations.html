Lesson 2: Core Concepts: the league of nations

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on the league of nations. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: The League of Nations was created as part of the Treaty of Versailles in June 1919. It was Woodrow Wilson's idea - his Fourteenth Point called for "a general association of nations." The League's headquarters were in Geneva, Switzerland. Its first meeting was held on 15 November 1920 with 42 member states.
Key Fact: The League's Covenant (its founding document) set out its key principles: collective security, arbitration of disputes, disarmament, and improved living and working conditions worldwide. Article 10 of the Covenant committed all members to "respect and preserve" each other's territorial integrity.
Key Fact: The Council was the most powerful body. It had permanent members (Britain, France, Italy, Japan) who could veto decisions. However, ALL Council decisions had to be UNANIMOUS - any member could veto action. This meant the League was often paralysed by disagreement. A single "no" vote could prevent the League from acting against an aggressor.
Key Fact: The League's membership was fundamentally flawed from the start. The USA never joined; Germany was excluded until 1926; the USSR was excluded until 1934; and Japan and Italy left in the 1930s. No organisation can maintain world peace without the world's most powerful nations.
Key Fact: The League was more successful in the 1920s than is often remembered. It resolved several international disputes peacefully and carried out important humanitarian work. The problems of the 1930s should not overshadow the genuine achievements of the 1920s.
Key Fact: Fridtjof Nansen, the League's High Commissioner for Refugees, won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1922 for his work. The "Nansen Passport" he created provided identity documents for stateless refugees and was recognised by over 50 countries. The ILO won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1969 for its decades of work improving labour conditions.

Lesson 3: Application: the league of nations

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: key terms from the league of nations. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain two weaknesses of the League of Nations. (4 marks)

Answer: The League had no army of its own - it could not enforce decisions militarily and had to rely on member states to provide troops, who often refused. The unanimity rule meant that any Council member could veto action, so a single "no" vote could prevent the League from responding to aggression.

Q2: How important was America's absence from the League of Nations? (8 marks)

Answer: America's absence was critically important. Without the USA, the League lacked the world's most powerful economy and military. Economic sanctions were weaker without American trade; other powers knew the League could not call on American support; and the League's moral authority was undermined. However, even with America, the League might still have struggled: the unanimity rule would still have applied; national self-interest would still have undermined collective security. America's absence was devastating but not the only weakness.

Q3: "The League of Nations was a complete failure." How far do you agree? (12 marks)

Answer: The League was NOT a complete failure. In the 1920s, it successfully resolved disputes (Aaland Islands 1921, Greece-Bulgaria 1925, Upper Silesia 1921). Its humanitarian work was genuinely impressive: Nansen helped 425,000 refugees; the ILO improved working conditions; the Health Organisation combatted disease; and 200,000 slaves were freed. However, the League failed catastrophically in the 1930s: it could not stop Japan in Manchuria (1931) or Italy in Abyssinia (1935); it failed on disarmament; and it collapsed as a peacekeeping body. It was not a "complete" failure, but it failed in its primary purpose of preventing war.

Q4: Explain why the League was more successful in the 1920s than the 1930s. (8 marks)

Answer: The League was more successful in the 1920s because: the international climate was more cooperative (Locarno Spring); the disputes involved small nations who could not defy the League; the major powers supported League decisions because it suited their interests; and the economic prosperity reduced tensions. In the 1930s, the Great Depression made nations more aggressive and self-interested; the disputes involved major powers who could defy the League; the rise of dictatorships undermined the cooperative spirit; and the League's weaknesses were exposed by serious crises.

Q5: "The League's humanitarian work was its greatest achievement." How far do you agree? (16 marks)

Answer: The humanitarian work was certainly impressive: the Health Organisation, ILO, Nansen's refugee work, anti-slavery campaigns, and drug control all achieved real results that outlasted the League itself. The ILO still exists today as a UN agency. However, the League's PRIMARY purpose was to prevent war through collective security - and in this it failed completely. If the League's greatest achievement was humanitarian rather than peacekeeping, that demonstrates how far it fell short of Wilson's vision. The humanitarian work was important but secondary to the League's core mission.
