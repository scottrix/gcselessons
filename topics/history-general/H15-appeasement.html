Lesson 2: Core Concepts: appeasement

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on appeasement. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Appeasement was the policy of giving in to some of Hitler's demands in the hope of avoiding war. It was most associated with British Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain (1937-1940), though it had been practised by earlier governments. The policy was based on the belief that some of Germany's grievances were legitimate and that negotiated concessions could satisfy Hitler and prevent another devastating war.
Key Fact: Appeasement was not simply cowardice or stupidity. It was a rational policy based on genuine concerns: war weariness, military unpreparedness, economic problems, fear of communism, and the belief that some German grievances were legitimate.
Key Fact: Neville Chamberlain became Prime Minister in May 1937 and made appeasement the cornerstone of British foreign policy. He believed that personal diplomacy could solve international problems and conducted his own negotiations with Hitler, bypassing the Foreign Office.
Key Fact: The strongest argument for appeasement was that it bought time for rearmament. In 1938, Britain had only 5 radar stations and very few Spitfires. By September 1939, the radar chain was complete and the RAF had over 300 Spitfires and Hurricanes. However, Germany also used the time to rearm, so the balance of power may not have changed significantly in Britain's favour.
Key Fact: Appeasement ended on 31 March 1939 when Chamberlain guaranteed Poland's independence, promising that Britain would defend Poland if Germany attacked. This was a direct response to Hitler's seizure of Czechoslovakia on 15 March 1939, which proved that his aims went beyond self-determination and that he could not be trusted.

Lesson 3: Application: appeasement

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: History Exam Tips — Appeasement. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain two reasons why Britain followed a policy of appeasement. (4 marks)

Answer: War weariness: the memory of WWI (750,000 British dead) made the public and politicians desperate to avoid another war. Military unpreparedness: Britain's armed forces were not ready for war in 1938 - the RAF needed expansion and the army was too small for continental warfare. Appeasement bought time for rearmament.

Q2: How far was appeasement justified? (8 marks)

Answer: Appeasement was partially justified in 1938: Britain was militarily unprepared; the RAF needed time to develop radar and Spitfires; public opinion strongly opposed war; and some German grievances (especially self-determination for Sudeten Germans) appeared legitimate. However, it was also misguided: it encouraged Hitler by showing that aggression would not be resisted; it abandoned Czechoslovakia, a valuable ally; it was based on trusting Hitler, who proved untrustworthy; and it failed to recognise that Hitler's aims went far beyond Versailles revision. With hindsight, appeasement was a mistake, but the alternatives in 1938 were limited.

Q3: "Appeasement was cowardly and misguided." How far do you agree? (12 marks)

Answer: Appeasement was not cowardly - it was a rational policy based on genuine concerns: avoiding another catastrophic war, military unpreparedness, and the belief that some German grievances were legitimate. Chamberlain was brave enough to fly to Germany three times to negotiate. However, it was misguided because it fundamentally misjudged Hitler. It assumed he was a reasonable statesman who could be satisfied by concessions, when in fact he was determined on conquest. Each concession encouraged further demands. The abandonment of Czechoslovakia at Munich was particularly misguided, as it gave Hitler the Czech army's weapons. Appeasement was understandable but based on a fatal miscalculation.

Q4: Explain why appeasement was abandoned in March 1939. (8 marks)

Answer: Appeasement was abandoned because Hitler's seizure of Czechoslovakia (15 March 1939) proved that his aims were conquest, not self-determination. He had broken his Munich promise that the Sudetenland was his "last territorial demand." The British public and Parliament now saw Hitler as an untrustworthy aggressor. The seizure of Memel and Italy's invasion of Albania reinforced the threat. Chamberlain issued the guarantee to Poland (31 March 1939) as a clear warning that further aggression would mean war. Appeasement had failed because Hitler could not be trusted to keep his promises.

Q5: "Chamberlain was wrong to pursue appeasement." How far do you agree? (16 marks)

Answer: Chamberlain was wrong to pursue appeasement in the sense that it misjudged Hitler's character and aims. He trusted a dictator who had already shown his ruthlessness (Night of the Long Knives, persecution of Jews). Each concession made Hitler stronger and more confident. The Munich Agreement abandoned a democratic ally and gave Germany Czech military assets. However, Chamberlain was right that Britain needed time to rearm - the RAF was significantly stronger in September 1939 than in September 1938. He also reflected genuine public opinion, which was overwhelmingly anti-war. The real error was not appeasement itself but failing to recognise when to stop - after the Rhineland (1936) or certainly after Czechoslovakia (March 1939), it should have been clear that Hitler could not be satisfied.
