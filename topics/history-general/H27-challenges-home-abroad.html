Lesson 2: Core Concepts: challenges at home and abroad

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on challenges at home and abroad. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Context: After Pope Pius V excommunicated Elizabeth in 1570, Catholic plots to overthrow her intensified. Mary Queen of Scots, Elizabeth's Catholic cousin, was the focal point of every conspiracy - she was the obvious Catholic alternative to Elizabeth. Elizabeth's survival depended on her spy network, led by Francis Walsingham, and her ability to outmanoeuvre the plotters without provoking an international crisis.
Significance of the Revolt: The Revolt of the Northern Earls was the only armed Catholic uprising of Elizabeth's reign. It showed that Catholic nobles in the north were willing to use force, but it also demonstrated the limits of their support. Most ordinary people, even Catholics, were unwilling to risk their lives for the cause. The revolt's failure strengthened Elizabeth's position and led to harsher measures against Catholics.
Walsingham's Spy Network: The Ridolfi Plot was discovered through the intelligence network of Francis Walsingham, Elizabeth's "spymaster." Walsingham (1532-1590) built an extensive network of agents and informers across England and Europe. He intercepted letters, employed double agents, and used codes and ciphers to gather intelligence. His network was so effective that he often knew of Catholic plots before they were fully formed. Walsingham's espionage was crucial to Elizabeth's survival throughout her reign.
Phelippes' Forgery: When Thomas Phelippes decoded Mary's letter of 17 July 1586, he added a postscript in Mary's cipher requesting the names of the conspirators. This was designed to extract more evidence, but the conspirators were arrested before they could respond. The fact that Walsingham's team forged part of the letter has led some historians to question whether the evidence was entirely fair - but Mary's approval of the assassination plot was genuinely in her own hand.
Significance of Mary's Execution: Mary's execution removed the figurehead of Catholic opposition, but it also gave Philip II of Spain the justification he needed to launch the Spanish Armada. Philip had claimed to be acting in Mary's interests; now he could claim to be avenging her death. Mary also became a Catholic martyr, and her execution hardened attitudes on both sides. However, the immediate threat of a Catholic coup was ended - there was no longer an obvious Catholic candidate to replace Elizabeth.
Escalating Threat: Each plot was more dangerous than the last. The Northern Earls' Revolt was a local uprising; the Ridolfi Plot involved a foreign invasion plan; the Throckmorton Plot had the backing of multiple Catholic powers; the Babington Plot directly targeted Elizabeth's life. The escalation shows why Elizabeth's government became increasingly ruthless in dealing with Catholics - the threat was real and growing.

Lesson 3: Application: challenges at home and abroad

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Context, Significance of the Revolt, Walsingham&#x27;s Spy Network. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Describe the Revolt of the Northern Earls in 1569. (4 marks)

Answer: In November 1569, the Earls of Northumberland (Thomas Percy) and Westmorland (Charles Neville) gathered approximately 4,500 armed men and marched into Durham, where they celebrated a Catholic Mass in the Cathedral. They planned to rescue Mary Queen of Scots and proclaim her queen. However, Elizabeth moved Mary further south, and the royal army of 7,000 men under the Earl of Sussex dispersed the rebellion. Over 800 rebels were executed afterwards.

Q2: Explain how Walsingham's spy network uncovered the Babington Plot. (4 marks)

Answer: Walsingham uncovered the Babington Plot by: infiltrating the conspiracy with a double agent (Gilbert Gifford); intercepting Mary's letters, which were smuggled in beer barrels; having his secretary Thomas Phelippes decode the letters using cipher; waiting until Mary wrote back approving the assassination of Elizabeth on 17 July 1586. This written evidence proved Mary's direct involvement in treason and provided the justification for her execution.

Q3: Why did Elizabeth hesitate before signing Mary Queen of Scots' death warrant? (4 marks)

Answer: Elizabeth hesitated because: executing a fellow monarch was unprecedented and set a dangerous precedent; Mary was her cousin; she feared provoking an invasion by Catholic powers (especially Spain); she worried about the reaction of Mary's son, James VI of Scotland; she was uncertain about the morality of the execution. She delayed for months after the trial verdict before finally signing the death warrant.

Q4: How serious was the Catholic threat to Elizabeth's reign? (8 marks)

Answer: The Catholic threat was very serious: there were four major plots between 1569 and 1586, each escalating in ambition; the plots had backing from major Catholic powers (Spain, France, the Pope); Mary Queen of Scots provided a focal point for opposition; the 1570 excommunication made Catholic loyalty incompatible with loyalty to Elizabeth; the Babington Plot directly targeted Elizabeth's life. However, the threat was limited by: lack of popular support among ordinary English people; Walsingham's effective intelligence network; the disunity among Catholic powers; and the failure of every plot to achieve its aims.

Q5: "Walsingham's spy network was the most important reason Elizabeth survived the Catholic plots." How far do you agree? (16 marks + 4 SPaG)

Answer: A balanced answer should discuss: Walsingham's spy network (essential for uncovering all four plots, especially the Babington Plot where he set a trap); other factors including: Elizabeth's political skill and caution; the loyalty of most English people to the Protestant settlement; the weakness and disunity of Catholic powers; the Bond of Association and parliamentary legislation; the failure of Catholic plots to gain popular support. Argue that Walsingham was essential but not sufficient alone - Elizabeth's survival depended on a combination of intelligence, political skill, and the underlying weakness of the Catholic cause in England.

Q6: Compare the Ridolfi Plot and the Babington Plot. In what ways were they similar and different? (8 marks)

Answer: Similarities: both plots aimed to replace Elizabeth with Mary Queen of Scots; both had foreign Catholic backing; both were discovered by Walsingham's network; both led to executions of the key plotters. Differences: the Ridolfi Plot involved the Duke of Norfolk (the highest-ranking nobleman) and planned a Spanish invasion; the Babington Plot directly targeted Elizabeth's assassination and used Walsingham's deliberate trap to catch Mary in her own handwriting; the Babington Plot had far more serious consequences (Mary's execution).

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on challenges at home and abroad from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Know all four plots in chronological order: 1569, 1571, 1583, 1586 | Always use specific dates and named individuals | Explain Walsingham's methods in detail - the Babington Plot trap is a very common question | For comparison questions, structure around similarities AND differences | Link the plots together - show how they escalated and how Elizabeth's response intensified | Remember the significance of Mary's execution: it removed the threat but provoked the Armada | For evaluation questions, balance the danger of the plots against their failure
