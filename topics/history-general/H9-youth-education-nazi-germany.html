Lesson 2: Core Concepts: youth and education in nazi germany

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on youth and education in nazi germany. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: The Hitler Youth was made compulsory by law on 1 December 1936. By 1939, over 8.7 million young people (over 90% of eligible youth) were members. The organisation aimed to indoctrinate young Germans with Nazi ideology and prepare boys for military service.
Key Fact: The Bund Deutscher Mädel (BDM) was the girls' equivalent of the Hitler Youth. By 1939, it had over 2 million members. The BDM focused on physical fitness, domestic skills, and motherhood training - preparing girls to be healthy mothers for the Aryan race.
Key Fact: The BDM emphasised that a woman's highest duty was to bear healthy Aryan children. Members were taught that education was less important than physical fitness and motherhood. The BDM motto was: "Be faithful, be pure, be German." Members who became pregnant out of wedlock were not punished - the Nazis valued Aryan births regardless of marital status.
Key Fact: The Nazis took complete control of the education system. All teachers had to join the Nazi Teachers' League (NSLB) - by 1936, 97% had joined. The curriculum was rewritten to reflect Nazi ideology, and textbooks were replaced with racially "correct" versions.
Key Fact: Not all young people accepted Nazi indoctrination. Several youth groups resisted Nazi control, including the Edelweiss Pirates, Swing Youth, and the White Rose group. These groups represented different forms of opposition, from non-conformity to active resistance.
Key Judgement: The Nazi youth programme was partially successful. Many young people were genuinely indoctrinated and became committed Nazis. The 12th SS Panzer Division "Hitlerjugend," formed from Hitler Youth members, fought fiercely in 1944. However, compulsion was necessary because not all youth wanted to join. Truancy rates increased, and resistance groups showed that some young people rejected Nazi values entirely. The programme was most effective on younger children who knew no other system.

Lesson 3: Application: youth and education in nazi germany

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Judgement. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain two ways the Nazis controlled education. (4 marks)

Answer: Teachers were forced to join the Nazi Teachers' League (97% by 1936) or lose their jobs, ensuring only ideologically reliable teachers remained. The curriculum was rewritten to promote Nazi ideology - biology taught racial theory, history taught the "stab-in-the-back" myth, and PE became 15% of the timetable to build military fitness.

Q2: How far did the Hitler Youth succeed in indoctrinating young Germans? (8 marks)

Answer: The Hitler Youth partially succeeded. Over 90% of eligible youth were members by 1939, and many were genuinely committed to Nazi ideals. The 12th SS Panzer Division was formed from Hitler Youth members. However, membership was compulsory from 1936, meaning many joined because they had to, not because they wanted to. Truancy rates increased from 1 million (1936) to 3 million (1938). Resistance groups like the Edelweiss Pirates showed some youth actively rejected Nazism. The programme was most effective on those too young to remember alternatives.

Q3: "Youth resistance in Nazi Germany was ineffective." How far do you agree? (12 marks)

Answer: Youth resistance was limited in its impact but showed that Nazi control was never total. The Edelweiss Pirates rejected Hitler Youth discipline and some engaged in sabotage, but they were not a political movement and were brutally suppressed (12 hanged in Cologne 1944). The Swing Youth was cultural resistance, not political. The White Rose was the most serious resistance but was quickly crushed - Sophie and Hans Scholl were executed in February 1943. All resistance failed to change Nazi policy significantly. However, the existence of resistance proved that not all young people accepted indoctrination, and the White Rose leaflets provided evidence of moral courage that inspired post-war Germans.

Q4: Explain the differences between the Hitler Youth and the League of German Maidens. (8 marks)

Answer: The Hitler Youth prepared boys for military service: physical training, weapons drill, marching, and political indoctrination about German greatness. The BDM prepared girls for motherhood: domestic skills, health and hygiene, childcare training, and physical fitness to produce healthy Aryan children. Both organisations promoted Nazi ideology and racial theory, but their practical activities were entirely different - boys were trained to fight, girls were trained to breed. The BDM had no military training equivalent; the Hitler Youth had no domestic skills equivalent.

Q5: "The Nazis were more successful in controlling young people through schools than through youth organisations." How far do you agree? (16 marks)

Answer: Schools and youth organisations were both important but worked in different ways. Schools had a captive audience (all children had to attend) and could embed Nazi ideology across every subject - racial theory in biology, Versailles injustice in history, military maths. However, school was limited to lesson time. The Hitler Youth controlled leisure time - evenings, weekends, and holidays - providing a total immersion in Nazi culture. The youth organisations were arguably more effective because they created emotional loyalty through comradeship, camping, and a sense of belonging. However, increasing truancy from Hitler Youth meetings showed its limits. Schools could compel attendance more easily but produced intellectual conformity rather than passionate commitment. Both were necessary for the Nazis' total control of youth.
