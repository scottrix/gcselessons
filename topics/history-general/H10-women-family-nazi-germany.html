Lesson 2: Core Concepts: women and the family in nazi germany

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on women and the family in nazi germany. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: The Nazi ideal for women was summed up as the "Three Ks" - Kinder, Küche, Kirche (Children, Kitchen, Church). Hitler believed that a woman's proper place was in the home, producing Aryan children and supporting her husband. He said: "The woman is the eternal companion of the man, and her world is the family."
Key Fact: In June 1933, the Law for the Encouragement of Marriage offered marriage loans of 1,000 marks to young couples if the wife gave up her job. For each child born, 25% of the loan was cancelled. If a couple had four children, the entire loan was wiped out.
Key Fact: The Nazis introduced several policies to push women out of professional employment. In 1933, women in the civil service, medicine, and teaching were dismissed or encouraged to resign. By 1936, women were barred from legal professions and from being judges. The number of women at university was limited to 10% of enrolments.
Key Fact: Despite Nazi ideology, the number of women in employment actually INCREASED during the 1930s. By 1937, there were more women in work than when Hitler came to power. The reality was that Germany's economic recovery and rearmament programme needed workers, and many families needed a second income. The gap between Nazi ideology and economic reality was significant.
Key Fact: The Nazi birth rate policy had mixed results. The birth rate rose from 14.7 per 1,000 in 1933 to 19.0 per 1,000 in 1939 - a significant increase. However, it never returned to Weimar levels (23.2 per 1,000 in 1920), and by 1939 it was already starting to decline again.
Key Fact: The Law for the Prevention of Hereditarily Diseased Offspring (July 1933) allowed the forced sterilisation of women with supposed "hereditary illnesses" including schizophrenia, epilepsy, "feeble-mindedness," and alcoholism. By 1939, approximately 320,000 people had been forcibly sterilised - the majority were women.

Lesson 3: Application: women and the family in nazi germany

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Judgement. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain two ways the Nazis encouraged women to have more children. (4 marks)

Answer: Marriage loans (June 1933): offered 1,000 marks to couples if the wife gave up work; 25% of the loan was cancelled for each child born, so four children meant the entire loan was wiped out. The Mother's Cross (August 1938): awarded to mothers who had many children - bronze for 4+, silver for 6+, gold for 8+ children - giving social status and privileges to mothers.

Q2: How far did Nazi policies improve the lives of German women? (8 marks)

Answer: Nazi policies improved life for some women: marriage loans provided financial help, the Mother's Cross gave recognition to mothers, and family allowances supported larger families. However, many women lost professional careers and educational opportunities; women were excluded from politics and decision-making; abortion was banned and contraception restricted; and 320,000 women were forcibly sterilised. The policies only benefited women who accepted the Nazi ideal of motherhood and domesticity. Professional women, single women, and "racially impure" women suffered under Nazi rule.

Q3: "The Nazis failed to control women's employment." How far do you agree? (12 marks)

Answer: The Nazis did fail to control women's employment in practice. Despite policies to remove women from work (dismissal from civil service, marriage loans requiring women to leave jobs), the number of women in employment actually increased from 11.5 million (1933) to 12.7 million (1939). Economic recovery and rearmament created demand for labour that could not be met by men alone. By 1937, there were more women in work than in 1933. The war made this failure complete - by 1943, 14.8 million women were employed. However, the Nazis did succeed in reducing women in professional and senior positions, and they controlled the narrative about women's "proper" role. Their failure was in economic reality, not in propaganda.

Q4: Explain how the war changed women's roles in Nazi Germany. (8 marks)

Answer: The war dramatically changed women's roles. With millions of men conscripted, women were needed in factories, farms, and offices. By 1943, 52% of the workforce was female. From 1943, women were conscripted for war work. The Nazis had to abandon their ideology that women belonged in the home. Women also faced new challenges: managing households on reduced rations, surviving Allied bombing, and coping with family separation. By 1944-45, millions of women became refugees fleeing the Soviet advance. The war showed that Nazi ideology was incompatible with economic necessity.

Q5: "Nazi policies towards women were contradictory." How far do you agree? (16 marks)

Answer: Nazi policies were deeply contradictory. The Nazis preached that women should stay at home (the Three Ks), yet the economy needed women to work. They dismissed women from professions, yet by 1939 more women were employed than in 1933. They promoted motherhood, yet the birth rate never returned to Weimar levels and was declining by 1939. They honoured mothers with the Mother's Cross, yet forced 320,000 "unfit" women to be sterilised. The biggest contradiction was during the war: after years of telling women to stay home, the Nazis suddenly demanded they work in factories. These contradictions showed that Nazi ideology (women in the home) clashed with economic reality (the state needed women workers). The ideology was never abandoned in principle but was repeatedly violated in practice.
