Lesson 2: Core Concepts: nazi racial policy and the holocaust

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on nazi racial policy and the holocaust. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: The Nazis believed in a racial hierarchy with the "Aryan" race (tall, blonde, blue-eyed Germans and Nordics) at the top as the "master race" (Herrenvolk). At the bottom were "sub-humans" (Untermenschen) - Jews, Roma, Slavs, and black people. Hitler wrote in Mein Kampf (1925): "The Jew is and remains the eternal enemy of the Aryan."
Key Fact: Persecution of Jews began immediately after Hitler became Chancellor. On 1 April 1933, the Nazis organised a nationwide boycott of Jewish shops and businesses. SA men stood outside Jewish shops with signs reading "Germans! Defend yourselves! Don't buy from Jews!"
Key Fact: At the Nuremberg Rally on 15 September 1935, Hitler announced two laws that fundamentally changed the legal status of German Jews: the Reich Citizenship Law and the Law for the Protection of German Blood and Honour. These laws stripped Jews of citizenship and banned marriages between Jews and Germans.
Key Fact: The Nuremberg Laws were a turning point because they made anti-Semitism legal and official. Before 1935, persecution had been through SA violence and local regulations; after 1935, it was enshrined in national law. This gave persecution the authority of the state and made it systematic.
Key Fact: On the night of 9-10 November 1938, Nazi mobs attacked Jewish property across Germany in a organised pogrom called Kristallnacht ("Night of Broken Glass"). 191 synagogues were set on fire, over 7,000 Jewish shops were destroyed, 91 Jews were killed, and 20,000-30,000 Jewish men were arrested and sent to concentration camps.
Key Fact: After Germany invaded Poland in September 1939, 2 million more Jews came under Nazi control. The Nazis forced Polish Jews into sealed, overcrowded areas called ghettos. The largest was the Warsaw Ghetto, where over 400,000 Jews were confined in an area of just 3.4 square km.

Lesson 3: Application: nazi racial policy and the holocaust

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: key terms from nazi racial policy and the holocaust. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain how the Nuremberg Laws affected the lives of German Jews. (4 marks)

Answer: The Nuremberg Laws (September 1935) stripped Jews of German citizenship, reducing them to "state subjects" with no political rights. They banned marriages and sexual relationships between Jews and Germans, and forbade Jewish households from employing German women under 45. The laws made anti-Semitism official state policy, moving persecution from SA violence to systematic legal discrimination.

Q2: Why was Kristallnacht a significant turning point? (8 marks)

Answer: Kristallnacht (9-10 November 1938) was a turning point because it marked the shift from legal discrimination to open, organised violence. 191 synagogues were burned, over 7,000 Jewish businesses destroyed, 91 Jews killed, and 20,000-30,000 men sent to concentration camps. The Jewish community was fined 1 billion reichsmarks for the damage. It showed that there were no limits to Nazi persecution and convinced many Jews they had no future in Germany. After Kristallnacht, emigration accelerated and the remaining Jews in Germany lived in terror.

Q3: "The persecution of the Jews escalated gradually between 1933 and 1945." How far do you agree? (12 marks)

Answer: The persecution did escalate gradually, following a clear pattern: boycotts (1933) → legal discrimination (Nuremberg Laws 1935) → violent pogroms (Kristallnacht 1938) → ghettos (1939-1941) → mass shootings (Einsatzgruppen 1941) → extermination camps (1942-1945). Each stage was a radicalisation of the previous one. However, the escalation was not always smooth - there were leaps forward, particularly with Kristallnacht and the decision for the Final Solution after the invasion of the USSR. The war was crucial: it provided cover for genocide and brought millions more Jews under Nazi control. The escalation was gradual in method but accelerated dramatically by circumstances, especially the war.

Q4: Explain the purpose and decisions of the Wannsee Conference. (8 marks)

Answer: The Wannsee Conference (20 January 1942) was called by Reinhard Heydrich to coordinate the "Final Solution to the Jewish Question." The 15 officials present agreed that all 11 million European Jews would be transported to extermination camps in Poland. Eichmann would organise transport logistics. Some would be worked to death first ("annihilation through labour"). The conference did not decide to kill the Jews - that decision had already been taken. It was a bureaucratic meeting to organise the logistics of genocide efficiently across all occupied territories.

Q5: "Resistance to Nazi racial policy was impossible." How far do you agree? (16 marks)

Answer: Resistance was extremely difficult but not impossible. The White Rose group distributed anti-Nazi leaflets; the July 1944 bomb plot nearly killed Hitler; Jews in the Warsaw Ghetto fought the SS for 27 days; prisoners at Sobibor and Auschwitz led uprisings; individuals like Oskar Schindler and Raoul Wallenberg saved thousands. However, resistance was severely limited by: the terror apparatus (Gestapo, SS); the difficulty of organising in a police state; the fact that most Germans supported or acquiesced to Nazi policies; and the overwhelming military power of the state. All known resistance attempts failed to stop the genocide. Resistance was heroic but largely ineffective in changing outcomes.
