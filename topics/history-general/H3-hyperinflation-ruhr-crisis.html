Lesson 2: Core Concepts: hyperinflation and the ruhr crisis

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on hyperinflation and the ruhr crisis. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: In January 1923, French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr, Germany's industrial heartland, after Germany defaulted on reparations payments. The German government responded with a policy of passive resistance.
Key Fact: By November 1923, the German mark had become virtually worthless. At the peak of hyperinflation, 4.2 trillion marks equalled $1 USD. A loaf of bread that cost 1 mark in 1919 cost 200,000 million marks by November 1923. Workers were paid twice daily and given time off to spend their wages before they lost further value.
Key Fact: The mark had been losing value since the war - Germany had funded WWI by printing money rather than raising taxes, doubling the money supply between 1914-1918. The Ruhr crisis accelerated an existing problem into a catastrophe.
Key Fact: Hyperinflation destroyed the confidence of the German middle class in the Weimar Republic. Many who had been reasonably prosperous were reduced to poverty, and they blamed the democratic government for their suffering. This anger would later fuel support for extremist parties like the Nazis.
Key Fact: Gustav Stresemann became Chancellor in August 1923 and Foreign Secretary from 1924 until his death in October 1929. He was the key figure in Germany's economic and diplomatic recovery.

Lesson 3: Application: hyperinflation and the ruhr crisis

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: key terms from hyperinflation and the ruhr crisis. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain why France occupied the Ruhr in January 1923. (4 marks)

Answer: France occupied the Ruhr because Germany defaulted on reparations payments - specifically failing to deliver timber (December 1922) and coal (January 1923). French Prime Minister Poincaré was determined to enforce the Treaty of Versailles and believed taking resources directly was the only way to ensure payment.

Q2: How far did hyperinflation affect all groups in German society equally? (8 marks)

Answer: Hyperinflation did NOT affect all groups equally. Borrowers benefited because they could repay debts with worthless money. Industrialists who sold goods for foreign currency profited. Farmers benefited because food retained real value. However, savers, pensioners, the middle class on fixed incomes, and small businesses were devastated. Life savings became worthless overnight. The impact was deeply unequal - it destroyed the middle class while some wealthy industrialists grew richer.

Q3: "Hyperinflation was the worst crisis the Weimar Republic faced in the years 1919-1923." How far do you agree? (12 marks)

Answer: Hyperinflation was an extremely serious crisis because it destroyed the savings of the middle class, caused widespread starvation, and shattered confidence in the Republic. It also triggered the Munich Putsch. However, the political violence of 1919-1923 (Spartacists, Kapp Putsch, assassinations) was also a severe threat. The Treaty of Versailles undermined the Republic from the start. The Ruhr Crisis was the immediate trigger but hyperinflation was its economic consequence. Overall, hyperinflation was probably the worst crisis because it destroyed the economic foundation of the Republic and alienated the middle class permanently.

Q4: Explain how Stresemann solved the hyperinflation crisis. (8 marks)

Answer: Stresemann ended passive resistance in September 1923, stopping the need to print money to pay Ruhr strikers. He introduced the Rentenmark in November 1923, replacing the worthless mark at a rate of 1 Rentenmark = 1 trillion old marks, backed by land and industrial assets. He negotiated the Dawes Plan (August 1924), which reduced reparations and brought American loans. He replaced the temporary Rentenmark with the Reichsmark in August 1924. These measures restored confidence and stabilised the economy, though those who had lost savings could never recover them.

Q5: Why did the German government pursue a policy of passive resistance during the Ruhr Crisis? What were the consequences? (12 marks)

Answer: The government pursued passive resistance to show Germany would not accept French demands passively, to maintain national dignity, and because it could not afford to pay reparations. However, the consequences were catastrophic: the government printed money to pay striking workers, triggering hyperinflation; the economy collapsed; the middle class was destroyed; political extremism increased (Munich Putsch, Hamburg Uprising); and Stresemann eventually had to abandon the policy in September 1923, accepting that it was economically unsustainable.
