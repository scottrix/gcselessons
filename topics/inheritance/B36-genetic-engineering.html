B36: Genetic Engineering

Foundation Higher All Boards

Learn how genetic engineering is used to modify organisms, including gene therapy, GM crops, and forensic applications of DNA technology.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the process of genetic engineering
  • Understand how GM crops are produced and their benefits
  • Explain how gene therapy works
  • Evaluate the advantages and risks of genetic engineering

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of DNA and genes (B31)
  • Knowledge of genetic disorders (B32)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Diagrams of genetic engineering processes
  • Worksheets on GMO debates

Lesson 1: Genetic Engineering Techniques

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

GM Food

Ask: "Have you heard of GM (genetically modified) food? What do you think it is? Is it safe to eat?" Discuss that GM organisms have had genes from other organisms inserted into their DNA.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Genetic engineering: the process of modifying an organism's DNA by adding, removing, or changing genes. Basic process:
  1. Identify the gene that codes for the desired characteristic (e.g. gene for insulin production)
  2. Use restriction enzymes (molecular scissors) to cut the gene out of the donor organism's DNA
  3. Use ligase enzymes (molecular glue) to insert the gene into a vector (carrier)
    • Common vectors: bacterial plasmids (small circular DNA), viruses
  4. The vector inserts the gene into the target organism's cells
  5. The target organism now expresses the new gene and produces the new protein
Applications:
  • GM crops:
    • Golden Rice: engineered to produce beta-carotene (vitamin A) — helps prevent blindness in developing countries
    • Herbicide-resistant crops: can survive weedkillers, so farmers can spray fields without killing the crop
    • Pest-resistant crops: produce their own insecticide (from Bacillus thuringiensis gene) — reduces need for chemical pesticides
    • Drought-resistant crops: survive with less water
  • Medical applications:
    • Production of human insulin by genetically modified bacteria (E. coli) — replacing animal insulin
    • Production of human growth hormone for treating growth disorders
    • Production of clotting factors for haemophilia patients
  • Gene therapy: replacing or adding a functional copy of a defective gene to treat genetic disorders (see Lesson 2)
Worked Example

"Explain how bacteria can be used to produce human insulin." Answer: (1) The human gene for insulin production is identified. (2) The gene is cut out using restriction enzymes. (3) The gene is inserted into a bacterial plasmid using ligase enzymes. (4) The plasmid is put into bacteria. (5) The bacteria reproduce and express the human insulin gene. (6) Insulin is harvested from the bacterial culture. (7) The insulin is purified and used to treat diabetes.

Common Misconception

"Genetic engineering is the same as selective breeding"

Selective breeding selects for naturally occurring variations that already exist in a population. Genetic engineering introduces entirely new genes from a different organism, creating combinations that would never occur naturally.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on: "Genetic engineering cuts a gene from one organism and puts it into another." Steps: cut gene → put in vector → insert into target → target shows new trait. Provide a diagram.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing technology. How does it differ from traditional genetic engineering? Why is it considered more precise?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Four Steps

Students write the four key steps of genetic engineering: identify gene, cut gene, insert via vector, target organism expresses new gene.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe genetic engineering and give applications
  • Getting there: Knows what genetic engineering is but cannot describe the process
  • Not yet: Cannot describe genetic engineering

Lesson 2: Gene Therapy and Evaluation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Gene Therapy

Ask: "If a genetic disorder is caused by a faulty gene, could you fix it by putting in a working copy of the gene?" Introduce gene therapy.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Gene therapy:
  • A medical technique that treats genetic disorders by adding a functional copy of a gene into the patient's cells
  • The faulty gene is NOT removed — the new functional gene is added alongside it
  • The new gene produces the protein that was missing or defective
  • Methods of delivery:
    • Viruses are used as vectors — the virus is modified to carry the functional gene and infect the patient's cells
    • Liposomes (fat bubbles) carrying the gene can be inhaled (for lung diseases like cystic fibrosis)
    • Injection directly into target tissue
  • Currently treats: severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID), some forms of blindness, some cancers
  • Research ongoing for: cystic fibrosis, sickle cell anaemia, muscular dystrophy
  • Can be: somatic (affects only the patient, not passed to offspring) or germline (affects sex cells, passed to offspring — currently banned in most countries)
Evaluation of genetic engineering:
  • Benefits:
    • Treat genetic disorders (gene therapy)
    • Reduce pesticide use (GM pest-resistant crops)
    • Improve nutrition (Golden Rice)
    • Increase crop yields to feed growing population
    • Produce medicines cheaply (insulin)
  • Risks/concerns:
    • GM crops could cross-pollinate with wild plants → "superweeds"
    • Unknown long-term health effects of eating GM food
    • Reduces biodiversity — monocultures are vulnerable
    • Multinational companies control GM seed patents → farmers become dependent
    • Ethical concerns about "playing God" with DNA
    • Gene therapy could lead to "designer babies" if used on embryos
Worked Example

"Evaluate the use of GM crops." Benefits: (1) Can be engineered to resist pests, reducing pesticide use and environmental damage. (2) Can be made more nutritious (e.g. Golden Rice providing vitamin A). Risks: (1) GM crops could cross-pollinate with wild relatives, creating herbicide-resistant weeds. (2) Farmers may become dependent on seed companies that control patents.

Common Misconception

"Gene therapy removes the faulty gene"

Gene therapy does NOT remove the faulty gene. It adds a functional copy of the gene alongside the faulty one. The new gene produces the missing protein, which compensates for the faulty gene. The faulty gene remains in the DNA.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on: "Gene therapy adds a working gene to replace a faulty one. GM crops can resist pests and be more nutritious. But there are risks — we don't know all the long-term effects." Provide a simple pros/cons table.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the ethical debate around germline gene editing. Should parents be able to choose their child's genes? Discuss the case of He Jiankui (who edited human embryos in 2018).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Benefit and Risk

Students write one benefit and one risk of genetic engineering. Quick share and discuss.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain gene therapy and evaluate genetic engineering with balanced arguments
  • Getting there: Can describe gene therapy but cannot evaluate pros and cons
  • Not yet: Cannot explain gene therapy or evaluate

Homework Suggestions

  • Research Golden Rice: how was it made, what problem does it solve, what are the controversies?
  • Create a poster showing the steps of genetic engineering with diagrams
  • Write a balanced argument for or against GM crops (at least 3 points each side)
  • Research CRISPR-Cas9 and explain how it differs from traditional genetic engineering
