B31: DNA and the Genome

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand the structure of DNA, how genes code for proteins, and what the Human Genome Project revealed about our genetic blueprint.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the structure of DNA and its role in inheritance
  • Explain how genes code for proteins
  • Describe the structure of chromosomes and how they are inherited
  • Understand what the Human Genome Project achieved

Prerequisites

  • Basic knowledge of cells and the nucleus (B1)
  • Understanding of cell division (B2)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • DNA model or diagrams
  • Printed worksheet on DNA structure

Lesson 1: Structure of DNA

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Blueprint

Ask: "If a builder needs a blueprint to build a house, what does a cell need to build a protein?" Discuss that DNA contains the instructions for making proteins.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid): the molecule that carries genetic information in all living organisms. Structure of DNA:
  • DNA is a polymer (long chain) made up of nucleotides
  • Each nucleotide has three parts: a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group, and a base (one of four: A, T, C, G)
  • The nucleotides join together by phosphodiester bonds (sugar-phosphate-sugar-phosphate backbone)
  • DNA exists as a double helix — two strands twisted around each other
  • The two strands are joined by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases:
    • A (adenine) always pairs with T (thymine)
    • C (cytosine) always pairs with G (guanine)
    This is called complementary base pairing.
  • The sequence of bases along a DNA strand forms the genetic code
Genes:
  • A gene is a small section of DNA that contains the code for a specific protein
  • The sequence of bases in a gene determines the sequence of amino acids in the protein
  • Humans have about 20,000-25,000 genes
  • Genes are found at specific positions (loci) on chromosomes
Chromosomes:
  • DNA is coiled tightly around proteins called histones to form chromosomes
  • Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes (46 total) in each body cell — this is the diploid number
  • 22 pairs are autosomes (non-sex chromosomes)
  • 1 pair are sex chromosomes: XX (female) or XY (male)
  • Sex cells (gametes) have 23 individual chromosomes (haploid number) — half the diploid number
The genome: the entire set of genetic material of an organism (all the DNA).
Worked Example

"Describe the structure of a DNA nucleotide." Answer: A DNA nucleotide consists of: (1) a deoxyribose sugar, (2) a phosphate group attached to carbon 5 of the sugar, and (3) a nitrogenous base (adenine, thymine, cytosine, or guanine) attached to carbon 1 of the sugar. Nucleotides are joined by phosphodiester bonds between the phosphate of one nucleotide and the sugar of the next.

Common Misconception

"Genes are made of protein"

Genes are made of DNA. DNA wraps around histone proteins to form chromosomes, but the gene itself is a section of DNA that codes for a protein. The protein is the product, not the component, of a gene.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on: "DNA = instructions, genes = sections of code, chromosomes = packages of DNA." Provide a diagram of DNA structure for labelling. Simplify base pairing: "A always pairs with T, C always pairs with G."
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the structure of the DNA double helix discovered by Watson and Crick. Discuss the contributions of Rosalind Franklin. Why was the base pairing rule important for understanding DNA replication?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Key Terms

Students write definitions for: DNA, gene, chromosome, genome, nucleotide, base pairing. Quick self-check with a partner.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe DNA structure including nucleotides, base pairing, and double helix
  • Getting there: Knows DNA is in chromosomes but cannot describe the structure
  • Not yet: Cannot describe the structure of DNA

Lesson 2: How Genes Code for Proteins

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Code Question

Ask: "If you have a 4-letter alphabet (A, T, C, G), how many different 3-letter words could you make? (4³ = 64.) This is similar to how DNA codes for amino acids."

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The genetic code:
  • Groups of three bases (a triplet or codon) code for a specific amino acid
  • There are 4 bases but 20 amino acids, so three bases are needed: 4³ = 64 possible codons
  • Some codons are "stop" codons (signal the end of a protein)
  • One codon (AUG) is the "start" codon and codes for methionine
Protein synthesis (simplified):
  1. In the nucleus, the DNA code for a gene is copied into messenger RNA (mRNA) — this is called transcription. The mRNA is a single strand that carries the code out of the nucleus.
  2. mRNA leaves the nucleus through nuclear pores and attaches to a ribosome in the cytoplasm.
  3. The ribosome reads the mRNA code three bases (one codon) at a time. Each codon matches a transfer RNA (tRNA) carrying a specific amino acid.
  4. Amino acids are joined together in the correct order to form a polypeptide (protein chain) — this is called translation.
  5. The polypeptide folds into a specific 3D shape to form a functional protein.
Mutations:
  • A mutation is a change in the base sequence of DNA
  • Some mutations change the amino acid sequence of a protein (missense mutation)
  • Some mutations create a premature stop codon (nonsense mutation)
  • Some mutations have no effect (silent mutation — the codon still codes for the same amino acid)
  • Most mutations are harmful; some are neutral; very few are beneficial
Worked Example

"Explain how a gene codes for a protein." Answer: (1) The gene is a section of DNA with a specific sequence of bases. (2) The DNA code is transcribed into mRNA in the nucleus. (3) mRNA carries the code to ribosomes in the cytoplasm. (4) The ribosome reads the mRNA in groups of three bases (codons). (5) Each codon codes for a specific amino acid. (6) Amino acids are joined in the correct order to form a polypeptide, which folds into a protein.

Common Misconception

"One gene codes for one protein"

This is generally true — one gene codes for one polypeptide (which may be one protein or part of a protein). However, some genes code for RNA molecules that are not translated into proteins (e.g. tRNA, rRNA). Also, alternative splicing can produce different proteins from the same gene in eukaryotes.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Simplify: "DNA → mRNA → protein. The code in DNA is copied to mRNA, which goes to the ribosome. The ribosome reads the code and builds a protein from amino acids." Provide a flow diagram.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research how the genetic code is "degenerate" (multiple codons can code for the same amino acid). Why is this advantageous? Investigate how mutations in the haemoglobin gene cause sickle cell anaemia.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Three Steps

Students write the three key steps of protein synthesis: transcription (DNA → mRNA), translation (mRNA → protein), folding (protein → functional shape).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain how genes code for proteins through transcription and translation
  • Getting there: Knows that DNA makes protein but cannot describe the process
  • Not yet: Cannot explain the link between genes and proteins

Lesson 3: The Human Genome Project

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Question

Ask: "How many genes do humans have? How does this compare to other organisms?" Discuss that the Human Genome Project mapped all human genes.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The Human Genome Project (HGP):
  • An international research project that started in 1990 and was completed in 2003
  • Goal: to determine the complete sequence of the human genome (all 3.2 billion base pairs)
  • Identified about 20,000-25,000 protein-coding genes
  • Mapped the location of genes on chromosomes
Benefits of the HGP:
  • Medicine: identification of genes linked to diseases (e.g. BRCA1/BRCA2 for breast cancer, genes for cystic fibrosis, Huntington's disease). Allows genetic testing and early diagnosis.
  • Forensics: DNA profiling for identification in criminal cases and paternity tests
  • Evolution: comparing genomes of different species to understand evolutionary relationships
  • Pharmacogenomics: developing personalised medicine based on an individual's genome
  • Gene therapy: correcting defective genes to treat genetic disorders
Ethical issues:
  • Privacy: who should have access to your genetic information?
  • Insurance: should insurance companies know your genetic predispositions?
  • Genetic discrimination: could people be treated differently based on their genes?
  • Designer babies: should parents be able to choose their child's traits?
  • Ownership: can genes be patented?
Worked Example

"Give two benefits and one ethical concern of the Human Genome Project." Benefits: (1) Identification of genes linked to diseases, allowing genetic testing and early treatment. (2) Development of personalised medicine tailored to an individual's genetic makeup. Ethical concern: genetic information could be used for discrimination (e.g. insurance companies refusing coverage based on genetic predisposition to disease).

Common Misconception

"The Human Genome Project mapped all human genes and we know what every gene does"

The HGP mapped the DNA sequence, but the function of many genes is still not fully understood. Only about 1-2% of the genome codes for proteins. The rest was once called "junk DNA" but we now know much of it has regulatory functions. Research is ongoing.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on: "The HGP found all our genes. Benefits include finding disease genes and improving medicine. Concerns include privacy and discrimination." Provide a simple pros/cons list.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing — how does it work and what are its potential applications? Discuss the ethics of editing human embryos. Should we allow "designer babies"?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Benefit and Concern

Students write one benefit and one ethical concern of the Human Genome Project on mini-whiteboards. Share and discuss.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the HGP and evaluate its benefits and ethical concerns
  • Getting there: Can describe the HGP but cannot evaluate ethical issues
  • Not yet: Cannot describe what the HGP achieved

Homework Suggestions

  • Draw and label a DNA double helix showing nucleotides, base pairing, and the sugar-phosphate backbone
  • Create a flowchart showing how a gene codes for a protein (DNA → mRNA → protein)
  • Research one genetic disorder (e.g. cystic fibrosis, sickle cell anaemia) and explain how it is caused by a mutation
  • Write a balanced argument for or against genetic engineering in humans
