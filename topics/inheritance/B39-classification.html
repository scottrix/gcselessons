B39: Classification

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand how organisms are classified using the Linnaean system, the three-domain system, and how modern classification uses DNA evidence.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the Linnaean classification system and binomial nomenclature
  • Understand the five-kingdom and three-domain systems
  • Explain how classification has changed with DNA evidence
  • Use classification keys to identify organisms

Prerequisites

  • Basic knowledge of cell structure (B1)
  • Understanding of evolution and common ancestry (B33)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Classification keys (simple dichotomous keys)
  • Images of organisms for classification practice
  • Worksheets on binomial nomenclature

Lesson 1: The Linnaean System and Binomial Nomenclature

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Name Game

Ask: "Why do scientists use Latin names for organisms instead of common names? What problems might common names cause?" (e.g. "robin" means different birds in different countries.)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Classification: the process of sorting organisms into groups based on their characteristics. Linnaean classification system:
  • Developed by Carolus Linnaeus (1707-1778), a Swedish botanist
  • Organisms are grouped into a hierarchy of increasingly specific groups
  • The hierarchy (from broadest to most specific):
    • Domain (e.g. Eukaryota)
    • Kingdom (e.g. Animalia)
    • Phylum (e.g. Chordata)
    • Class (e.g. Mammalia)
    • Order (e.g. Primates)
    • Family (e.g. Hominidae)
    • Genus (e.g. Homo)
    • Species (e.g. sapiens)
    Mnemonic: "Dear King Philip Came Over For Good Soup"
Binomial nomenclature (two-name naming system):
  • Every organism is given a two-part Latin name
  • First part: Genus (capitalised)
  • Second part: species (lowercase)
  • Both are italicised (or underlined when handwritten)
  • Example: humans = Homo sapiens, domestic cat = Felis catus
  • Advantages: universal system, avoids confusion from common names, shows relationships (same genus = closely related)
Species: a group of organisms that can reproduce to produce fertile offspring.
  • This is the biological species concept
  • Limitation: some organisms can reproduce but produce infertile offspring (e.g. horse × donkey = mule)
Worked Example

"Write the correct binomial name for humans and explain what each part means." Answer: Homo sapiens. "Homo" is the genus (capitalised), "sapiens" is the species (lowercase). Both parts are italicised. This tells us humans belong to the genus Homo, which also includes extinct species like Homo erectus.

Common Misconception

"The species is the smallest group in classification"

Species is the most specific level in the Linnaean hierarchy, but subspecies and varieties exist below species level. More importantly, modern classification also considers genetics, not just physical characteristics.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on: "Scientists give every organism a two-part Latin name. The hierarchy goes from big (Domain) to small (Species)." Provide a diagram of the hierarchy using humans as an example. Practice writing binomial names.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research how DNA analysis has changed classification. Why was the orangutan moved from Pongo to a different classification? How do cladograms show evolutionary relationships?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Hierarchy

Students write the Linnaean hierarchy from largest to smallest group. Quick self-check.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the Linnaean system and write binomial names correctly
  • Getting there: Knows organisms are grouped but cannot recall the hierarchy
  • Not yet: Cannot describe the classification system

Lesson 2: Kingdoms, Domains, and Modern Classification

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Five Groups

Ask: "If you had to sort all living things into just five groups, how would you do it?" Discuss the five-kingdom system.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Five-kingdom system:
  • Animalia (animals): multicellular, eukaryotic, heterotrophic, no cell walls, can usually move
  • Plantae (plants): multicellular, eukaryotic, autotrophic (photosynthesise), have cell walls made of cellulose
  • Fungi: multicellular (except yeast), eukaryotic, heterotrophic (absorb nutrients), cell walls made of chitin
  • Protoctista (protists): mostly unicellular, eukaryotic, some are photosynthetic, some are parasitic (e.g. Plasmodium)
  • Prokaryotae (bacteria): unicellular, prokaryotic (no nucleus), cell walls (not cellulose or chitin)
Three-domain system (Higher only):
  • Proposed by Carl Woese in 1977 based on ribosomal RNA analysis
  • Splits the prokaryotes into two domains:
    • Bacteria: true bacteria
    • Archaea: extremophiles and other ancient bacteria (different cell membrane chemistry)
  • All eukaryotes (animals, plants, fungi, protoctists) belong to the domain Eukaryota
  • Three domains: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukaryota
Modern classification using DNA:
  • Organisms are now classified based on DNA/DNA base sequences as well as physical characteristics
  • Organisms with more similar DNA sequences are more closely related
  • This has changed some classifications:
    • Fungi are now more closely related to animals than to plants
    • Archaea are now classified separately from bacteria
Classification keys:
  • Dichotomous keys: a series of paired statements (questions) that lead to the identification of an organism
  • Each pair offers two choices — follow the one that matches the organism
Worked Example

"Place these organisms in the correct kingdom: mushroom, oak tree, Amoeba, Escherichia coli, lion." Answer: Mushroom = Fungi, Oak tree = Plantae, Amoeba = Protoctista, Escherichia coli = Prokaryotae (Bacteria), Lion = Animalia.

Common Misconception

"Fungi are plants"

Fungi were once classified as plants, but DNA analysis shows they are more closely related to animals. Fungi are heterotrophic (they absorb nutrients from other organisms), have cell walls made of chitin (not cellulose), and store energy as glycogen (like animals, not starch like plants).

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on the five kingdoms: "Animals move and eat. Plants make their own food. Fungi absorb food. Protoctists are tiny and mostly single-celled. Bacteria are tiny with no nucleus." Provide images to sort into kingdoms. Use a simple dichotomous key.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research why the three-domain system was proposed. How does ribosomal RNA analysis work? Why is this more reliable than physical characteristics for classification?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Kingdom Sort

Students sort 10 organisms into the five kingdoms. Quick peer-check.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can classify organisms and explain the three-domain system
  • Getting there: Can use the five-kingdom system but cannot explain the three domains
  • Not yet: Cannot classify organisms into kingdoms

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a classification hierarchy for 5 different organisms (from Domain to Species)
  • Make a dichotomous key for 6-8 items (can use everyday objects or organisms)
  • Research why DNA analysis changed the classification of fungi
  • Investigate how many species are estimated to exist on Earth and how many have been described
