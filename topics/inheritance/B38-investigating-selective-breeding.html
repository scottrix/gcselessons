B38: Investigating Selective Breeding

Foundation Higher All Boards

Plan and carry out an investigation into selective breeding, analysing data from breeding experiments to understand how desired characteristics are selected over generations.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the process of selective breeding and its applications
  • Plan an investigation into selective breeding using data or simulation
  • Analyse results and draw conclusions about the effectiveness of selective breeding
  • Evaluate the limitations of selective breeding

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of genetic variation and inheritance (B32-B33)
  • Basic understanding of natural selection (B33)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Computer simulation of selective breeding (if available)
  • Data sheets on crop yields or animal characteristics
  • Graph paper or spreadsheet software

Lesson 1: Selective Breeding Process

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Dog Breeds

Show images of different dog breeds (Chihuahua, Great Dane, Labrador, Poodle). Ask: "These are all the same species. How did they become so different?" Discuss selective breeding by humans.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Selective breeding (artificial selection):
  • Humans choose organisms with desired characteristics and breed them together
  • Over many generations, the desired trait becomes more common and more pronounced in the population
  • This has been practised for thousands of years (domestication of plants and animals)
Process of selective breeding:
  1. Identify the desired characteristic (e.g. higher crop yield, docile temperament)
  2. Choose parents that show the desired characteristic
  3. Breed these parents together
  4. Select the offspring that show the strongest expression of the desired trait
  5. Repeat over many generations
  6. The trait becomes more pronounced and consistent in the population
Examples:
  • Crops: wheat bred for higher yield, disease resistance, shorter stems (to prevent lodging), drought tolerance
  • Animals: dairy cows for more milk, chickens for more eggs, dogs for specific appearances and behaviours
  • Disease resistance: crops bred to resist specific diseases (e.g. wheat resistant to fungal infections)
Limitations:
  • Reduced genetic variation: breeding from similar individuals reduces the gene pool
  • Inbreeding: if too closely related individuals breed, harmful recessive alleles become more common (inbreeding depression)
  • Vulnerability: low genetic variation means the population may be vulnerable to new diseases or environmental changes
  • Takes many generations — cannot introduce new characteristics, only select from existing variation
Worked Example

"Describe how selective breeding could be used to produce disease-resistant wheat." Answer: (1) Grow wheat plants and expose them to the disease. (2) Identify plants that survive (these may have resistance genes). (3) Breed the surviving plants together. (4) Expose the offspring to the disease again. (5) Select and breed the survivors. (6) Repeat over many generations until most plants are resistant.

Common Misconception

"Selective breeding creates new genes"

Selective breeding only selects from genetic variation that ALREADY EXISTS in the population. It cannot create new characteristics — it can only make existing ones more common. To create entirely new characteristics, genetic engineering is needed.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on the process: "Choose the best, breed them, select the best offspring, repeat." Use dog breeds as a familiar example. Provide a simplified flowchart of the process.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research specific examples of selective breeding in agriculture. How has it changed crop yields over the last 100 years? What are the risks of relying on monocultures?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Process Steps

Students write the four key steps of selective breeding. Quick self-check.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the process of selective breeding and its limitations
  • Getting there: Can describe the process but cannot explain limitations
  • Not yet: Cannot describe selective breeding

Lesson 2: Investigation and Data Analysis

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Data Question

Show data on crop yields over 10 generations of selective breeding. Ask: "What trend do you see? Is selective breeding effective?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Investigation approach: Option A: Computer simulation
  • Use an online selective breeding simulation (e.g. virtual pet breeding, crop simulation)
  • Students select organisms with desired traits over multiple generations
  • Record data on trait frequency in each generation
  • Analyse whether the trait becomes more common
Option B: Data analysis
  • Provide datasets on real selective breeding programmes (e.g. corn yield over 50 years, chicken egg production)
  • Students plot graphs and analyse trends
  • Calculate percentage change in the trait over generations
Option C: modelling activity
  • Use coloured beads or cards to represent alleles in a population
  • Students "select" specific colours (representing desired traits) and "breed" them
  • Track how the frequency of the selected trait changes over generations
  • Compare results when selecting strongly vs weakly
Data analysis skills:
  • Plot line graphs showing trait change over generations
  • Calculate mean, median, and mode if multiple trials are conducted
  • Identify trends: is the trait increasing? Is the rate of change slowing?
  • Discuss reliability: repeat the investigation and compare results
  • Evaluate limitations: small sample size, only one trait considered, real populations are more complex
Worked Example

"A student selects the tallest plants from a population and breeds them. After 5 generations, the average height has increased from 20cm to 35cm. Analyse these results." Answer: The data shows that selective breeding was effective — average height increased by 75% over 5 generations. This suggests that taller height is genetically influenced and can be selected for. However, the rate of increase may slow down as the population reaches its genetic potential for height.

Common Misconception

"Selective breeding always produces better organisms"

Selective breeding produces organisms better suited to the specific trait being selected, but this may come at a cost. For example, broiler chickens bred for rapid growth often have leg problems and heart issues. Selective breeding for one trait can inadvertently select against other desirable traits.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Use the bead/card modelling activity for a hands-on approach. Focus on: "When you select the tallest each time, the average gets taller." Provide a pre-made graph template to complete.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Investigate the "middling" effect — why does the rate of improvement slow down over generations? Research the concept of genetic potential and environmental limits on phenotype.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Conclusion

Students write a conclusion for their investigation: "Selective breeding [was/was not] effective because..." Support with evidence from their data.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can plan, carry out, and evaluate a selective breeding investigation
  • Getting there: Can collect data but cannot analyse or evaluate effectively
  • Not yet: Cannot plan or carry out an investigation

Homework Suggestions

  • Research one selective breeding programme (e.g. dog breeds, crop improvement) and create a timeline of changes over generations
  • Analyse data on crop yields over 20 years and write a conclusion about the effectiveness of selective breeding
  • Compare selective breeding with genetic engineering — which is more effective and why?
  • Investigate inbreeding in purebred dogs — what health problems are associated with it?
