B34: Reproduction

Foundation Higher All Boards

Compare sexual and asexual reproduction, understand the process of meiosis, and explore different methods of reproduction in plants and animals.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Compare sexual and asexual reproduction
  • Describe the process of meiosis and why it is important
  • Understand different methods of reproduction in plants
  • Explain the advantages and disadvantages of each reproductive method

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of DNA, genes, and chromosomes (B31)
  • Knowledge of cell division (B2)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Diagrams of meiosis and mitosis
  • Worksheets on reproduction methods

Lesson 1: Sexual vs Asexual Reproduction

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Question

Ask: "Can you think of organisms that reproduce without a partner? How do they do it?" Discuss: bacteria divide by binary fission, strawberry plants send out runners, daffodils grow from bulbs.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Sexual reproduction:
  • Involves the fusion of male and female gametes (sex cells) — this is called fertilisation
  • Two parents contribute genetic material
  • The fusion produces a zygote with genetic material from both parents
  • Offspring are genetically different from parents and from each other (variation)
Asexual reproduction:
  • Involves ONLY ONE parent
  • No fusion of gametes
  • Offspring are produced by cell division (mitosis)
  • Offspring are genetically identical to the parent — they are clones
  • No genetic variation (unless mutation occurs)
Comparison table:
FeatureSexualAsexual
ParentsTwoOne
GametesYes (fused)No
FertilisationYesNo
Offspring genetically identical?NoYes (clones)
VariationYesNo
SpeedSlowerFaster
Energy neededMoreLess
Advantages/disadvantages:
  • Sexual reproduction advantages: variation → species can adapt to changing environments, helps eliminate harmful mutations
  • Sexual reproduction disadvantages: requires two parents, slower, needs more energy, not all organisms can find a mate
  • Asexual reproduction advantages: fast, efficient, only needs one parent, no need to find a mate
  • Asexual reproduction disadvantages: no variation → if environment changes, whole population may be wiped out, harmful mutations accumulate
Worked Example

"Explain why a population that reproduces sexually is more likely to survive a change in the environment than one that reproduces asexually." Answer: Sexual reproduction produces offspring with genetic variation (due to combining genes from two parents). This means some offspring may have characteristics that help them survive in the new environment. Asexual reproduction produces clones with no variation — if the environment changes, ALL offspring may be equally affected and the whole population could die.

Common Misconception

"Asexual reproduction is always better because it is faster"

Asexual reproduction is faster and more efficient, but it produces no variation. In a stable environment, this is fine. But if the environment changes (new disease, temperature change), a population with no variation may not survive because none of the offspring are different enough to be better suited.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on the key differences: "Sexual = two parents, variation. Asexual = one parent, clones." Provide a simplified comparison table. Use real examples (bacteria = asexual, humans = sexual).
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research organisms that can do BOTH: e.g. daffodils (bulbs asexually, seeds sexually), malarial parasites (asexual in humans, sexual in mosquitoes). Why would an organism use both methods?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Advantage/Disadvantage

Students write one advantage and one disadvantage of sexual and asexual reproduction. Quick share.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can compare sexual and asexual reproduction with advantages and disadvantages
  • Getting there: Knows the basic difference but cannot evaluate
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between sexual and asexual reproduction

Lesson 2: Meiosis

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Half the Chromosomes

Ask: "If body cells have 46 chromosomes and gametes have 23, how does the cell halve the chromosome number?" Introduce meiosis.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Meiosis: a type of cell division that produces gametes (sex cells) with half the number of chromosomes. Why meiosis is needed:
  • Body cells are diploid (2n) — have two copies of each chromosome (one from each parent)
  • Gametes must be haploid (n) — have one copy of each chromosome
  • If gametes were diploid, fertilisation would double the chromosome number each generation
  • Meiosis halves the chromosome number so that fertilisation restores it
Process of meiosis:
  1. Chromosomes replicate (copy themselves) during interphase (before meiosis begins)
  2. Meiosis I: homologous pairs (matching pairs of chromosomes from each parent) line up at the centre of the cell
  3. Homologous pairs are separated — one chromosome from each pair goes to each daughter cell
  4. Meiosis II: the two daughter cells divide again
  5. Sister chromatids (the two copies of each chromosome) are separated
  6. Result: four haploid cells, each genetically different
Genetic variation from meiosis:
  • Independent assortment: the way homologous pairs line up randomly at the centre of the cell. Each pair lines up independently, so there are 2²³ (about 8 million) possible combinations of chromosomes in each gamete.
  • Crossing over: (Higher only) during meiosis I, homologous chromosomes swap sections of DNA. This creates new combinations of alleles on a single chromosome.
  • Random fertilisation: any sperm can fuse with any egg, increasing variation further.
Worked Example

"Explain two ways meiosis produces genetically different gametes." Answer: (1) Independent assortment — homologous chromosome pairs line up randomly at the centre of the cell during meiosis I, so each gamete gets a random mix of chromosomes from each parent. (2) Crossing over — homologous chromosomes exchange sections of DNA during meiosis I, creating new combinations of alleles on each chromosome.

Common Misconception

"Meiosis produces two identical cells"

Meiosis produces FOUR genetically DIFFERENT haploid cells. Each cell has a unique combination of chromosomes due to independent assortment and crossing over. This is very different from mitosis, which produces two identical diploid cells.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on: "Meiosis makes gametes. It halves the chromosome number. It makes four different cells." Compare with mitosis (two identical cells). Provide a simple diagram to label.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Investigate non-disjunction: what happens if chromosomes do not separate properly in meiosis? How does this cause conditions like Down syndrome (trisomy 21)?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Meiosis vs Mitosis

Students complete a comparison table: mitosis (2 identical diploid cells, body cell growth/repair) vs meiosis (4 different haploid cells, gamete production).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe meiosis and explain how it produces genetic variation
  • Getting there: Knows meiosis makes gametes but cannot describe the process
  • Not yet: Cannot describe meiosis or its purpose

Lesson 3: Reproduction in Plants

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Plant Reproduction

Ask: "How do plants reproduce? They can't move to find a mate!" Discuss: some plants use seeds (sexual), some use runners or bulbs (asexual), some use both.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Sexual reproduction in flowering plants:
  • Flowers contain male and female reproductive parts
  • Male: anthers produce pollen (contains male gametes)
  • Female: ovary contains ovules (each contains a female gamete/egg cell)
  • Pollination: transfer of pollen from anther to stigma (by wind, insects, birds, bats, water)
  • Fertilisation: pollen grain grows a tube down the style to the ovule. Male gamete travels down the tube and fuses with the female gamete.
  • After fertilisation: ovule → seed, ovary → fruit, petals fall off
  • Seeds are dispersed by wind, water, animals, or explosion
  • Seeds germinate when conditions are right (water, oxygen, warmth)
Asexual reproduction in plants:
  • Runners: horizontal stems that grow along the ground and produce new plants at nodes (e.g. strawberry plants)
  • Bulbs: underground storage organs that can produce new plants (e.g. daffodils, onions)
  • cuttings: a piece of stem is cut off and planted to grow roots
  • Division: a plant is divided into smaller sections, each grows into a new plant
  • All produce genetically identical clones
Advantages of each:
  • Sexual: variation, seeds can be dispersed long distances, seeds survive harsh conditions (dormancy)
  • Asexual: fast, efficient, plants don't need to find a mate, good for gardeners who want identical plants
Worked Example

"Describe the path of pollen in sexual reproduction in plants." Answer: (1) Pollen is produced in the anthers. (2) Pollination: pollen is transferred from the anther to the stigma (by wind, insects, etc.). (3) A pollen tube grows down the style. (4) The male gamete travels down the tube. (5) Fertilisation: the male gamete fuses with the female gamete in the ovule.

Common Misconception

"Pollination and fertilisation are the same thing"

Pollination is the transfer of pollen to the stigma. Fertilisation is the fusion of male and female gametes. Pollination must happen before fertilisation, but they are separate events. Pollination can happen without fertilisation (e.g. if pollen is incompatible).

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on: "Flowers make seeds. Pollen goes to stigma (pollination). Male meets female gamete (fertilisation). Seed grows into new plant." Provide labelled diagrams of a flower. Use practical: dissect a flower.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Investigate plant tissue culture — how can commercial growers produce thousands of identical plants from a small piece of tissue? What are the advantages for conservation and agriculture?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Flower Parts

Students label the key parts of a flower: anther, stigma, style, ovary, ovule, petals, sepals. Self-check with a diagram.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe sexual and asexual reproduction in plants with examples
  • Getting there: Knows flowers are for reproduction but cannot describe the process
  • Not yet: Cannot describe plant reproduction methods

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a comparison table of sexual vs asexual reproduction with at least 6 features
  • Draw and label the stages of meiosis with brief descriptions
  • Investigate methods of seed dispersal with examples
  • Research one plant that reproduces both sexually and asexually and explain why
