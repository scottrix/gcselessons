B33: Variation and Evolution

Foundation Higher All Boards

Explore the causes of variation, how evolution occurs through natural selection, and the evidence supporting evolution.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the difference between genetic and environmental variation
  • Explain how natural selection leads to evolution
  • Describe the evidence for evolution
  • Understand how mutations and selective breeding drive change

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of genetics and alleles (B32)
  • Basic knowledge of ecosystems (B40 will build on this)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Diagrams of peppered moths / antibiotic-resistant bacteria
  • Worksheets on natural selection

Lesson 1: Variation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Look Around

Ask: "Look at your classmates. What differences can you see? (Height, hair colour, eye colour, hand size...) Why do we all look different?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Variation: the differences between individuals of the same species. Two types of variation:
  • Genetic variation: caused by differences in DNA/genes. Inherited from parents. Examples: eye colour, blood group, genetic disorders, finger length.
  • Environmental variation: caused by conditions in which an organism develops. NOT inherited. Examples: language spoken, weight (due to diet), scars, muscle development (due to exercise), tan.
Most characteristics are influenced by BOTH genes and environment:
  • Height: genetics sets a potential range, nutrition affects where you end up in that range
  • Skin colour: genetics determines potential, sun exposure affects actual colour
  • Intelligence: genetics affects potential, education and environment affect development
Mutations:
  • Spontaneous changes in the DNA sequence
  • Can be caused by: errors in DNA replication, radiation, chemicals, viruses
  • Most mutations have no effect (silent mutations)
  • Some cause genetic variation — this is the raw material for evolution
  • Mutations in sex cells can be inherited by offspring
Worked Example

"Classify these as genetic, environmental, or both: (a) blue eyes, (b) scarred knee, (c) speaking French, (d) having diabetes (Type 1)." Answer: (a) genetic, (b) environmental, (c) environmental, (d) genetic (Type 1 diabetes has a genetic component).

Common Misconception

"Evolution means individuals change"

Evolution is a change in the genetic makeup of a POPULATION over generations, not a change in individual organisms during their lifetime. Individuals do not evolve — populations do.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on: "Genetic = from genes, environmental = from surroundings, most = both." Provide a sorting activity with examples to classify.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research epigenetics — how can environmental factors affect gene expression WITHOUT changing the DNA sequence? How might stress or diet in one generation affect the next?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Classify

Students classify 5 characteristics as genetic, environmental, or both. Quick peer-check.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can distinguish between genetic and environmental variation with examples
  • Getting there: Understands there are two types but cannot always classify correctly
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between genetic and environmental variation

Lesson 2: Natural Selection and Evolution

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Peppered Moths

Discuss: before the Industrial Revolution, most peppered moths were light-coloured. After pollution darkened trees, most became dark-coloured. Why? Introduce natural selection.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Evolution: the gradual change in the inherited characteristics of a population over time, which may result in the formation of new species. Charles Darwin's theory of natural selection:
  1. Variation: individuals in a population show variation (genetic differences)
  2. Overproduction: more offspring are produced than can survive
  3. Competition: organisms compete for limited resources (food, water, mates, territory)
  4. Survival of the fittest: individuals with advantageous characteristics (best suited to the environment) are more likely to survive and reproduce
  5. Inheritance: these advantageous characteristics are passed on to offspring
  6. Over time: the frequency of these advantageous alleles increases in the population — the population evolves
Classic examples:
  • Peppered moths: before pollution, light moths were camouflaged on lichen-covered trees. Dark moths were eaten. After pollution killed lichen and darkened trees, dark moths were camouflaged and light moths were eaten. Dark allele increased in frequency.
  • Antibiotic-resistant bacteria: random mutations give some bacteria resistance to antibiotics. When antibiotics are used, resistant bacteria survive and reproduce. Non-resistant bacteria die. Over time, the population becomes resistant.
Selective breeding (artificial selection):
  • Humans choose organisms with desired characteristics and breed them together
  • Over many generations, the desired trait becomes more pronounced
  • Examples: high-yield crop plants, docile domestic animals, disease-resistant crops
  • Risk: reduces genetic variation → populations may be more vulnerable to disease
Worked Example

"Explain how antibiotic-resistant bacteria evolve." Answer: (1) Within a population of bacteria, random mutations occur. (2) Some mutations may confer resistance to an antibiotic. (3) When antibiotics are used, non-resistant bacteria are killed. (4) Resistant bacteria survive and reproduce. (5) The resistant bacteria pass on their resistance genes. (6) Over time, most bacteria in the population become resistant.

Common Misconception

"Bacteria become resistant because they are exposed to antibiotics"

The resistance mutation already exists in the population BEFORE exposure to antibiotics. The antibiotic does not cause the mutation — it selects for bacteria that already have it. This is natural selection in action.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Simplify natural selection to five steps: variation → overproduction → competition → survival → inheritance. Use the peppered moth example throughout. Provide a cloze passage to complete.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research Darwin's four postulates. Why was his theory controversial? How did Alfred Russel Wallace contribute? Investigate Lamarck's theory of inheritance of acquired characteristics — why was it wrong?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Five Steps

Students write the five key steps of natural selection. Quick self-check.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain natural selection and provide examples
  • Getting there: Understands natural selection but cannot apply to examples
  • Not yet: Cannot explain how natural selection works

Lesson 3: Evidence for Evolution and Extinction

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Fossil Question

Ask: "How do we know that organisms changed over millions of years? What evidence is there?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Evidence for evolution:
  • Fossils: the preserved remains or traces of organisms from millions of years ago
    • Formed by: mineralisation (minerals replace organic material), moulds and casts, preservation in amber/ice/peat
    • Found in sedimentary rock layers (strata)
    • Fossils show how organisms have changed over time (e.g. horse evolution — smaller to larger)
    • Gaps in fossil record (not all organisms fossilise well)
  • Antibiotic resistance: direct observation of evolution in bacteria happening in real time
  • Anatomy: homologous structures (similar bone structures in different organisms, e.g. human arm, whale flipper, bat wing) suggest common ancestry
  • Embryology: similar embryonic stages in different vertebrates suggest common ancestry
Extinction:
  • An species is extinct when the last individual dies
  • Causes: environmental changes, new predators, new diseases, catastrophic events (asteroid impacts, volcanic eruptions), competition from other species
  • About 99% of all species that have ever lived are now extinct
Selective breeding examples:
  • Crops: wheat, maize — bred for higher yield, disease resistance
  • Animals: cows for more milk, chickens for more eggs, dogs for specific traits
Worked Example

"Give two pieces of evidence for evolution." Answer: (1) Fossils show that organisms have changed over millions of years — older fossils show simpler organisms, newer fossils show more complex organisms. (2) Antibiotic-resistant bacteria demonstrate evolution happening in real time — bacterial populations become resistant through natural selection.

Common Misconception

"Fossils are the remains of dead organisms"

Fossils include remains (bones, teeth, shells) AND traces (footprints, burrows, dung). A fossil is any preserved evidence of life from the past, not just the physical remains of the organism itself.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on: "Fossils show change over time. Antibiotic resistance shows evolution now. Extinction happens when conditions change too fast." Provide images of fossils and a timeline.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research radiocarbon dating and stratigraphy — how do scientists determine the age of fossils? Why are there gaps in the fossil record?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Evidence Summary

Students write two pieces of evidence for evolution and one cause of extinction. Share with partner.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe evidence for evolution and explain extinction
  • Getting there: Knows fossils are evidence but cannot explain how they form
  • Not yet: Cannot describe evidence for evolution

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a poster showing the stages of natural selection using a specific example
  • Research how antibiotic resistance is a global health threat
  • Investigate Darwin's voyage on the HMS Beagle and what he discovered
  • Create a timeline of horse evolution using fossil evidence
