B32: Genetic Inheritance

Foundation Higher All Boards

Learn how characteristics are inherited through alleles, dominant and recessive genes, and how to predict offspring outcomes using genetic diagrams.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define key terms: allele, dominant, recessive, homozygous, heterozygous, genotype, phenotype
  • Use Punnett squares to predict the outcomes of genetic crosses
  • Understand how sex is determined in humans
  • Use genetic diagrams to work out probabilities of inherited conditions

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of DNA and genes (B31)
  • Knowledge that chromosomes come in pairs (diploid)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Punnett square templates
  • Genetic cross worksheets

Lesson 1: Key Terms and Dominant/Recessive Alleles

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Family Resemblance

Ask: "Why do you look a bit like your parents but not exactly the same? Why do some siblings have different eye colours?" Discuss that characteristics are inherited through genes.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Key vocabulary:
  • Gene: a section of DNA that codes for a specific characteristic
  • Allele: a version of a gene. Genes come in pairs (one from each parent), and each can have different alleles. e.g. the gene for eye colour might have a brown allele and a blue allele.
  • Dominant allele: an allele that is expressed (shows its effect) even if only one copy is present. Represented by a CAPITAL letter (e.g. B).
  • Recessive allele: an allele that is only expressed if TWO copies are present (no dominant allele present). Represent by a lowercase letter (e.g. b).
  • Homozygous: having two identical alleles for a gene (e.g. BB or bb). Also called "pure breeding."
  • Heterozygous: having two different alleles for a gene (e.g. Bb). Also called a "hybrid."
  • Genotype: the combination of alleles an organism has (e.g. BB, Bb, or bb)
  • Phenotype: the physical expression of the genotype — what the organism looks like (e.g. brown eyes or blue eyes)
Example: fur colour in cats (simplified)
  • B = brown fur (dominant), b = white fur (recessive)
  • BB = brown fur, Bb = brown fur (B masks b), bb = white fur
  • Only the recessive phenotype appears when homozygous recessive (bb)
Worked Example

"A cat is heterozygous for fur colour (Bb). What is its phenotype?" Answer: The cat has brown fur because B (brown) is dominant and masks the effect of b (white). The genotype is Bb (heterozygous) but the phenotype is brown fur.

Common Misconception

"Dominant alleles are more common in the population"

Dominant means the allele is expressed when present, not that it is more common. A dominant allele can be rare in a population. For example, polydactyly (extra fingers) is caused by a dominant allele, but it is rare in the population.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on the key terms: allele = version of gene, dominant = shows if one copy, recessive = only shows if two copies. Provide a glossary card. Use simple examples (e.g. brown vs blue eyes).
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research codominance (e.g. blood groups AB) and incomplete dominance (e.g. red × white snapdragon → pink). How do these differ from simple dominant/recessive inheritance?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Term Check

Students define: allele, dominant, recessive, homozygous, heterozygous, genotype, phenotype. Self-check with a glossary.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can define all key terms and explain dominant/recessive inheritance
  • Getting there: Can define some terms but confuses genotype and phenotype
  • Not yet: Cannot define the key genetic terms

Lesson 2: Punnett Squares

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Cross Question

Ask: "If two heterozygous parents (Bb) have children, what are the chances of having a child with white fur (bb)?" Students guess, then learn to work it out using a Punnett square.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Punnett squares: a diagram used to predict the outcomes of a genetic cross between two parents. Monohybrid cross (one gene): Example: both parents are Bb (heterozygous for fur colour)
B (from father)b (from father)
B (from mother)BBBb
b (from mother)Bbbb
Results:
  • Genotypic ratio: 1 BB : 2 Bb : 1 bb (1:2:1)
  • Phenotypic ratio: 3 brown : 1 white (3:1)
  • Probability of brown fur: 75% (3/4)
  • Probability of white fur: 25% (1/4)
Test cross: used to find the genotype of an organism showing the dominant phenotype. Cross it with a homozygous recessive (bb):
  • If BB × bb → all offspring are Bb (all dominant phenotype)
  • If Bb × bb → offspring are 1 Bb : 1 bb (half dominant, half recessive phenotype)
Worked Example

"In peas, tall (T) is dominant over short (t). A heterozygous tall plant (Tt) is crossed with a short plant (tt). Predict the offspring." Answer:

T (from parent 1)t (from parent 1)
t (from parent 2)Tttt
t (from parent 2)Tttt
Results: 2 Tt (tall) : 2 tt (short) = 1:1 ratio. 50% tall, 50% short.

Common Misconception

"A 3:1 ratio means exactly 3 brown to 1 white"

The 3:1 ratio is a probability — each offspring has a 75% chance of brown and 25% chance of white. In a small sample, the actual numbers may differ. The ratio becomes more accurate with larger numbers of offspring.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a step-by-step guide to completing a Punnett square: (1) Write parent genotypes, (2) Set up the grid, (3) Fill in alleles, (4) Identify genotypes and phenotypes. Practice with simple crosses first.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Practice dihybrid crosses (two genes). Research genetic disorders that follow Mendelian inheritance (e.g. cystic fibrosis, polydactyly).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Cross

Students complete a Punnett square for Bb × Bb and state the genotypic and phenotypic ratios. Check answers.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can complete Punnett squares accurately and predict genotypic and phenotypic ratios
  • Getting there: Can complete simple crosses but makes errors with ratios
  • Not yet: Cannot complete a Punnett square

Lesson 3: Inherited Disorders and Sex Determination

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Genetic Condition

Ask: "Why do some genetic conditions run in families?" Discuss that if both parents carry a recessive allele for a condition, there is a chance their children will inherit it.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Inherited genetic disorders:
  • Cystic fibrosis: a recessive disorder (cf). Affected individuals are homozygous recessive (cf cf). Carriers are heterozygous (CF cf) and do not show symptoms. If both parents are carriers: 25% chance of affected child, 50% chance of carrier, 25% unaffected.
  • Polydactyly: having extra fingers or toes. Caused by a DOMINANT allele (P). An individual with Pp or PP has extra digits. If one parent is heterozygous (Pp) and the other is normal (pp): 50% chance of polydactyly in each child.
Sex determination:
  • Sex is determined by sex chromosomes: XX = female, XY = male
  • Males produce two types of sperm: those carrying X and those carrying Y (50:50 ratio)
  • Females produce only eggs carrying X
  • Punnett square: X × X = XX (girl), X × Y = XY (boy). 50% chance of each.
Sex-linked genetic disorders:
  • Some genes are carried on the X chromosome (not the Y)
  • Haemophilia: a recessive disorder on the X chromosome. Males (XY) only need one affected X to have the disease. Females (XX) need two affected Xs. This is why haemophilia is much more common in males.
  • Colour blindness: also X-linked recessive, more common in males.
Worked Example

"Both parents are carriers for cystic fibrosis (Cc). What is the probability their child will have cystic fibrosis?" Answer:

Cc
CCCCc
cCccc
Probability of cc (cystic fibrosis): 1/4 = 25%. Probability of being a carrier (Cc): 2/4 = 50%. Probability of unaffected (CC): 1/4 = 25%.

Common Misconception

"If parents are carriers, their child will definitely have the condition"

Carriers (heterozygous) do not have the condition themselves. If both parents are carriers, there is only a 25% chance of having an affected child. Each pregnancy is an independent event with the same probability.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on cystic fibrosis inheritance: "If both parents carry one copy, 1 in 4 children will have it." Provide pre-made Punnett squares to complete. Simplify sex determination: "Father determines sex — X sperm = girl, Y sperm = boy."
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research genetic counselling: how are at-risk families helped to understand the probability of having an affected child? Discuss the ethics of genetic screening.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Probability Check

Give three genetic cross scenarios. Students calculate the probability of the offspring having the dominant and recessive phenotypes. Quick-fire answers.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can predict inheritance of genetic disorders and explain sex determination
  • Getting there: Can do simple genetic crosses but struggles with sex-linked inheritance
  • Not yet: Cannot predict inheritance patterns or explain sex determination

Homework Suggestions

  • Complete 10 Punnett square practice problems with answers
  • Research one genetic disorder and create a fact sheet: inheritance pattern, symptoms, and treatment
  • Draw a genetic diagram showing how colour blindness is inherited through a family
  • Explain why more males than females are colour blind using a Punnett square
