Lesson 2: Core Concepts: legal problem questions

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on legal problem questions. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Problem questions present a factual scenario and ask you to advise on the legal position — they test your ability to apply law to facts, not just recall legal rules
Key Fact: The ILAC method provides a clear structure: Issue (identify the legal question), Law (state the relevant legal rules), Application (apply the law to the facts), Conclusion (state the likely outcome)
Key Fact: Identify all the legal issues in the scenario — there are usually multiple issues, such as actus reus, mens rea and any defences in a criminal problem
Key Fact: State the law accurately — name the relevant statute or case, define the legal principle precisely and set out the elements that must be proved
Key Fact: Apply the law to the facts — use the specific details from the scenario to show how the legal rule would operate, explaining each step of your reasoning
Key Fact: Consider alternative arguments — acknowledge where the outcome is uncertain and explain what the prosecution or defence would argue

Extended question: Full-Mark Response How should you apply the ILAC method to a criminal law scenario involving assault? (15 marks) <div class="

When applying ILAC to a criminal law assault scenario, begin by identifying the Issue: the question is whether the defendant is liable for assault (or battery, ABH, GBH depending on the scenario). Next, state the Law: define the offence precisely. For assault under the Criminal Justice Act 1988, the actus reus is an act which causes the victim to apprehend immediate unlawful violence, and the mens rea is intention or recklessness as to causing that apprehension. For battery, the actus reus is the application of unlawful force and the mens rea is intention or recklessness. Cite the relevant case law — for example, Fagan v MPC confirms that battery requires unlawful force, and R v Ireland establishes that psychiatric harm can amount to bodily harm. Then apply the law to the facts: use the specific details from the scenario. If the defendant raised their fist and moved towards the victim, explain that this constitutes the actus reus of assault because the victim would apprehend immediate unlawful violence.
