Lesson 2: Core Concepts: law reform &amp; contemporary issues

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on law reform &amp; contemporary issues. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Law reform is necessary because society changes and the law must adapt to address new situations, correct injustices and keep pace with technology and social values
Key Fact: The Law Commission is an independent body established by the Law Commissions Act 1965 with a duty to keep the law under review and recommend reform
Key Fact: The Law Commission produces reports with draft Bills; however, implementation depends on Parliament — only around 70% of Law Commission recommendations are eventually implemented, often with significant delay
Key Fact: Parliamentary reform through Acts of Parliament is the primary method of law reform; Parliament can change any area of law and is not bound by previous legislation
Key Fact: Judges reform the law through the development of precedent — the Supreme Court and Court of Appeal can depart from previous decisions to update the law (e.g. the Supreme Court in Montgomery changed the law on informed consent)
Key Fact: The 1966 Practice Statement allowed the House of Lords (now Supreme Court) to depart from its own previous decisions when it appears right to do so

Extended question: Full-Mark Response How effective is the Law Commission in achieving law reform? (15 marks) <div class="

The Law Commission plays a vital role in identifying the need for law reform and producing well-researched recommendations, but its effectiveness is limited by its dependence on Parliament for implementation. The Commission's strengths are significant: it provides expert, independent analysis of defective or outdated law, consulting widely with legal professionals, interest groups and the public. Its reports are thorough and often accompanied by draft Bills that are ready for parliamentary consideration, saving legislative time. Many important reforms originated from Law Commission work, including the Fraud Act 2006, the Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007 and reforms to the law of bribery. However, the Commission's effectiveness is severely limited by the implementation gap. Only around 70% of recommendations are eventually implemented, and many take years to reach the statute book. The Commission has no power to compel Parliament to act, and government priorities may not align with the Commission's recommendations. Some reports have been left unimplemented for over a decade.
