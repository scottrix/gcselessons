Lesson 2: Core Concepts: legal evaluation skills

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on legal evaluation skills. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Legal evaluation requires assessing the strengths and weaknesses of a law, principle or proposal and reaching a supported judgement
Key Fact: A balanced argument presents points on both sides — for and against — before reaching a conclusion; one-sided answers cannot access top mark bands
Key Fact: Always support your points with specific legal examples, cases or statistics — vague or generalised statements score poorly
Key Fact: When evaluating a legal rule, consider: how effectively it achieves its purpose, whether it is clear and certain, whether it is fair and just, and whether there are gaps or inconsistencies
Key Fact: When evaluating a reform proposal, consider: whether it addresses the identified problem, whether it is practical and enforceable, whether it has unintended consequences, and whether it balances competing interests
Key Fact: A justified conclusion is one that follows logically from your analysis — it should not introduce new arguments or simply repeat one side

Extended question: Full-Mark Response What makes an effective evaluation in a GCSE Law essay? (15 marks) <div class="

An effective evaluation in a GCSE Law essay requires a balanced, evidence-based assessment that leads to a justified conclusion. The first essential element is balance: you must present arguments on both sides of the issue. For example, when evaluating the effectiveness of the Human Rights Act 1998, discuss both its strengths (such as incorporating Convention rights into domestic law and requiring public authorities to respect rights) and its weaknesses (such as the inability to strike down legislation and the limited scope of horizontal effect). The second element is specific legal evidence: every point should be supported by a relevant case, statute or example. Saying 'the HRA protects rights effectively' is weak; saying 'section 3 has been used to interpret legislation compatibly with Convention rights in cases such as Ghaidan v Godin-Mendoza' is strong. The third element is considering the law in practice: a law may look effective on paper but fail in reality. For instance, legal aid exists in theory but LASPO 2012 restrictions mean many people cannot access it in practice. Exam Tips: Always signpost your evaluation: 'A significant strength is...', 'However, a key weakness is...', 'On balance...' | Use the PEEL structure for paragraphs: Point, Evidence (legal example), Explanation, Link back to the question | Don't sit on the fence — reach a clear judgement, but make sure it is supported by your analysis | Include at least one specific case or statute in each paragraph — this demonstrates legal knowledge and supports your evaluation | Practise writing conclusions that summarise the key reasons for your judgement in two to three sentences
Common Errors: ✗ Writing a one-sided argument that only considers strengths or only weaknesses ✓ Evaluation requires balance — present both sides before reaching a conclusion; one-sided answers cannot access the highest mark bands ✗ Making generalised statements without supporting evidence ✓ Every evaluative point should be supported by a specific legal example, case, statute or statistic — vague claims such as 'the law is unfair' without explanation score poorly ✗ Introducing new arguments in the conclusion ✓ The conclusion should draw together and judge the arguments already made — it should not introduce new points that were not discussed in the body of the essay ✗ Confusing description with evaluation
