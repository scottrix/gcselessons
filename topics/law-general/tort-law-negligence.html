Lesson 2: Core Concepts: tort law &amp; negligence

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on tort law &amp; negligence. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: A tort is a civil wrong — the claimant sues the defendant for compensation, unlike criminal law where the state prosecutes
Key Fact: Negligence is the most common tort — it requires duty of care, breach of that duty, causation and resulting damage
Key Fact: Duty of care exists where the defendant owed a legal duty to the claimant — established by the Caparo three-stage test
Key Fact: The Caparo test requires: (1) foreseeability of harm, (2) proximity of relationship and (3) it is fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty
Key Fact: Breach of duty occurs where the defendant fell below the standard of the reasonable person — the objective test
Key Fact: The reasonable person takes reasonable precautions against foreseeable risks — professionals must meet the standard of their profession

Extended question: Full-Mark Response How far does the tort of negligence provide effective compensation for claimants? (15 marks) <div class="

The tort of negligence provides a framework for compensating those harmed by others' carelessness, but significant limitations mean that many claimants do not receive effective compensation. Where all four elements are established, the claimant can recover damages to put them in the position they would have been in had the negligence not occurred. This provides meaningful compensation for those with clear, foreseeable injuries caused by an identifiable defendant. Vicarious liability extends protection by holding employers liable for employees' negligence, ensuring that claimants can sue a party with the resources to pay. Contributory negligence, while reducing damages, still allows partially at-fault claimants to recover something. However, the negligence framework has major limitations. The duty of care requirement excludes claimants where the relationship is insufficiently proximate or where the courts consider it unfair to impose liability — this has been used to limit liability for omissions and economic loss.
