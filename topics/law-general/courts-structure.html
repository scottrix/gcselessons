Lesson 2: Core Concepts: courts &amp; court structure

Duration: 50 minutes

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on courts &amp; court structure. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Criminal courts deal with offences against the state; civil courts deal with disputes between individuals or organisations
Key Fact: The Magistrates' Court hears 95% of criminal cases — summary offences and either-way offences
Key Fact: The Crown Court hears indictable offences (serious crimes) and appeals from the Magistrates' Court
Key Fact: The High Court hears the most serious civil cases, divided into three divisions: Queen's Bench, Chancery and Family
Key Fact: The County Court handles most civil claims including contract disputes, tort and debt recovery
Key Fact: The Court of Appeal (Criminal and Civil Divisions) hears appeals on points of law and sentence

Extended question: Full-Mark Response How effective is the court hierarchy in delivering justice? (15 marks) <div class="

The court hierarchy is broadly effective in delivering justice because it ensures consistency, provides clear appeal routes and assigns cases to appropriate levels of formality and seriousness. The doctrine of precedent, which depends on the hierarchy, ensures that lower courts follow decisions of higher courts, creating predictability and fairness in the law. Appeal routes allow errors to be corrected — a defendant convicted unfairly in the Magistrates' Court can appeal to the Crown Court, and points of law can reach the Supreme Court for final determination. The division between summary, either-way and indictable offences means that minor cases are dealt with quickly and cheaply in the Magistrates' Court, while serious cases receive the full formality and safeguards of the Crown Court, including jury trial. Tribunals extend access to justice by providing specialist, cheaper and less formal procedures for particular disputes. However, the court hierarchy has significant weaknesses. Delays are a serious problem — Crown Court cases can take months to come to trial, denying justice to both defendants and victims. Exam Tips: Know the full court hierarchy for both criminal and civil cases | Use the correct names: Magistrates' Court (not 'magistrate court'), Crown Court, High Court | Explain the difference between summary, either-way and indictable offences | Tribunals are increasingly important — know their advantages over courts | ADR is often examined — know the three types and their benefits
Common Errors: ✗ Confusing criminal and civil court hierarchies ✓ Criminal: Magistrates' Court → Crown Court → Court of Appeal (Criminal) → Supreme Court. Civil: County Court/High Court → Court of Appeal (Civil) → Supreme Court ✗ Thinking the Magistrates' Court only deals with minor cases ✓ The Magistrates' Court hears 95% of all criminal cases and also handles some civil matters and youth cases ✗ Confusing tribunals with courts ✓ Tribunals are specialist bodies that are cheaper, less formal and have expertise in specific areas — they are not courts ✗ Forgetting the Youth Court as part of the criminal court system ✓ The Youth Court handles criminal cases for defendants aged 10-17 and has special procedures
