S6: Scatter Graphs and Correlation

Foundation Higher All Boards

Construct and interpret scatter graphs. Describe correlation (positive, negative, none, strong, weak). Draw and use lines of best fit for interpolation and extrapolation. Understand the difference between correlation and causation.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Plot and interpret scatter graphs for bivariate data
  • Describe correlation: positive, negative, none; strong, weak, moderate
  • Draw a line of best fit by eye (and understand least squares conceptually)
  • Use line of best fit for interpolation and extrapolation
  • Understand the dangers of extrapolation
  • Distinguish between correlation and causation
  • Identify outliers and discuss their effect

Prerequisites

  • Plotting coordinates (x, y)
  • Drawing straight line graphs (y = mx + c)
  • Understanding of variables (independent/dependent)
  • Gradient and intercept interpretation

Materials & Equipment

  • Graph paper / squared paper
  • Rulers, sharp pencils
  • Transparent ruler for line of best fit
  • Bivariate data sets (printed or digital)
  • Calculators for mean point check

Lesson 1: Plotting and Describing Correlation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Pattern Spotting

Show 4 scatter graphs: clear positive, clear negative, no pattern, curved. "What do you notice? Can you draw a line through the points? What would the line look like?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Bivariate data: Pairs of values (x, y) for each individual. x = independent/explanatory variable (horizontal axis), y = dependent/response variable (vertical axis).
Plotting: Choose appropriate scales. Label axes with variable names and units. Plot points accurately. No joining lines. Title the graph.
Correlation types:
• Positive: As x increases, y tends to increase. Points slope upward.
• Negative: As x increases, y tends to decrease. Points slope downward.
• None (no correlation): No discernible pattern. Points scattered randomly.
• Non-linear: Curved pattern (e.g. quadratic, exponential).
Strength:
• Strong: Points close to a straight line
• Moderate: Clear trend but more scatter
• Weak: Trend visible but points widely scattered

Outliers: Points far from the general pattern. Can affect correlation strength and line of best fit.
Worked Example: Describe Correlation

Scatter graph of height vs weight for 20 adults.
Description: "Positive correlation. As height increases, weight tends to increase. Moderate strength — points show clear upward trend but with some scatter. One outlier at (150 cm, 90 kg) — unusually heavy for height."

Examples of Correlation Types

Positive: Height vs weight, temperature vs ice cream sales, study time vs exam score.
Negative: Price vs demand, age vs reaction time, exercise vs resting heart rate.
None: Shoe size vs IQ, birthday month vs test score, hair colour vs favourite food.
Non-linear: Speed vs fuel efficiency (curved), time vs bacterial growth (exponential).

Common Misconception

"No correlation means no relationship at all."

No linear correlation. There could be a non-linear relationship (e.g. U-shaped). Always check the pattern visually.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide pre-scaled axes. Use large data points. Give correlation vocabulary bank: positive/negative/none, strong/moderate/weak. Practice with 5–6 points first.
Stretch:
Given a scatter graph, estimate the correlation coefficient r (conceptually: r ≈ 1, 0.7, 0, -0.5, -1). Sketch scatter graphs for given descriptions.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Correlation or Not?

Statements: "As temperature rises, coat sales fall" (negative). "Taller people have bigger feet" (positive). "People born in July are better at maths" (none — coincidence). Students classify.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Plots accurately, describes correlation with direction and strength, identifies outliers.
  • Getting there: Plots correctly but uses vague descriptions ("it goes up") or misses outliers.
  • Not yet: Plots axes swapped, joins points with lines, cannot describe pattern. Reteach coordinate plotting.

Lesson 2: Line of Best Fit and Prediction

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Draw the Line

Show a scatter graph with clear positive correlation. Ask students to draw a line that "best fits" the points. Compare lines. "What makes a good line? Should it go through any points? Through the origin?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Line of best fit: Straight line that best represents the trend. Drawn by eye (GCSE) or calculated by least squares (A-level).
Guidelines for drawing by eye:
1. Line should follow the general trend of the points
2. Roughly equal number of points above and below the line
3. Line should pass through the mean point (x̄, ȳ) — this is a useful check
4. Line does NOT need to go through the origin (unless context suggests it should)
5. Line does NOT need to go through any actual data points

Equation of line: y = mx + c. m = gradient (rate of change), c = y-intercept (value of y when x=0).
Interpolation: Predicting y for an x value within the range of the data. Reliable.
Extrapolation: Predicting y for an x value outside the range of the data. Unreliable — trend may not continue.

Mean point: (mean of x values, mean of y values). Line of best fit should pass through this point.
Worked Example: Line of Best Fit

Data: Hours studied (x) vs Test score (y)
x: 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12
y: 45, 55, 60, 65, 72, 75, 80, 85
Mean point: x̄ = 7, ȳ = 67.1
Draw line through (7, 67.1) following trend.
Gradient ≈ (85−45)/(12−2) = 40/10 = 4 marks per hour
Intercept ≈ 45 − 4×2 = 37 (or from graph: where x=0, y≈37)
Equation: y = 4x + 37 (approx)

Worked Example: Interpolation vs Extrapolation

Line: y = 4x + 37. Data range: x = 2 to 12.
Interpolation: Predict score for x = 7 hours. y = 4×7 + 37 = 65. Reliable (within range).
Extrapolation: Predict score for x = 20 hours. y = 4×20 + 37 = 117. Unreliable — max score likely 100, diminishing returns, model breaks down.

Line of best fit: y = mx + c
Gradient m = (change in y) / (change in x) — use two points on the line, not data points
Line should pass through mean point (x̄, ȳ)
Interpolation: x within data range → reliable
Extrapolation: x outside data range → unreliable

Common Misconception

"The line of best fit must go through the origin."

Only if the context implies y=0 when x=0 (e.g. distance vs time from start). Generally, the intercept represents the baseline value when x=0.

Differentiation

Support:
Use transparent ruler. Provide grid with mean point marked. Step-by-step: plot mean point, place ruler, adjust for balance, draw.
Stretch:
Compare line of best fit by eye vs calculator regression line. Calculate residuals (actual − predicted). Sum of residuals ≈ 0. Sum of squared residuals minimised.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Prediction Check

Given line y = 2.5x + 10, data x = 1–10. "Predict y for x = 5 (interpolation). Predict y for x = 20 (extrapolation). Which do you trust? Why?"

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Draws reasonable line of best fit, finds gradient and intercept, distinguishes interpolation/extrapolation.
  • Getting there: Draws line but doesn't use mean point, or uses data points for gradient instead of line points.
  • Not yet: Joins first and last points, or draws curve. Reteach: line represents trend, not connections.

Lesson 3: Correlation vs Causation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Spurious Correlations

Show: "Ice cream sales and drowning deaths correlate positively." "Nicholas Cage films and pool drownings correlate." Ask: "Does ice cream cause drowning? Does Nicholas Cage cause drownings? What's really happening?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Correlation ≠ Causation: Just because two variables correlate does not mean one causes the other.
Possible explanations for correlation:
1. Direct causation: x causes y (e.g. smoking → lung cancer)
2. Reverse causation: y causes x (e.g. illness → low income; or low income → illness? Both possible)
3. Common cause (confounding variable): z causes both x and y (e.g. temperature → ice cream sales AND drowning)
4. Coincidence: Spurious correlation (e.g. cheese consumption vs bedsheet deaths)
5. Feedback loop: x affects y and y affects x (e.g. predator-prey populations)

Establishing causation: Requires controlled experiments (RCTs), not just observational data. Bradford Hill criteria (beyond GCSE).

Outliers in scatter graphs: Can inflate or deflate correlation. Check if outlier is error or genuine. Consider analysing with and without outlier.
Worked Example: Identify Explanation

1. Positive correlation: Number of firefighters at a fire vs damage cost.
Explanation: Common cause — bigger fire → more firefighters AND more damage. Not: firefighters cause damage.
2. Negative correlation: Vitamin D levels vs depression rates.
Explanation: Could be direct (vitamin D affects mood), reverse (depression → less sunlight), or common cause (season). Need experiment.
3. Positive correlation: Shoe size vs reading ability in children.
Explanation: Common cause — age. Older children have bigger feet AND better reading.

Worked Example: Outlier Effect

Scatter graph of 10 points: strong positive correlation. Add 1 outlier far from trend.
Correlation appears weaker. Line of best fit pulled toward outlier. Gradient changes.
Decision: Investigate outlier. Data error? Remove. Genuine extreme? Keep but note. Report both analyses.

Common Misconception

"Strong correlation proves causation."

No matter how strong the correlation (r = 0.99), it never proves causation on its own. Only controlled experiments or strong theoretical mechanisms can establish causation.

Differentiation

Support:
Use "Because... but... so..." sentence stems: "Ice cream and drowning correlate because... but... so the real cause is..." Provide framework cards for 5 explanations.
Stretch:
Research "spurious correlations" website (tylervigen.com). Design an experiment to test causation for a correlational claim. Discuss: why can't we just do experiments for everything? (Ethics, cost, time).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Headline Critique

"Study shows chocolate eaters are thinner. Chocolate causes weight loss!" Students write 3 flaws: 1. Observational not experimental. 2. Reverse causation possible (thin people eat more chocolate). 3. Confounding variables (exercise, diet).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains correlation≠causation, identifies confounding variables, critiques causal claims from observational data.
  • Getting there: Knows the phrase but struggles to apply to specific examples, or only spots coincidence.
  • Not yet: Assumes correlation means causation. Reteach with multiple clear counterexamples.

Homework Suggestions

  • Collect bivariate data (e.g. hand span vs height for family/friends). Plot scatter graph, describe correlation, draw line of best fit, make predictions.
  • Find 3 real-world correlations in news/media. For each, suggest possible explanations (causation, reverse, common cause, coincidence).
  • Given a scatter graph with an outlier, draw line of best fit with and without outlier. Compare equations.
  • Past paper questions on scatter graphs, lines of best fit, and correlation vs causation.
