S5: Describing Populations

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand the difference between populations and samples, parameters and statistics. Use sample statistics to estimate population parameters. Introduction to capture-recapture method and limitations of inference.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish between population and sample, parameter and statistic
  • Understand that sample statistics estimate population parameters
  • Calculate and interpret the capture-recapture estimate for population size
  • Understand assumptions and limitations of capture-recapture
  • Recognise that different samples give different estimates (sampling variability)
  • Identify when inference is appropriate and when it is not

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of sampling methods (from S1)
  • Proportional reasoning (fractions, percentages)
  • Basic algebra for capture-recapture formula
  • Understanding of bias and representativeness

Materials & Equipment

  • Coloured counters/beans (two colours) for capture-recapture simulation
  • Opaque bags or containers
  • Calculators
  • Spreadsheets for simulation

Lesson 1: Populations, Samples, Parameters, Statistics

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

What Do We Know?

"The average height of all 14-year-olds in the UK is 162 cm." Ask: "How do we know this? Did someone measure every 14-year-old? What's the difference between 'all 14-year-olds' and 'the 500 we measured'?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Key definitions:
• Population — the entire group of interest (all individuals, items, events)
• Sample — a subset of the population selected for study
• Parameter — a numerical characteristic of a population (e.g. population mean μ, population proportion p, population standard deviation σ)
• Statistic — a numerical characteristic of a sample (e.g. sample mean x̄, sample proportion p̂, sample standard deviation s)

Notation: Population: μ, σ, p, N. Sample: x̄, s, p̂, n.
Inference: Using sample statistics to estimate population parameters. The sample mean x̄ estimates μ. The sample proportion p̂ estimates p.

Sampling variability: Different samples give different statistics. The sample mean varies from sample to sample. This is why we need sampling distributions (beyond GCSE) and confidence intervals (Higher extension).
Identify Parameter vs Statistic

1. "The mean height of all Year 9 students in England is 162.3 cm" → Parameter (μ)
2. "In a sample of 200 Year 9 students, the mean height was 161.8 cm" → Statistic (x̄)
3. "60% of voters support Party X" → Parameter (p)
4. "In a poll of 1000 voters, 58% supported Party X" → Statistic (p̂)

Worked Example: Sample Proportion

A sample of 400 students: 120 walk to school.
Sample proportion p̂ = 120/400 = 0.3 = 30%.
This estimates the population proportion p of all students who walk to school.
We don't know p exactly, but 0.3 is our best estimate from this sample.

Sample proportion: p̂ = (number with characteristic) / (sample size)
Sample mean: x̄ = Σx / n
Population parameter estimated by corresponding sample statistic

Common Misconception

"The sample statistic equals the population parameter."

The statistic is an estimate of the parameter. They are rarely exactly equal. The parameter is fixed (but unknown); the statistic varies from sample to sample.

Differentiation

Support:
Use physical population (bag of 100 coloured counters). Take samples of 10, calculate proportion of red. Repeat 5 times. Show variability. Vocabulary cards: population/sample, parameter/statistic.
Stretch:
Simulate sampling distribution: take 100 samples of size 30 from a known population (spreadsheet). Plot sample means. Discuss shape, centre, spread. Introduction to standard error.

Plenary (5 minutes)

News Headlines

"Poll shows 52% support for policy." Students identify: population (all voters), sample (polled voters), parameter (true support p), statistic (52% = p̂).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Correctly identifies population/sample, parameter/statistic in contexts. Uses notation correctly.
  • Getting there: Understands concepts but mixes up notation (e.g. uses μ for sample mean).
  • Not yet: Thinks sample statistic is the true parameter. Reteach with physical simulation.

Lesson 2: Capture-Recapture Method

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

How Many Fish?

"A lake has an unknown number of fish. How could you estimate it without catching them all?" Discuss ideas. Introduce: tag some, release, recapture, count tagged.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Capture-recapture (Lincoln-Petersen method):
1. Capture a sample, mark/tag them, release (M = number marked)
2. Wait for mixing
3. Recapture a second sample (n = second sample size)
4. Count how many in second sample are marked (m)
5. Assume proportion marked in second sample = proportion marked in population
m / n = M / N → N = (M × n) / m

Assumptions:
• Population is closed (no births, deaths, immigration, emigration)
• Marks don't fall off or affect survival/behaviour
• Complete mixing between capture and recapture
• All individuals equally likely to be caught
• No trap-shyness or trap-happiness

Limitations: Violations cause bias. E.g. if marked animals avoid traps, m is too small → N overestimated.
Worked Example: Capture-Recapture

Ecologist estimates woodlice population in a garden.
1. Captures 50 woodlice, marks with paint, releases. M = 50.
2. Waits 24 hours.
3. Captures 40 woodlice. n = 40.
4. Finds 8 are marked. m = 8.
Estimate N = (50 × 40) / 8 = 2000 / 8 = 250 woodlice.

Worked Example: Assumption Check

Same scenario. What if paint makes woodlice more visible to predators? Marked woodlice die → fewer marked in recapture → m smaller → N overestimated.
What if woodlice learn to avoid traps? Trap-shy → m smaller → N overestimated.
What if new woodlice born between captures? Population not closed → N overestimated.

Capture-recapture estimate: N = (M × n) / m
M = number marked and released
n = size of second sample
m = number of marked individuals in second sample

Common Misconception

"Capture-recapture gives the exact population size."

It gives an estimate. The true population size is unknown. The estimate has uncertainty (confidence intervals not covered at GCSE).

Differentiation

Support:
Physical simulation: bag with 100 beans (population). Student takes 20 (M), marks with pen, returns. Shakes. Takes 15 (n), counts marked (m). Calculates N. Repeat. Compare estimates.
Stretch:
Derive formula from proportion equality. Research Schnabel index (multiple recaptures). Discuss: why might N not be integer? What does rounding mean?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Design a Study

"Estimate the number of daisies on the school field." Students design capture-recapture: what to mark, how many, how to mix, assumptions to check.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Applies formula correctly, states assumptions, identifies violations and direction of bias.
  • Getting there: Uses formula but forgets assumptions, or cannot explain why bias occurs.
  • Not yet: Confuses M, n, m. Reteach with colour-coded example: first sample = blue, second sample = green, overlap = marked in both.

Lesson 3: Inference and Limitations

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Can We Generalise?

Scenarios: 1. Survey 50 Year 7s about bedtime → infer for all Year 7s? 2. Survey 50 Year 7s → infer for all secondary students? 3. Test 10 lightbulbs from factory → infer for all bulbs? 4. Ask friends about favourite game → infer for UK teens? Discuss representativeness.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
When can we infer?
• Sample must be representative (random, stratified, no bias)
• Sample size must be adequate (larger = more precise, but diminishing returns)
• Population must be well-defined

Sampling variability: Even with perfect random sample, statistic ≠ parameter. Different samples → different estimates.
Margin of error (intuitive): Sample proportion ± margin. For n=100, margin ≈ 10%. For n=1000, margin ≈ 3%. Formula: 1/√n (rough rule).
Confidence (Higher): "We are 95% confident the true proportion is between 45% and 55%." Not covered in detail at GCSE but conceptually important.

Inappropriate inference:
• Convenience sample → generalise to population
• Small sample → precise claims
• Different population → apply results (e.g. adult drug trial → children)
• Correlation → causation (leads to S6)
Worked Example: Margin of Error

Survey of 100 people: 60% support policy.
Rough margin of error ≈ 1/√100 = 1/10 = 10%.
Estimate: 50% to 70%.
Same survey, 1000 people: 60% support.
Margin ≈ 1/√1000 ≈ 3%.
Estimate: 57% to 63%. More precise.

Worked Example: Bad Inference

"A study of 20 marathon runners found they eat 3000 calories/day. Therefore all adults should eat 3000 calories/day."
Problems: Sample not representative (marathon runners ≠ general population). Small sample. Different population. Causation implied.

Common Misconception

"A larger sample is always representative."

A large biased sample (e.g. 10,000 self-selected online respondents) is worse than a small random sample (e.g. 100 randomly selected). Method matters more than size.

Differentiation

Support:
Checklist for valid inference: Random? Representative? Adequate size? Same population? Use traffic light cards (green/amber/red) for scenarios.
Stretch:
Calculate approximate margin of error for different sample sizes. Research "confidence interval for a proportion" formula: p̂ ± 1.96√(p̂(1−p̂)/n). Discuss why 1.96.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Critique the Claim

"A shampoo company surveys 500 customers at a hair salon. 90% say their hair is healthier. Ad claims '90% of women have healthier hair.'" Students list flaws.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Evaluates whether inference is valid, identifies sampling bias, understands variability.
  • Getting there: Spots obvious bias but misses subtle issues (e.g. population mismatch).
  • Not yet: Accepts any sample statistic as true for population. Reteach: sample ≠ population.

Homework Suggestions

  • Capture-recapture simulation: use a bag of 200 counters, sample 30, mark, recapture 25, estimate. Repeat 5 times. Calculate mean estimate.
  • Find a news article with a statistic (e.g. "40% of Britons think..."). Identify population, sample, parameter, statistic. Critique the sampling method.
  • Design a capture-recapture study for a local population (birds, insects, pets). List assumptions and how you'd check them.
