S4: Central Tendency and Spread

Foundation Higher All Boards

Calculate and interpret measures of central tendency (mean, median, mode) and measures of spread (range, interquartile range, standard deviation). Compare distributions using these statistics.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 4
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Calculate mean, median, mode from raw data and frequency tables
  • Calculate range and interquartile range (IQR)
  • Understand when to use each measure of central tendency
  • Calculate standard deviation and variance (Higher)
  • Compare two distributions using measures of centre and spread
  • Identify outliers and understand their effect on statistics

Prerequisites

  • Ordering numbers, finding middle value
  • Basic arithmetic: addition, division, squaring
  • Frequency tables and Σ notation
  • Square roots (for standard deviation)

Materials & Equipment

  • Calculators (with statistics mode if available)
  • Data sets (printed or digital)
  • Whiteboards for quick calculations
  • Spreadsheet software for checking standard deviation

Lesson 1: Mean, Median, Mode from Raw Data and Tables

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Three Averages

Data: 2, 3, 3, 5, 7, 8, 50. Mean = 11.1, Median = 5, Mode = 3. "Which best represents the 'typical' value? Why is mean so high?" (Outlier 50).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Mean: Σx / n. Uses all data. Sensitive to outliers. Best for symmetric distributions.
Median: Middle value when ordered. Position (n+1)/2. Robust to outliers. Best for skewed distributions.
Mode: Most frequent value. Can have 0, 1, or multiple modes. Only measure for nominal data. Not affected by outliers.

From frequency table:
• Mean = Σ(f×x) / Σf
• Median: find cumulative frequency, locate (n+1)/2 position
• Mode: value with highest frequency

Choosing: Symmetric → mean. Skewed → median. Nominal → mode. Bimodal → report both modes or use median.
Worked Example: Frequency Table

Score | Freq (f) | f×x | Cum. Freq
1 | 2 | 2 | 2
2 | 5 | 10 | 7
3 | 8 | 24 | 15
4 | 6 | 24 | 21
5 | 4 | 20 | 25
Total | 25 | 80 |
Mean = 80/25 = 3.2
Median position = 13th value → falls in score 3 (cum freq 7 to 15). Median = 3.
Mode = 3 (highest frequency 8).

Mean = Σx / n (raw data) or Σ(fx) / Σf (frequency table)
Median position = (n + 1) / 2
Mode = value with highest frequency

Common Misconception

"The median is the middle number in the list."

Only if the list is ordered. Always order first. For even n, median is the mean of the two middle values.

Differentiation

Support:
Use small data sets (n < 10). Provide ordered lists. Use counters to physically find middle. Frequency table template with f×x column.
Stretch:
Given mean of 5 numbers is 12, and four numbers are 8, 10, 15, 14. Find the fifth. Create a data set with mean=10, median=8, mode=5.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Which Average?

Scenarios: "Average salary in a company with one billionaire owner", "Most common shoe size sold", "Typical house price in a street". Students choose mean/median/mode and justify.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Calculates all three from raw data and tables, chooses appropriate measure for context.
  • Getting there: Calculates correctly but struggles to justify choice, or forgets to order for median.
  • Not yet: Confuses median position formula, or divides by wrong n for mean from table. Reteach with visual aids.

Lesson 2: Range and Interquartile Range

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Spread Matters

Two classes, both mean test score 70%. Class A: all scores 65–75. Class B: scores 20–95. "Same mean, very different. What statistics capture this?" Range, IQR.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Range: Maximum − minimum. Simple but uses only two values, sensitive to outliers.
Interquartile Range (IQR): Q3 − Q1. Measures spread of middle 50%. Robust to outliers.
Quartiles: Q1 = median of lower half (25th percentile), Q3 = median of upper half (75th percentile).
Finding quartiles (raw data): Order data. Find median (Q2). Split into lower/upper halves. Find median of each half.
For odd n: Exclude median from halves. For even n: split at middle.

From frequency table/cumulative frequency: Use positions n/4 and 3n/4 (or (n+1)/4, 3(n+1)/4). Interpolate if needed.
Worked Example: Quartiles and IQR

Data: 4, 7, 8, 10, 12, 14, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25 (n=11)
Median (Q2) = 6th value = 14
Lower half: 4, 7, 8, 10, 12 → Q1 = 3rd value = 8
Upper half: 15, 18, 20, 22, 25 → Q3 = 3rd value = 20
IQR = 20 − 8 = 12
Range = 25 − 4 = 21

Worked Example: Outlier Effect

Data: 10, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 20. Range = 10, IQR = 6 (Q1=12, Q3=18).
Add outlier 50: 10, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 20, 50. Range = 40 (×4), IQR = 6 (unchanged).
Mean: 14.875 → 19.375. Median: 15 → 15.5. Median and IQR robust.

Range = Maximum − Minimum
IQR = Q3 − Q1
Q1 position = (n + 1) / 4
Q3 position = 3(n + 1) / 4

Common Misconception

"Range and IQR measure the same thing."

Range measures total spread (min to max). IQR measures spread of middle 50% (Q1 to Q3). Range is sensitive to outliers; IQR is not.

Differentiation

Support:
Use small odd data sets. Provide step-by-step quartile finder sheet. Visual: fold paper strip at median, then fold halves for quartiles.
Stretch:
Compare methods for finding quartiles (Tukey vs M&M vs interpolated). Research why different methods give slightly different Q1/Q3.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Box Plot Preview

Sketch a box plot from min, Q1, median, Q3, max. "The box width = IQR. Whiskers = range (roughly). Outliers = points beyond whiskers."

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Calculates range and IQR correctly, identifies outliers' effect, chooses appropriate spread measure.
  • Getting there: Finds range correctly but makes errors splitting data for quartiles (especially even n).
  • Not yet: Confuses Q1/Q3 with min/max. Reteach: median splits data, quartiles split halves.

Lesson 3: Standard Deviation and Variance (Higher)

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Deviation from Mean

Data: 4, 6, 8, 10, 12. Mean = 8. Deviations: -4, -2, 0, 2, 4. Sum = 0 (always). "How to get a single measure of spread?" Square deviations → mean of squares → square root.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Variance (σ²): Average squared deviation from mean. σ² = Σ(x − μ)² / n
Standard deviation (σ): Square root of variance. σ = √[Σ(x − μ)² / n]
Alternative formula: σ = √[Σx²/n − μ²] (easier for calculators)
Sample vs population: GCSE uses population formula (divide by n). A-level uses sample formula (divide by n−1).
Units: SD has same units as data. Variance has units².
Interpretation: ~68% of data within 1 SD of mean (normal distribution). Higher SD = more spread.
Worked Example: Standard Deviation

Data: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. Mean = 6.
Deviations: -4, -2, 0, 2, 4
Squared: 16, 4, 0, 4, 16. Sum = 40.
Variance = 40/5 = 8
SD = √8 = 2.83

Worked Example: Frequency Table

Score x | Freq f | fx | fx²
1 | 2 | 2 | 2
2 | 5 | 10 | 20
3 | 8 | 24 | 72
4 | 6 | 24 | 96
5 | 4 | 20 | 100
Total | 25 | 80 | 290
Mean = 80/25 = 3.2
Σx²/n = 290/25 = 11.6
Variance = 11.6 − 3.2² = 11.6 − 10.24 = 1.36
SD = √1.36 = 1.17

Population standard deviation: σ = √[Σ(x − μ)² / n]
Alternative: σ = √[Σx²/n − (Σx/n)²]
Variance = σ²

Common Misconception

"Standard deviation is the average distance from the mean."

It's the root mean square distance, not the mean absolute distance. Squaring gives more weight to larger deviations.

Differentiation

Support:
Use calculator statistics mode (SD/σn). Provide table template with fx² column. Focus on interpretation over calculation.
Stretch:
Prove Σ(x−μ)² = Σx² − nμ². Show that adding a constant to all data doesn't change SD; multiplying by k multiplies SD by |k|. Compare SD of two data sets with same mean.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Calculator Check

Students enter data into calculator statistics mode, read off mean and σn. Compare with manual calculation.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Calculates SD and variance correctly (manual and calculator), interprets in context.
  • Getting there: Uses calculator correctly but struggles with manual formula, or confuses Σx² with (Σx)².
  • Not yet: Forgets to square root for SD, or uses n−1. Reteach: GCSE uses n.

Lesson 4: Comparing Distributions

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Two Box Plots

Show box plots for Class A and Class B test scores. "Compare the distributions. What's similar? What's different? Which class did better? Which is more consistent?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Comparison framework:
1. Centre: Compare means/medians. "On average, Class A scored higher."
2. Spread: Compare ranges/IQRs/SDs. "Class B's scores were more spread out."
3. Shape: Symmetric, skewed, bimodal (from histograms).
4. Outliers: Any extreme values?
5. Context: What does this mean? "Class A more consistent, Class B has wider ability range."

Standardised scores (Higher): z = (x − μ) / σ. Compare values from different distributions.

Exam language: "Compare the distributions" → must mention at least one measure of centre AND one measure of spread, in context.
Worked Example: Comparison

Class A: mean 72, median 73, IQR 12, range 35
Class B: mean 68, median 70, IQR 20, range 50
Comparison: "Class A performed better on average (mean 72 vs 68). Class A's scores were more consistent (IQR 12 vs 20, range 35 vs 50). Both distributions slightly negatively skewed (mean < median)."

Worked Example: Standardised Score

Test 1: mean 60, SD 10. Student scores 75. z = (75−60)/10 = 1.5
Test 2: mean 50, SD 5. Student scores 58. z = (58−50)/5 = 1.6
Student did relatively better on Test 2 (1.6 SD above mean vs 1.5).

Common Misconception

"Class A is better because their mean is higher."

Must compare BOTH centre and spread. A class with higher mean but huge spread might have more failing students. Context matters.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide comparison writing frame: "On average... [centre]. The spread... [spread]. This means... [context]." Use pre-drawn box plots.
Stretch:
Given summary statistics for 3 groups, write a full comparison report. Research standardised scores in exam grading (UMS).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exam Question

"The box plots show the heights of Year 7 and Year 11 students. Compare the distributions." Students write 3-mark answer: centre, spread, context.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Makes valid comparisons using centre and spread, uses correct terminology, interprets in context.
  • Getting there: Mentions centre and spread but not in context, or only compares one aspect.
  • Not yet: Only describes one distribution, doesn't compare. Reteach comparison language.

Homework Suggestions

  • Collect two data sets (e.g. heights of boys/girls). Calculate mean, median, range, IQR, SD. Write comparison.
  • Given summary statistics for 3 classes, determine which class has most consistent performance.
  • Past paper questions on comparing box plots and distributions.
  • Research: how are standardised scores used in GCSE grading (UMS)?
