S1: Sampling

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand different sampling methods, their advantages and limitations, and how to select appropriate samples to represent a population.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define population, sample, census, and sampling frame
  • Describe and apply random sampling, systematic sampling, stratified sampling, quota sampling, and opportunity sampling
  • Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of each sampling method
  • Calculate sample sizes for stratified sampling (Higher)
  • Identify sources of bias in sampling

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of what data is and why we collect it
  • Basic probability concepts (randomness)
  • Ability to calculate percentages and fractions

Materials & Equipment

  • Random number generator (calculator, spreadsheet, or random number tables)
  • Printed sampling frames (e.g. class lists, postcode directories)
  • Coloured counters or cards for physical sampling demonstrations
  • Spreadsheet software for stratified sampling calculations

Lesson 1: Types of Sampling

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Why Sample?

Ask: "The school wants to know what students think about the new canteen menu. Should they ask all 1,200 students or just some? How many? How do they choose?" Discuss census vs sample.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Key terms:
• Population — the whole group you want to study
• Census — collecting data from every member of the population
• Sample — a subset of the population used to represent the whole
• Sampling frame — a list of all members of the population
• Sampling unit — an individual member of the population

Sampling methods:
1. Simple random sampling — every member has an equal chance of being selected. Use random number generator. Advantage: unbiased. Disadvantage: needs complete sampling frame, may not represent subgroups.
2. Systematic sampling — select every kth member from an ordered list (k = population size / sample size). Advantage: simple, quick. Disadvantage: bias if list has hidden pattern.
3. Stratified sampling — population divided into strata (subgroups), then random sample from each stratum proportional to size. Advantage: represents all subgroups. Disadvantage: needs population data on strata, more complex.
4. Quota sampling — researcher selects people to fill quotas for each subgroup. Non-random. Advantage: quick, cheap. Disadvantage: selection bias, not representative.
5. Opportunity (convenience) sampling — select whoever is available. Advantage: easiest. Disadvantage: highly biased, not representative.
Worked Example: Stratified Sampling

A school has 300 students: 120 Year 7, 90 Year 8, 90 Year 9. A sample of 50 is needed. Year 7: (120/300) × 50 = 20 students. Year 8: (90/300) × 50 = 15 students. Year 9: (90/300) × 50 = 15 students. Then random sample within each year.

Stratified sample size for each stratum = (stratum size / population size) × total sample size
Always round to whole numbers, adjusting so total matches required sample size.

Common Misconception

"Random sampling means haphazard or asking whoever you meet."

Random sampling has a precise meaning: every member of the population has an equal, known probability of being selected. Haphazard selection introduces bias.

Differentiation

Support:
Use physical demonstration: put 20 red and 10 blue counters in a bag (strata). Draw samples. Compare with proportional stratified sampling. Provide a template for stratified sampling calculations.
Stretch:
Research cluster sampling and multi-stage sampling. When are they used? Compare with stratified sampling for a national survey.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Method Match

Give scenarios (e.g. "survey shoppers at a mall on Saturday", "select 100 students from school register using random numbers"). Students identify the sampling method and state one advantage/disadvantage.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Names all five methods, explains how each works, and evaluates pros/cons.
  • Getting there: Knows methods but confuses systematic and stratified, or misses key advantages/disadvantages.
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between random and non-random methods. Reteach with clear examples of bias.

Lesson 2: Stratified Sampling Calculations (Higher)

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Proportional Reasoning

Quick fire: "If 30% of a population are female, how many females in a sample of 50? 200? 17?" Practice proportional calculations.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Recap stratified sampling. Focus on calculation steps:
1. Identify strata and their sizes
2. Calculate total population
3. For each stratum: (stratum size ÷ population size) × sample size
4. Round to nearest whole number
5. Check total matches required sample size; adjust if needed (usually adjust largest stratum)

Key point: Stratified sampling ensures the sample reflects the population structure. It reduces sampling variability for subgroup estimates.

Higher tier extension: Discuss when strata sizes are not known — pilot survey, or use previous census data. Mention that stratification variables should be relevant to the study (e.g. age for health survey, income for spending survey).
Worked Example: Adjusting Rounding

Population: 450 (A: 150, B: 180, C: 120). Sample size: 50.
A: (150/450)×50 = 16.67 → 17
B: (180/450)×50 = 20.00 → 20
C: (120/450)×50 = 13.33 → 13
Total = 17+20+13 = 50 ✓ No adjustment needed.

Worked Example: Adjustment Needed

Population: 400 (X: 130, Y: 150, Z: 120). Sample size: 30.
X: (130/400)×30 = 9.75 → 10
Y: (150/400)×30 = 11.25 → 11
Z: (120/400)×30 = 9.00 → 9
Total = 10+11+9 = 30 ✓

Common Misconception

"Always round each stratum calculation to the nearest whole number."

After rounding, the total may not equal the required sample size. You must adjust (usually the largest stratum) so the sum matches exactly.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a structured worksheet with columns for stratum size, calculation, rounded value, and adjustment. Use simpler numbers (divisible totals).
Stretch:
Given a sample and population data, work backwards: was this sample likely selected via stratified sampling? Chi-squared test introduction (optional).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Spot the Error

Show a stratified sampling calculation with a deliberate rounding error (total ≠ sample size). Students identify and fix it.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Correctly calculates stratum sample sizes, handles rounding adjustments.
  • Getting there: Calculates proportions correctly but forgets to check/adjust total.
  • Not yet: Confuses stratum size with population size. Reteach the formula with visual aids.

Lesson 3: Bias and Sampling in Context

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Biased Headlines

Show: "9 out of 10 cat owners prefer Whiskas!" Ask: How were the 10 cat owners chosen? What's wrong? (Small sample, likely biased selection).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Sources of bias:
• Selection bias — sampling method systematically excludes some groups (e.g. landline phone survey misses mobile-only households)
• Non-response bias — people who respond differ from those who don't (e.g. busy people, unhappy customers)
• Response bias — question wording or interviewer influences answers
• Undercoverage bias — sampling frame doesn't include all population (e.g. electoral register misses homeless)

Evaluating a sampling plan:
1. Is the sampling frame complete?
2. Is the method random or non-random?
3. Is the sample size adequate?
4. Are subgroups represented?
5. Could non-response be a problem?

Real-world contexts: Opinion polls, quality control in manufacturing, medical trials, ecological surveys, market research.
Case Study: 1948 US Election

The Chicago Tribune headline "Dewey Defeats Truman" was based on a telephone poll. In 1948, telephones were more common in wealthier (Republican-leaning) households. The sampling frame was biased. Truman won.

Improving a Survey

"A student wants to survey 50 students about homework. They ask their friends at lunch." Problems: opportunity sampling, friendship group bias, small sample. Better: stratified random sample by year group using school register.

Common Misconception

"A larger sample is always better."

A large biased sample is worse than a small random sample. 10,000 responses from a self-selecting online poll are less reliable than 500 from a stratified random sample.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a bias checklist worksheet. Match bias types to scenarios. Use colour-coding: red = bias, green = good practice.
Stretch:
Design a sampling plan for a real context (e.g. "investigate local air quality"). Justify every choice: population, frame, method, sample size, bias mitigation.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

"A researcher wants to estimate the average height of oak trees in a 100-hectare forest. Describe a suitable sampling method. Explain why your method reduces bias." Students write a 3-mark answer.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Identifies bias types, designs appropriate sampling plans, justifies choices.
  • Getting there: Recognises bias in given scenarios but struggles to design unbiased plans from scratch.
  • Not yet: Confuses bias with random variation. Reteach: bias is systematic, not chance.

Homework Suggestions

  • Design a stratified sampling plan for a survey of 200 students across 4 year groups (provide population data).
  • Find a real survey reported in the news. Identify the sampling method used and critique it for bias.
  • Complete a worksheet: given 5 scenarios, choose the best sampling method and justify.
  • Use a spreadsheet to simulate random sampling from a dataset of 1000 items. Compare sample statistics to population parameters.
