C22: Reversible Reactions and Equilibrium

Foundation All Boards

Describe reversible reactions and dynamic equilibrium, and explain how changing conditions affects the position of equilibrium.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define reversible reactions and give examples
  • Explain dynamic equilibrium and how changing conditions affects the position of equilibrium
  • Apply Le Chatelier's principle to predict the effect of changes on equilibrium

Prerequisites

  • Rate of Reaction (C21) — collision theory
  • Exothermic and Endothermic (C19) — energy changes

Lesson 1: Reversible Reactions

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Two-Way Street

Imagine a road where cars can travel in both directions. At any moment, some cars go A→B and some go B→A. If the flow is equal in both directions, the number of cars at A and B stays constant. How is this like a reversible reaction?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Reversible reactions are reactions where the products can react to reform the original reactants. They are shown using the ⇌ symbol.

Example: Haber process
N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)
• Forward reaction: nitrogen + hydrogen → ammonia (exothermic)
• Reverse reaction: ammonia → nitrogen + hydrogen (endothermic)

Example: thermal decomposition of ammonium chloride
NH₄Cl(s) ⇌ NH₃(g) + HCl(g)
• Forward: solid → gases (endothermic — absorbs heat)
• Reverse: gases → solid (exothermic — releases heat)

Dynamic equilibrium:
• In a reversible reaction, equilibrium is reached when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction
• Both reactions are still happening (hence 'dynamic') — but the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant
• Equilibrium can only be reached in a closed system (nothing escapes)

Le Chatelier's principle:
If a change is made to a system at equilibrium, the system will adjust to partially oppose the change and restore a new equilibrium.

Effect of changing conditions:
• Temperature: increasing temperature favours the endothermic direction (absorbs the extra heat). Decreasing temperature favours the exothermic direction.
• Concentration: increasing concentration of a reactant favours the forward reaction. Increasing concentration of a product favours the reverse reaction.
• Pressure (gases only): increasing pressure favours the side with fewer moles of gas. Decreasing pressure favours the side with more moles of gas.
• Catalysts: speed up both forward and reverse reactions equally — they do NOT change the position of equilibrium, only how quickly equilibrium is reached.
Haber process and equilibrium

N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (forward is exothermic)
To maximise ammonia yield:
• Low temperature: favours exothermic (forward) direction → more NH₃. But too low = too slow.
• Compromise: ~450°C (fast enough rate, reasonable yield)
• High pressure: 4 moles gas → 2 moles gas, so high pressure favours forward → more NH₃
• Remove NH₃ as it forms: shifts equilibrium forward → more NH₃ made

Ammonium chloride equilibrium

NH₄Cl(s) ⇌ NH₃(g) + HCl(g) (forward is endothermic)
Heat the solid: equilibrium shifts forward (endothermic direction) → more gas produced, tube fills with white clouds. Cool the tube: equilibrium shifts back (exothermic direction) → white solid reappears on cool part.

Reversible reaction: A + B ⇌ C + D

Dynamic equilibrium: rate forward = rate reverse (concentrations constant)

Le Chatelier's principle: system opposes changes
• ↑ Temperature → favours endothermic direction
• ↑ Pressure → favours side with fewer gas moles
• ↑ Concentration of reactant → favours forward reaction
• Catalyst: no effect on position, only speed

Common Misconception

At equilibrium, the forward and reverse reactions stop.

At equilibrium, both reactions are still happening at the same rate. The concentrations of reactants and products remain constant because they are being formed and consumed at equal rates. This is why it is called 'dynamic' equilibrium.

Differentiation

Support:
Use the seesaw analogy: equilibrium is a balanced seesaw. Push one side (add reactant) and the seesaw tips, but it adjusts back. Use physical demonstrations with the ammonium chloride equilibrium.
Stretch:
Research: why is the Haber process one of the most important industrial reactions in the world? What is ammonia used for?

Plenary (5 minutes)

For the reaction A + B ⇌ C + D (forward is exothermic), predict the effect of: (a) increasing temperature, (b) increasing pressure (if A, B, C, D are all gases and there are 2 moles on each side), (c) adding more A.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains reversible reactions and dynamic equilibrium, and applies Le Chatelier's principle to predict changes.
  • Getting there: Knows what dynamic equilibrium means but cannot always predict the effect of changes.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain what equilibrium means. Reteach with the seesaw analogy and the ammonium chloride demonstration.

Lesson 2: Factors Affecting Equilibrium

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Le Chatelier Challenge

For N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2NO₂(g) (forward is endothermic, colourless → brown): what colour change would you observe if you heated the equilibrium mixture? Why?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Detailed application of Le Chatelier's principle:

1. Temperature changes:
• Increase temperature → equilibrium shifts in endothermic direction (to absorb the extra heat)
• Decrease temperature → equilibrium shifts in exothermic direction (to release heat)
• Note: changing temperature also changes the value of K (equilibrium constant)

2. Concentration changes:
• Add more reactant → shifts forward (uses up the extra reactant)
• Add more product → shifts reverse (uses up the extra product)
• Remove product → shifts forward (makes more product to replace it)
• Note: concentration changes do NOT change the value of K

3. Pressure changes (gases only):
• Increase pressure → shifts to side with fewer moles of gas (fewer particles = less pressure)
• Decrease pressure → shifts to side with more moles of gas
• If equal moles on both sides → pressure change has no effect
• Note: pressure changes do NOT change the value of K

4. Catalysts:
• Speed up both forward and reverse reactions equally
• Equilibrium is reached faster but the position is unchanged
• No effect on yield

Industrial applications:
• Haber process (ammonia): N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃. High pressure (200 atm), moderate temperature (450°C), iron catalyst. Remove NH₃ as formed.
• Contact process (sulfuric acid): 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃. Exothermic forward reaction. 450°C, 2 atm, vanadium(V) oxide catalyst.
N₂O₄ ⇌ NO₂ equilibrium

N₂O₄ is colourless, NO₂ is brown. Heating shifts equilibrium forward (endothermic) → more NO₂ → darker brown colour. Cooling shifts equilibrium backward (exothermic) → more N₂O₄ → lighter/colourless. You can see the colour change!

Contact process

2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ (exothermic forward). Low temperature favours forward but is too slow. Compromise: 450°C. High pressure favours forward (3 moles → 2 moles) but 2 atm is enough. V₂O₅ catalyst speeds it up.

Le Chatelier's principle summary:
Change → System adjusts to oppose change → New equilibrium

• ↑ T → favours endothermic direction
• ↓ T → favours exothermic direction
• ↑ Concentration of reactant → shifts forward
• ↑ Concentration of product → shifts reverse
• ↑ Pressure → favours side with fewer gas moles
• Catalyst → no effect on position, only speed

Common Misconception

Increasing pressure always increases the yield of products.

Increasing pressure only favours the side with fewer moles of gas. If there are equal moles of gas on both sides, pressure change has no effect on the position of equilibrium. For example, H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI has equal moles on both sides — pressure change has no effect.

Differentiation

Support:
Create a decision flowchart: What change? (T, P, concentration, catalyst) → Which direction is affected? → Which side is favoured?
Stretch:
Calculate the percentage yield of ammonia in the Haber process at different temperatures. Why is 450°C a compromise?

Plenary (5 minutes)

For each change, predict the effect on equilibrium: CO(g) + H₂O(g) ⇌ CO₂(g) + H₂(g) (forward is exothermic, equal moles on each side). (a) Increase temperature, (b) increase pressure, (c) add more CO, (d) add a catalyst.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Applies Le Chatelier's principle accurately to all types of changes and explains industrial compromises.
  • Getting there: Predicts the effect of temperature and concentration changes but struggles with pressure.
  • Not yet: Cannot apply Le Chatelier's principle. Reteach with the seesaw analogy and worked examples.

Homework Suggestions

  • Practise applying Le Chatelier's principle to 10 different equilibrium reactions
  • Research the Haber process: why is 450°C used instead of a lower temperature?
  • Draw a flowchart showing how to predict the effect of changes on equilibrium
