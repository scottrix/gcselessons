C21: Rate of Reaction

Foundation All Boards

Describe what affects the rate of reactions using collision theory, and explain how to measure rates experimentally.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define rate of reaction and describe how to measure it
  • Explain how concentration, temperature, surface area and catalysts affect rate using collision theory
  • Interpret rate graphs and calculate rates from experimental data

Prerequisites

  • Atoms and Elements (C1) — particle model
  • Exothermic and Endothermic (C19) — activation energy

Lesson 1: What Affects Rate?

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Dissolving Sugar

Which dissolves faster: a sugar cube or granulated sugar in the same amount of hot water? Why?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Rate of reaction = how fast reactants turn into products. Measured as product formed per unit time (e.g. cm³/s) or reactant used up per unit time (e.g. g/s).

Collision theory: for a reaction to happen, particles must:
1. Collide with each other
2. With enough energy (≥ activation energy)
3. In the correct orientation

Factors affecting rate:

1. Concentration (solutions):
• Higher concentration → more particles per unit volume → more frequent collisions → faster rate

2. Temperature:
• Higher temperature → particles move faster → more frequent collisions AND more particles have energy ≥ activation energy → much faster rate
• 10°C increase → roughly doubles the rate

3. Surface area (solids):
• Larger surface area → more particles exposed → more frequent collisions → faster rate
• Powder reacts faster than lumps (same mass, more surface area)

4. Catalyst:
• Provides an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy → more particles have enough energy → faster rate
• Catalyst is not used up in the reaction
• Does not change the amount of product formed (only how fast)
Marble chips and acid

CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. Powdered CaCO₃ reacts much faster than chips because more particles are exposed. The same total amount of CO₂ is produced eventually, but the powder produces it much quicker.

Why temperature has a bigger effect

Increasing concentration only increases collision frequency. Increasing temperature increases both collision frequency AND the proportion of particles with enough energy to react. This is why temperature has a much bigger effect on rate.

Collision theory: particles must collide with enough energy (≥ Ea) and correct orientation

Rate ∝ concentration (for solutions)
Rate ∝ surface area (for solids)
Rate increases with temperature (more than proportionally)
Catalysts lower Ea → increase rate without being used up

Common Misconception

A catalyst makes more product.

A catalyst only speeds up the reaction — it does not change the total amount of product formed. The same amount of product is made, just faster.

Differentiation

Support:
Use physical demonstrations. Drop magnesium ribbon in acid (slow) vs magnesium powder (fast). Let children observe the rate difference.
Stretch:
Research: what is the difference between a homogeneous and heterogeneous catalyst? Give an example of each.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Explain using collision theory why: (a) food goes off faster in warm weather, (b) powdered medicine works faster than tablets, (c) a catalyst speeds up a reaction.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains all four factors using collision theory, and can predict the effect on rate.
  • Getting there: Knows that higher concentration/temperature/surface area increases rate but cannot explain using collision theory.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain what affects rate. Reteach with physical demonstrations.

Lesson 2: Measuring Rate of Reaction

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

How Fast?

How would you measure how fast a reaction produces gas? What equipment would you need?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Methods for measuring rate of reaction:

1. Gas volume (gas syringe):
• Collect gas produced using a gas syringe
• Record volume at regular time intervals
• Plot volume vs time graph

2. Mass loss (balance):
• Place reaction flask on a balance
• Record mass at regular intervals
• Mass decreases as gas escapes

3. Precipitation:
• Place flask over a cross on paper — time until cross disappears
• Faster rate = shorter time

Reading rate graphs:
• Gradient of the curve at any point = rate at that moment
• Steeper gradient = faster rate
• Flat section = reaction has stopped
• Draw a tangent to the curve at the point of interest
• Rate = change in y ÷ change in x (of the tangent)

Initial rate: the rate at the very start (gradient of tangent at time = 0). Often the fastest rate because reactant concentration is highest.
Measuring CO₂ production

CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. Set up conical flask with HCl, add marble chips, quickly attach bung with delivery tube to gas syringe. Record volume every 10 seconds. Graph starts steep (fast) and flattens (slowing).

Reading a rate graph

To find rate at t = 30s: draw tangent to curve at t = 30s. Measure gradient: rise ÷ run. If tangent goes up 20cm³ in 10s, rate = 2cm³/s at that moment.

Rate = change in quantity ÷ time

From graph: rate = gradient = Δy ÷ Δx
Initial rate: gradient of tangent at t = 0
Steeper gradient = faster rate
Flat section = reaction complete

Common Misconception

The rate is constant throughout the reaction.

The rate usually decreases as the reaction progresses because reactant concentration decreases. The steepest part is at the start, and the curve flattens as the reaction slows and stops.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide pre-drawn rate graphs. Practice drawing tangents and calculating gradients. Start with straight-line examples before curved graphs.
Stretch:
Calculate the initial rate from a graph where the tangent at t = 0 goes through (0,0) and (5, 25cm³). Rate = 5cm³/s.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Look at a rate graph. Where is the rate fastest? When does the reaction stop? How would you find the rate at a specific time?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Describes methods for measuring rate, reads rate from graphs, and draws tangents to find rate at specific times.
  • Getting there: Can measure rate from data but cannot read rate from graphs accurately.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain how to measure rate. Reteach with practical demonstrations and simple graphs.

Lesson 3: Collision Theory in Depth

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Particle Diagrams

Draw two containers: one with gas at low temperature, one with gas at high temperature. How do the particles differ? What does this tell us about collisions?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Particle diagrams and rate:
Use particle diagrams to show why factors affect rate:

Increasing concentration:
• More particles in the same volume
• More collisions per second → faster rate

Increasing temperature:
• Particles move faster (longer arrows in diagram)
• More collisions per second
• More particles have energy ≥ activation energy
• Both effects increase rate significantly

Increasing surface area:
• More particles exposed on the surface
• More collisions with other particles → faster rate

Catalysts:
• Provide an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy
• More particles have energy ≥ the new, lower Ea
• Rate increases without changing temperature or concentration

Energy level diagrams with catalysts:
• Draw the same reaction profile with and without catalyst
• The catalyst lowers the 'hill' (activation energy)
• The starting and ending energy levels stay the same (ΔH unchanged)
• The reaction happens faster because more particles can get over the lower hill
Particle diagram: concentration

Container A: 5 particles in small space. Container B: 10 particles in same space. Container B has more collisions per second because particles are closer together. This is why higher concentration = faster rate.

Catalyst and activation energy

Without catalyst: Ea = 100kJ/mol. With catalyst: Ea = 60kJ/mol. At the same temperature, many more particles now have energy ≥ 60kJ/mol than ≥ 100kJ/mol. The reaction is much faster. But the same total energy is released (ΔH unchanged).

Catalyst effect on activation energy:
• Lowers Ea → more particles have enough energy
• Does not change ΔH (same amount of energy released)
• Does not change the amount of product
• Is not used up → can be reused

Particle diagrams show: more particles = more collisions = faster rate

Common Misconception

A catalyst is used up in the reaction.

A catalyst is not used up — it is chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction and can be reused. It speeds up the reaction by providing an alternative pathway with lower activation energy.

Differentiation

Support:
Draw particle diagrams together on the whiteboard. Use different coloured counters to represent particles. Move them to show collisions.
Stretch:
Draw a reaction profile showing both the catalysed and uncatalysed pathway. Label Ea and ΔH for each. Explain why the ΔH is the same.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Draw particle diagrams for: (a) low concentration vs high concentration, (b) low temperature vs high temperature. Explain the difference in rate using your diagrams.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Uses particle diagrams to explain rate, and shows how a catalyst lowers activation energy on a reaction profile.
  • Getting there: Can draw particle diagrams but cannot link them to activation energy.
  • Not yet: Cannot use particle diagrams to explain rate. Reteach with physical counters and step-by-step drawing.

Homework Suggestions

  • Draw particle diagrams showing the effect of concentration, temperature, and surface area on rate
  • Practise drawing tangents on rate graphs and calculating gradients
  • Research: how do catalytic converters in cars reduce pollution? What catalysts are used?
