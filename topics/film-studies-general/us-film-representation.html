Lesson 2: Core Concepts: representation in us film

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on representation in us film. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Representation in film refers to the ways in which different social groups, identities and experiences are constructed, depicted and made meaningful on screen.
Key Fact: Gender representation in Hollywood has historically prioritised male protagonists and marginalised female characters into limited roles such as the damsel, the love interest or the mother figure.
Key Fact: The Bechdel Test highlights the minimal representation of women in film by asking whether a film features two named women who talk to each other about something other than a man.
Key Fact: Laura Mulvey's concept of the male gaze argues that Hollywood cinema positions women as objects of visual pleasure for a presumed heterosexual male viewer, fragmenting the female body through camera work.
Key Fact: Racial representation in Hollywood has been characterised by under-representation, stereotyping and whitewashing, with ethnic minorities often confined to narrow, marginal or criminal roles.
Key Fact: Stereotypes are reductive, oversimplified representations that flatten complex identities into a few easily recognisable traits, often serving dominant ideological positions.

The film constructs its representation of its central social group through a combination of film language choices, narrative positioning and ideological assumptions that both challenge and reproduce dominant representational patterns. At the narrative level, the protagonist is granted agency and moral complexity that defies the stereotypical roles typically available to this group in Hollywood cinema — they drive the plot, make consequential decisions and undergo genuine transformation. However, the film language sometimes undermines this progressive narrative positioning. Cinematography frequently frames the character through the gaze of other characters, positioning the audience as external observers rather than as identificatory subjects. Mise-en-scene choices — particularly costume and setting — at moments re-inscribe conventional associations that the narrative seems to reject. The film's representation is thus contradictory: it advances beyond stereotype in some respects while remaining constrained by it in others.
