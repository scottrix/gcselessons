Lesson 2: Core Concepts: international film movements

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on international film movements. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Film movements are coherent groups of filmmakers who share aesthetic principles, production methods and ideological commitments during a specific historical period.
Key Fact: Italian Neo-Realism emerged after World War II, rejecting studio artificiality in favour of location shooting, non-professional actors and stories about working-class struggle.
Key Fact: The French New Wave of the late 1950s and 1960s challenged mainstream French cinema with location filming, handheld camera, jump cuts and self-reflexive storytelling.
Key Fact: German Expressionism of the 1920s used distorted set design, exaggerated shadows and stylised performance to convey psychological turmoil and social anxiety in post-World War I Germany.
Key Fact: Soviet Montage of the 1920s, led by Eisenstein and Vertov, used dynamic editing techniques to create meaning through the collision of images rather than continuous narrative.
Key Fact: Italian Neo-Realist films such as Bicycle Thieves and Rome, Open City depicted poverty, unemployment and moral crisis with documentary-like authenticity and compassion.

Extended question: Full-Mark Response How did the social and historical context of post-World War I Germany shape the visual style of German Expressionist cinema? <div class="

German Expressionist cinema emerged from a society traumatised by military defeat, political revolution and economic collapse, and its visual style directly externalised this collective psychological damage. The distorted, angular sets of films like The Cabinet of Dr Caligari, with their painted shadows and impossible architecture, visualised a world where stable reality had broken down and perception could no longer be trusted. Chiaroscuro lighting with harsh contrasts between deep shadow and stark light reflected a nation divided between dark threats and fragile hope. The stylised, exaggerated performances conveyed emotions too extreme for naturalistic expression, mirroring the intense anxieties of a population facing hyperinflation and political extremism. The movement's preference for stories of madness, doubles and supernatural menace expressed broader fears about the fragility of sanity and civilisation. Expressionist cinema did not merely depict a disturbed world; it made the audience experience that disturbance through a visual environment that refused the comfort of realism. Exam Tips: Always identify the specific film movement by name and era rather than making vague references to old or foreign films. | Link visual techniques to their historical context: German Expressionism's distortion reflects post-World War I anxiety, not just stylistic preference. | Use named films as examples: reference Bicycle Thieves for Neo-Realism, Breathless for the New Wave, The Cabinet of Dr Caligari for Expressionism. | Explain how film movements challenged the dominant cinema of their time, not just what their techniques were. | Consider how the influence of historical film movements can be seen in contemporary films you have studied.
Common Errors: ✗ Describing film movements only by their visual techniques without explaining why they emerged. ✓ Always link a movement's style to its social and historical context: Neo-Realism's location shooting resulted from damaged studios and post-war poverty. ✗ Confusing German Expressionism with expressionist acting in general. ✓ German Expressionism is a specific 1920s movement with distorted set design, chiaroscuro lighting and stylised performance; not all stylised acting is Expressionist. ✗ Saying the French New Wave 'invented' handheld camera or location shooting. ✓ These techniques existed before but the New Wave used them deliberately to break conventions and create a new cinematic language,
