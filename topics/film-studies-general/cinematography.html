Lesson 2: Core Concepts: cinematography

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on cinematography. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Cinematography encompasses all visual choices made with the camera to create meaning and emotion in film.
Key Fact: Camera shots are defined by distance: extreme long shot establishes setting, long shot shows full body, medium shot frames waist-up, close-up fills the frame with face or object, extreme close-up isolates a tiny detail.
Key Fact: Camera angles communicate power and status: low angles make subjects appear powerful and dominant, high angles make them seem vulnerable or small, eye-level angles feel neutral and objective.
Key Fact: Dutch or canted angles create unease, disorientation and psychological instability, commonly used in horror and thriller genres.
Key Fact: Camera movement includes pans, tilts, tracking shots, dolly shots, crane shots, handheld and steadicam, each producing distinct emotional and narrative effects.
Key Fact: Tracking and dolly shots follow subjects through space, creating audience immersion and involvement in the on-screen action.

Extended question: Full-Mark Response How does the cinematography in the opening sequence of your chosen film create meaning for the audience? <div class="

In the opening sequence, cinematography creates meaning through a combination of shot types, angles, movement and lighting that position the audience in relation to the protagonist. The extreme long shot establishes the isolated setting, with the small figure dwarfed by the vast landscape, conveying vulnerability and insignificance. A high-angle shot looks down on the character as they approach, making them appear small and powerless against their environment. Medium close-ups with shallow focus isolate the character's anxious expression against a blurred background, intensifying unease. Handheld camera during the interior sequence creates instability that mirrors the protagonist's disorientation and makes the audience feel the same uncertainty. Low-key lighting with a single practical light source casts deep shadows across the character's face, the chiaroscuro effect suggesting a divided or hidden nature. A Dutch angle at the moment of revelation tilts the frame to express psychological disturbance. Exam Tips: Always link cinematography choices to meaning — never just identify a technique, explain its effect on the audience. | Use precise terminology: say 'low-angle shot' not 'shot from below', and 'shallow focus' not 'blurred background'. | Compare cinematography across scenes or films to show how different choices create different meanings. | When writing about lighting, consider whether it is motivated by an in-film source or unmotivated and purely atmospheric. | Think about how cinematography works with other film language elements — a close-up is more powerful when combined with diegetic silence.
Common Errors: ✗ Describing the shot type without explaining its effect on meaning or the audience. ✓ Always connect the shot type to its purpose: 'The close-up emphasises the character's fear, drawing the audience into their emotional state.' ✗ Confusing camera angles — e.g. saying a high-angle shot makes a character look powerful. ✓ A high-angle shot looks down on a subject making them appear weak or vulnerable; a low-angle shot looks up making them appear powerful. ✗ Saying 'the camera moves' without specifying the type of movement (pan, tilt, track, dolly). ✓ Use precise movement terms: 'The camera tracks left alongside the character' rather than 'the camera moves.' ✗ Discussing lighting only in terms
