Lesson 2: Core Concepts: genre theory &amp; conventions

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on genre theory &amp; conventions. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Film genre is a categorisation system that groups films sharing similar narrative patterns, visual styles, themes and emotional effects.
Key Fact: Steve Neale argued that genre depends on both repetition of conventions and difference and variation to sustain audience interest.
Key Fact: The 'repertoire of elements' describes the set of conventions that characterise a genre — setting, character types, iconography, narratives and themes.
Key Fact: Iconography refers to the visual symbols and recurring images associated with a genre: cowboy hats in westerns, spaceships in sci-fi, masks in horror.
Key Fact: Audience expectations are shaped by genre knowledge — viewers anticipate certain experiences and pleasures based on the genre they have chosen.
Key Fact: Genre conventions are not fixed rules but shared expectations between filmmakers and audiences that can be fulfilled or subverted.

Genre theory provides an essential framework for understanding how the film operates, how it positions its audience and how it functions within broader cinematic contexts. Applying Neale's concept of repetition and difference reveals that the film is not simply a genre exercise but a sophisticated negotiation between convention and innovation. The film repeats core genre conventions: its iconography is immediately recognisable, its narrative follows the expected trajectory and its character types are familiar. However, it introduces significant variations that refresh the formula and demand active interpretation. The protagonist subverts the typical genre hero by lacking conventional qualities of strength and certainty, instead presenting vulnerability and moral ambiguity. Altman's semantic and syntactic framework further illuminates the film's construction. At the semantic level, the film deploys the expected visual vocabulary that immediately signals its genre. Yet at the syntactic level, the narrative structure rearranges expected relationships between elements, creating thematic meanings that go beyond the genre's typical concerns.
