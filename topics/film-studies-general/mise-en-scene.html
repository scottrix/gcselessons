Lesson 2: Core Concepts: mise-en-scène

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on mise-en-scène. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Mise-en-scene is a French term meaning 'everything placed in the frame' and includes setting, props, costume, makeup, lighting and the positioning and performance of actors.
Key Fact: Setting establishes time period, location, social context and atmosphere — it grounds the narrative and can function as a character itself.
Key Fact: Props carry symbolic meaning and can signal character traits, narrative development or thematic concerns; a motif is a recurring prop that gains significance through repetition.
Key Fact: Costume communicates identity, status, group belonging and character transformation across the film.
Key Fact: Makeup and hair design contribute to character identity, period authenticity and genre conventions such as monstrous appearance in horror.
Key Fact: Staging or blocking refers to the physical positioning and movement of actors within the frame, revealing relationships, power dynamics and emotional states.

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response How does mise-en-scene construct meaning in a key sequence from your chosen film? <div class="

The mise-en-scene creates meaning through a carefully constructed combination of setting, props, costume, makeup and performance that establishes a world of social division and personal desperation. The setting is a cramped, dimly lit apartment with peeling wallpaper and minimal furniture, immediately communicating poverty and neglect. The worn fabric of the sofa and the empty fridge function as props that reinforce the character's economic hardship without dialogue. The protagonist's faded, oversized coat and scuffed shoes contrast sharply with the crisp, tailored suit of the visitor, visually encoding the power imbalance between them. Makeup enhances this contrast: the protagonist's pallid complexion and dark circles suggest exhaustion, while the visitor appears well-groomed and healthy. The staging places the visitor standing in the centre of the room looking down at the protagonist who sits hunched on the edge of the sofa, their positions literalising the hierarchy between them. A recurring motif — a child's drawing pinned to the wall — appears in the background, its bright colours the only vibrant element in the muted palette, symbolising hope and the stakes of the encounter.

Exam Tips: Don't just list mise-en-scene elements — explain what each one communicates to the audience. | Use specific examples from the film: name the prop, describe the costume, identify the colour change. | Show how mise-en-scene changes across scenes to track character or thematic development. | Connect mise-en-scene to genre: how do setting, costume and props signal the film's genre conventions? | Remember that mise-en-scene works with cinematography — a prop's meaning changes depending on how it is framed.
Common Errors: ✗ Listing mise-en-scene elements without explaining what they mean or why they are significant. ✓ Always analyse: 'The peeling wallpaper and dim lighting suggest decay and neglect, reflecting the family's decline.' ✗ Confusing mise-en-scene with cinematography — e.g. calling a camera angle part of mise-en-scene. ✓ Mise-en-scene covers what is IN the frame; cinematography covers HOW the frame captures it. A prop is mise-en-scene; a close-up of it is cinematography. ✗ Discussing costume only in terms of what characters wear, ignoring colour, condition and change. ✓ Analyse costume details: 'Her transition from muted grey to vivid red signals her growing confidence and shift from passive to act
