Lesson 2: Core Concepts: writing about film

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on writing about film. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Analytical writing about film moves beyond personal opinion to sustained, evidence-based argument about how meaning is constructed.
Key Fact: Every claim in a film essay must be supported by specific evidence from the film: name the technique, describe its use and explain its effect.
Key Fact: Evaluative writing requires you to make a judgement and justify it, weighing evidence and considering alternative interpretations before reaching a conclusion.
Key Fact: Structure is essential: an introduction establishing your argument, focused body paragraphs each making one point, and a conclusion that synthesises your reasoning.
Key Fact: Film language terminology must be used precisely throughout: the mark scheme rewards correct use of technical vocabulary and penalises vagueness.
Key Fact: Avoid first-person phrases like 'I think' or 'in my opinion'; instead, present interpretations as arguments supported by evidence.

Extended question: Full-Mark Response How can you ensure your film studies writing is analytical rather than merely descriptive? <div class="

Analytical writing distinguishes itself from description by consistently explaining how and why film techniques create meaning, rather than simply identifying what is on screen. The key principle is that every observation must be followed by interpretation. Where description states 'there is a close-up of the character's face,' analysis continues: 'the close-up isolates her expression, positioning the audience to share her emotional state and creating intimacy that contrasts with the distance established in the preceding wide shot.' This movement from technique to effect transforms a list into an argument. Structure supports analysis: using PEAL paragraphs ensures each point has evidence and interpretation, preventing descriptive drift. Precise terminology demonstrates understanding and enables specific analysis: 'shallow focus' and 'low-key lighting' are analytical tools, while 'blurred background' and 'dark lighting' remain descriptive. Evaluative language further strengthens analysis: rather than stating a technique 'is used,' argue that it 'serves to,' 'functions as' or 'constructs' meaning, showing deliberate authorial purpose.
