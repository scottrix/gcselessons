Lesson 2: Core Concepts: editing

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on editing. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Editing is the process of selecting, arranging and combining shots to construct narrative, meaning and emotional impact.
Key Fact: Continuity editing creates a seamless, invisible flow that maintains spatial and temporal coherence for the viewer.
Key Fact: The 180-degree rule ensures the camera stays on one side of an imaginary line between characters to preserve screen direction and spatial consistency.
Key Fact: Shot-reverse-shot alternates between two characters in conversation, creating the impression of a natural conversational exchange.
Key Fact: Match on action cuts during a continuous movement so the action flows seamlessly across the cut, making the edit invisible.
Key Fact: Eyeline match cuts from a character looking off-screen to what they are looking at, establishing spatial relationships and directing audience attention.

Extended question: Full-Mark Response How does editing construct meaning and create impact in a key sequence from your chosen film? <div class="

The editing creates meaning through a carefully controlled progression from slow, measured cutting to increasingly rapid cross-cutting that builds to a climactic moment of tension. The sequence opens with long takes averaging eight to ten seconds each, allowing the audience to settle into the domestic setting and absorb the everyday normality. Match-on-action cuts link the character's movements across the space seamlessly, using continuity editing to establish spatial coherence. As the narrative shifts, the editing pace subtly accelerates with shot durations shortening to four or five seconds, creating a creeping sense of unease. The editor introduces cross-cutting between the protagonist investigating inside and an unknown figure approaching outside, building dramatic irony and suspense. Shot-reverse-shot maintains continuity within interior sequences while exterior shots employ a different visual palette, the contrast heightening the sense of danger. As the two lines of action converge, cutting rate increases further with shots lasting only one or two seconds. Exam Tips: Always name the editing technique precisely — 'dissolve' not 'a gradual change', 'match on action' not 'a smooth cut'. | Explain the effect of each editing choice on the audience, not just what the technique is. | Consider how editing pace changes within a sequence and what that shift communicates. | Link editing to narrative: how do editing choices advance the story, create suspense or reveal information? | Remember that editing is the one film language element that is entirely constructed — it does not exist in front of the camera.
Common Errors: ✗ Describing an edit without naming the specific technique — e.g. 'the scene changes to another scene.' ✓ Use precise terminology: 'A cross-cut shifts to the parallel action, building suspense as the audience watches both threads converge.' ✗ Confusing montage with continuity editing — treating all rapid cutting as montage. ✓ Distinguish them: continuity editing is seamless and invisible; montage creates meaning through deliberate, visible juxtaposition of images. ✗ Discussing pace only as 'fast' or 'slow' without linking it to emotional or narrative effect. ✓ Connect pace to meaning: 'The accelerating pace of cuts mirrors the character's rising panic, drawing the audience into their anxiety
