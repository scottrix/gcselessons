B18: Photosynthesis

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand the photosynthesis equation, the factors affecting the rate of photosynthesis, and how limiting factors can be investigated experimentally.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 4
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Write the word and balanced symbol equation for photosynthesis
  • Explain the role of chlorophyll, light, and carbon dioxide
  • Describe the factors that affect the rate of photosynthesis
  • Investigate limiting factors using the aquatic plant (Elodea) experiment

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of plant cell structure and chloroplasts (B1, B4)
  • Basic knowledge of chemical reactions and equations

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Elodea (pondweed) for practical
  • Beakers, test tubes, lamps with adjustable brightness
  • Sodium hydrogen carbonate solution (for CO₂ supply)
  • Ruler, stop watch
  • Thermometer

Lesson 1: The Photosynthesis Equation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Why It Matters

Ask: "Without photosynthesis, what would happen to life on Earth?" Discuss that photosynthesis produces the oxygen we breathe and the glucose that is the base of almost all food chains.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Photosynthesis: the process by which green plants use light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen. Word equation: Carbon dioxide + Water → Glucose + Oxygen (with light energy and chlorophyll) Balanced symbol equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ (with light energy and chlorophyll) Key points:
  • Occurs in chloroplasts, which contain the green pigment chlorophyll
  • Chlorophyll absorbs light energy (mainly red and blue wavelengths — reflects green, which is why plants look green)
  • Carbon dioxide enters through the stomata in leaves
  • Water is absorbed by the roots and transported up the xylem
  • Glucose is used by the plant for: respiration, making starch (storage), making cellulose (cell walls), making amino acids (with nitrate ions from soil)
  • Oxygen is released as a waste product through the stomata
The rate of photosynthesis can be measured by:
  • Counting bubbles of oxygen released from an aquatic plant
  • Measuring the volume of oxygen produced over time
Worked Example

"State the word equation for photosynthesis." Answer: Carbon dioxide + Water → Glucose + Oxygen. "Name two substances the plant needs to absorb and where they are absorbed from." Answer: Carbon dioxide (absorbed from the air through stomata in leaves) and water (absorbed from the soil through root hair cells).

Common Misconception

"Photosynthesis only happens in leaves"

Photosynthesis occurs in any green part of the plant that contains chloroplasts — this includes green stems, unripe fruits, and sepals. However, leaves are the main site because they have a large surface area and many palisade cells packed with chloroplasts.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide the equation with blanks to fill in. Focus on remembering the word equation: "CO₂ + water → glucose + oxygen." Use the mnemonic: "Cows Produce Green Oxygen."
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Discuss the light-dependent and light-independent reactions ( Calvin cycle) at a simplified level. Research how scientists use radioactive carbon-14 to trace the path of carbon in photosynthesis (Melvin Calvin's experiment).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Equation Speed Write

Students write the photosynthesis equation from memory. Check and discuss. Repeat until accurate.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can write the photosynthesis equation accurately and explain the role of each component
  • Getting there: Can write the word equation but confuses reactants and products
  • Not yet: Cannot write the photosynthesis equation

Lesson 2: Factors Affecting Photosynthesis

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Prediction

Show a plant in a dark cupboard vs. one in sunlight. Ask: "Why do plants need light? What would happen if there was no light?" Discuss that light provides the energy for photosynthesis.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Factors affecting the rate of photosynthesis:
  • Light intensity: more light → faster photosynthesis (up to a point). Increasing distance from a lamp decreases light intensity. At very high light intensity, the rate levels off because another factor becomes limiting.
  • Carbon dioxide concentration: more CO₂ → faster photosynthesis (up to a point). Normal atmospheric CO₂ is about 0.04%. Increasing it speeds up photosynthesis until another factor limits the rate.
  • Temperature: higher temperature → faster photosynthesis (up to an optimum of about 25-40°C). At very high temperatures (>40°C), enzymes in chloroplasts are denatured and the rate drops rapidly.
  • Amount of chlorophyll: less chlorophyll (e.g. yellowing leaves due to nutrient deficiency) → slower photosynthesis.
Limiting factor: the factor that is in shortest supply and therefore limits the rate of a process. At any given time, only ONE factor is limiting. Graph interpretation:
  • Light intensity graph: rate increases steeply, then plateaus — at the plateau, CO₂ or temperature is limiting
  • Temperature graph: rate increases then drops sharply above optimum — enzymes are denatured
  • CO₂ graph: rate increases then plateaus — at the plateau, light or temperature is limiting
Worked Example

"A plant is placed in bright light at 25°C with normal atmospheric CO₂. The light intensity is then doubled. Explain why the rate of photosynthesis does not double." Answer: At normal CO₂ levels, CO₂ is likely the limiting factor. Doubling the light intensity does not increase the rate because there is not enough CO₂ to use the extra light energy. To increase the rate, CO₂ concentration would need to be increased.

Common Misconception

"Increasing all factors at once always increases photosynthesis"

Only increasing the limiting factor will increase the rate. If light is the limiting factor, increasing CO₂ or temperature will have no effect. At any point, only one factor is limiting the rate.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on one factor at a time. For each factor, describe what happens when it increases and when it is too high. Use simple graph descriptions.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Design a complete investigation to test how one factor affects photosynthesis, including control of variables, dependent/independent variables, and how to present results.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Limiting Factor

Show four scenarios. Students identify the limiting factor in each. E.g. "A plant in a dark room" (light), "A plant in a sealed jar" (CO₂), "A plant at 50°C" (temperature/enzyme denaturation).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain how each factor affects photosynthesis and identify limiting factors from graphs
  • Getting there: Can name the factors but cannot explain their effects or identify limiting factors
  • Not yet: Cannot name the factors affecting photosynthesis

Lesson 3: Investigating Photosynthesis — Practical

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Experiment Design

Ask: "How could you measure the rate of photosynthesis using a pondweed?" Discuss: counting bubbles of oxygen, or measuring the volume of gas collected. Introduce the Elodea experiment.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The Elodea (pondweed) experiment — a classic required practical: Setup:
  • Place a piece of Elodea in a beaker of water with sodium hydrogen carbonate (NaHCO₃) — this provides a constant supply of CO₂
  • Invert a test tube over the cut end of the Elodea to collect gas bubbles
  • Place a lamp at a measured distance from the beaker
Measuring light intensity:
  • Move the lamp to different distances (e.g. 10cm, 20cm, 30cm, 40cm, 50cm)
  • Light intensity is inversely proportional to the square of the distance (intensity ∝ 1/d²)
Procedure:
  • Allow 2-3 minutes for the plant to adjust to each new condition
  • Count the number of oxygen bubbles released in 1 minute (or measure the volume of gas collected)
  • Repeat 3 times at each distance for reliability
  • Calculate the mean number of bubbles per minute
Controlling variables:
  • Same piece of Elodea each time
  • Same volume of water
  • Same concentration of sodium hydrogen carbonate
  • Same temperature (use a water bath to prevent lamp heating the water)
  • Allow adjustment time before counting
Results:
  • Plot a graph: distance (x-axis) vs bubbles per minute (y-axis)
  • Or: 1/d² (x-axis) vs bubbles per minute (y-axis) for a straight line through the origin (if other factors are not limiting)
Worked Example

"Explain why a water bath is placed between the lamp and the beaker in the Elodea experiment." Answer: The lamp produces heat as well as light. If the water temperature changes, this would be a confounding variable — you would not know whether changes in the rate were due to light intensity or temperature. A water bath absorbs the heat from the lamp while allowing light through, keeping temperature constant.

Common Misconception

"More bubbles always means faster photosynthesis"

The size of bubbles can vary, so counting bubbles is not a precise measure of the volume of oxygen produced. Measuring the actual volume of gas collected (using a gas syringe) is more accurate. Bubble counting is a suitable method for comparing relative rates.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on the basic setup and what is being measured. Simplify: "move lamp closer → more light → more bubbles → faster photosynthesis." Provide a results table template.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Calculate light intensity using 1/d² for each distance. Plot the graph of light intensity vs rate. Discuss why the graph does not go through the origin if there is a residual rate at large distances.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Fair Test Check

List five things that must be kept the same in this experiment. Students write on mini-whiteboards. Check answers.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the full experimental setup, explain how to control variables, and interpret results
  • Getting there: Can describe the setup but cannot explain why variables are controlled
  • Not yet: Cannot describe the Elodea experiment or explain what it investigates

Lesson 4: Photosynthesis and Climate (Higher)

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Carbon Cycle Link

Ask: "How does photosynthesis help to reduce the level of CO₂ in the atmosphere?" Discuss the link between photosynthesis, CO₂ levels, and climate change.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Photosynthesis and the carbon cycle:
  • Photosynthesis removes CO₂ from the atmosphere and stores carbon as glucose (and then starch, cellulose, etc.) in plant biomass
  • When plants are burned or decompose, the carbon is returned to the atmosphere as CO₂
  • Fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) are ancient plant remains — burning them releases carbon that was stored millions of years ago
  • Deforestation reduces the number of trees performing photosynthesis, leaving more CO₂ in the atmosphere
Higher content — rate calculations:
  • The rate of photosynthesis can be expressed as: rate = amount of product / time
  • Example: if a plant produces 10 cm³ of oxygen in 30 minutes, the rate = 10/30 = 0.33 cm³/min
  • Comparing rates at different conditions allows evaluation of limiting factors
Factors affecting rate in different environments:
  • In a greenhouse: farmers can increase CO₂ concentration and temperature to maximise photosynthesis and crop yield
  • In tropical rainforests: high temperature and rainfall mean photosynthesis occurs year-round, making these ecosystems highly productive
  • In winter: low light intensity and temperature limit photosynthesis, which is why plants go dormant
Worked Example

"A greenhouse grower wants to increase the rate of photosynthesis in tomato plants. Suggest two ways they could do this and explain the biological principle behind each." Answer: (1) Increase CO₂ concentration — CO₂ is a raw material for photosynthesis, so increasing its availability increases the rate (up to a limiting point). (2) Increase light intensity using artificial lighting — light provides the energy for the light-dependent reactions, so more light means more energy for photosynthesis.

Common Misconception

"Photosynthesis and respiration are opposite processes"

While the equations look like opposites, they are NOT simply reversible. Photosynthesis uses light energy to build glucose; respiration releases energy from glucose. They occur in different organelles (chloroplasts vs mitochondria) and involve completely different chemical pathways.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on the practical application: "How do farmers use greenhouses to grow more food?" Connect to factors affecting photosynthesis. Simplify the carbon cycle to: plants take in CO₂, animals release CO₂, burning releases CO₂.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Calculate rates from experimental data and compare. Research how climate change might affect photosynthesis rates globally. Discuss the implications for food security.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Rate Calculation

Give students data: "Plant A produces 15 cm³ O₂ in 20 min. Plant B produces 22 cm³ O₂ in 25 min. Which is photosynthesising faster?" Students calculate rates and compare.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain the carbon cycle link and calculate photosynthesis rates from data
  • Getting there: Understands the link between photosynthesis and CO₂ but cannot perform calculations
  • Not yet: Cannot explain how photosynthesis relates to the carbon cycle

Homework Suggestions

  • Write the photosynthesis equation from memory five times until perfect
  • Design an experiment to investigate how temperature affects the rate of photosynthesis
  • Research the carbon cycle and create a labelled diagram showing how carbon moves between atmosphere, organisms, and fossil fuels
  • Calculate photosynthesis rates from a set of data and draw a graph to show the results
