B21: Anaerobic Respiration

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand what happens when cells respire without oxygen, the production of lactic acid in animals and ethanol in plants/yeast, and the concept of oxygen debt.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Explain what anaerobic respiration is and when it occurs
  • Write the equations for anaerobic respiration in animals and in plants/yeast
  • Describe the effects of lactic acid buildup in muscles
  • Explain the concept of oxygen debt

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of aerobic respiration (B20)
  • Knowledge that mitochondria are the site of aerobic respiration

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Exercise equipment (optional — for demonstration)
  • Equation comparison table handout

Lesson 1: Anaerobic Respiration in Animals

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Sprint Challenge

Ask: "Can you sprint at top speed for 10 minutes? Why not?" Discuss that during intense exercise, muscles need energy faster than oxygen can be delivered. Introduce anaerobic respiration.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Anaerobic respiration: the chemical process in which glucose is broken down WITHOUT oxygen to release a small amount of energy. In animals (including humans): Glucose → Lactic acid (with a small amount of energy released) Key points:
  • Occurs when the demand for energy exceeds the oxygen supply (e.g. during sprinting)
  • Takes place in the cytoplasm (NOT mitochondria — no oxygen is used)
  • Produces much less energy than aerobic respiration (only 2 ATP per glucose vs 38 ATP)
  • Produces lactic acid as a waste product
  • Lactic acid builds up in muscles causing cramp, pain, and fatigue
  • If lactic acid builds up too much, the pH of the muscle drops (becomes more acidic), which inhibits enzyme activity and reduces muscle function
Oxygen debt:
  • After intense exercise, you continue to breathe heavily to take in extra oxygen
  • This extra oxygen is used to:
    • Convert lactic acid back to glucose (in the liver)
    • Repay the oxygen that was "borrowed" during anaerobic respiration
  • The period of extra oxygen intake after exercise is called "repaying the oxygen debt"

Oxygen needed?YesNo

ProductsCO₂ + H₂OLactic acid

Worked Example

"Explain why a sprinter breathes heavily after a race but a jogger does not." Answer: The sprinter relied heavily on anaerobic respiration during the race, producing lactic acid. After the race, they need extra oxygen (oxygen debt) to convert the lactic acid back to glucose in the liver. The jogger maintained a pace where aerobic respiration was sufficient, so no significant lactic acid accumulated and no oxygen debt needs repaying.

Common Misconception

"Anaerobic respiration only occurs in microorganisms"

Anaerobic respiration occurs in animal muscle cells during intense exercise when oxygen supply is insufficient. It also occurs in microorganisms (yeast, bacteria) — but the products are different (ethanol and CO₂ in yeast fermentation).

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on the key difference: aerobic needs oxygen and makes lots of energy; anaerobic doesn't need oxygen and makes less energy + lactic acid. Provide a simple comparison table to complete.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Calculate how many times more energy aerobic respiration releases compared to anaerobic (38 vs 2 ATP = 19 times more). Discuss why the body switches to anaerobic when it could continue aerobic.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Equation Check

Students write the anaerobic respiration equation in animals from memory. Compare with a partner.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe anaerobic respiration in animals, write the equation, and explain oxygen debt
  • Getting there: Knows anaerobic respiration produces lactic acid but cannot explain oxygen debt
  • Not yet: Cannot describe anaerobic respiration or distinguish it from aerobic

Lesson 2: Anaerobic Respiration in Plants and Yeast (Fermentation)

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Bread and Beer

Show a loaf of bread and a bottle of beer/cider. Ask: "What do these have in common?" (Both use yeast fermentation.) Ask: "What does yeast do to make bread rise?" (Produces CO₂ gas.) Introduce fermentation.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
In plants and yeast (anaerobic respiration = fermentation): Glucose → Ethanol + Carbon dioxide (with a small amount of energy released) Key points:
  • Occurs in the cytoplasm (no mitochondria involved)
  • Yeast is a single-celled fungus used in baking and brewing
  • In baking: yeast ferments sugar in bread dough → CO₂ makes the dough rise (bubbles trapped in gluten network)
  • In brewing: yeast ferments sugar in fruit juice or malted barley → ethanol (alcohol) is produced. Beer, wine, cider all use this process.
  • In winemaking: grapes are crushed → yeast (naturally present on grape skins or added) ferments the sugar → ethanol + CO₂
Why yeast fermentation is useful:
  • Baking: CO₂ makes bread rise; ethanol evaporates during baking
  • Brewing: ethanol is the alcohol in drinks
  • Biofuels: ethanol can be used as a renewable fuel
  • Food production: some traditional foods use fermentation (yoghurt, kimchi, sourdough)
Why plants ferment:
  • Plant roots in waterlogged soil cannot get oxygen → anaerobic respiration occurs → ethanol is produced
  • Too much ethanol is toxic to plant cells → roots die → this is why plants die in waterlogged conditions

EquationGlucose → Lactic acidGlucose → Ethanol + CO₂

Useful?Provides energy in emergenciesUsed in baking and brewing

Worked Example

"Write the equation for anaerobic respiration in yeast and give one use of this process." Answer: Glucose → Ethanol + Carbon dioxide. Use: in baking, the CO₂ gas makes bread dough rise, creating a light, airy texture.

Common Misconception

"Yeast dies when it produces alcohol"

Yeast can tolerate moderate alcohol concentrations. In brewing, alcohol levels eventually become too high and yeast dies or becomes dormant, which is why there is a maximum alcohol content that fermentation can achieve (typically 14-16% for wine, lower for beer).

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on the yeast equation: "sugar → alcohol + CO₂." Simple matching: yeast in bread → CO₂ makes it rise; yeast in beer → alcohol is produced.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the industrial uses of fermentation: biofuel production, pharmaceutical production (e.g. insulin using genetically modified bacteria), and food preservation (pickling, fermentation). Discuss the economic importance of the brewing industry.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Two Equations

Students write both anaerobic respiration equations (animals and yeast) from memory. Self-check with a partner.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can write both anaerobic respiration equations and explain their significance
  • Getting there: Can write the equations but cannot explain their uses or significance
  • Not yet: Cannot write the anaerobic respiration equations

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a comparison table of aerobic and anaerobic respiration (including both animal and yeast types)
  • Investigate fermentation at home: make bread using yeast and observe the rising process
  • Research why athletes train at high altitudes and how this affects their red blood cell count
  • Write an explanation of oxygen debt including what happens to lactic acid after exercise
