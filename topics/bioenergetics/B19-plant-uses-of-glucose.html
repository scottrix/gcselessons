B19: Plant Uses of Glucose

Foundation Higher All Boards

Learn how plants use the glucose produced during photosynthesis for respiration, growth, storage, and making other essential substances.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • List the five main uses of glucose in plants
  • Explain how glucose is converted to starch for storage
  • Describe how glucose is used to make cellulose, amino acids, and lipids
  • Understand the relationship between photosynthesis and respiration in plants

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of photosynthesis and its equation (B18)
  • Basic knowledge of plant cell structure

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Iodine solution and potato/starch test materials
  • Diagrams of plant metabolism

Lesson 1: Five Uses of Glucose

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Why Glucose?

Ask: "If photosynthesis produces glucose, why don't plants just store glucose directly? Why do they need to convert it to other things?" Discuss that glucose is useful but not always the most practical form for storage or structure.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Five main uses of glucose produced during photosynthesis:
  1. Respiration: glucose is used in aerobic respiration in mitochondria to release energy for all the plant's metabolic activities (growth, active transport, protein synthesis). All living cells respire — plants are no exception.
  2. Storage as starch: glucose is insoluble and cannot be dissolved in water. It is stored in amyloplasts in roots (e.g. potato), seeds, and fruits. Starch is a polymer of glucose — many glucose molecules joined together. When the plant needs energy, starch is broken back down to glucose.
  3. Making cellulose: glucose is converted to cellulose, which is the main component of cell walls. Cellulose provides structural support, keeping the plant rigid and upright. Cellulose fibres are strong and flexible.
  4. Making amino acids (with nitrate ions): glucose is combined with nitrate ions (absorbed from the soil through roots) to make amino acids. Amino acids are the building blocks of proteins. Proteins are used for: enzymes, structural components, transport molecules, and defence.
  5. Making lipids (fats and oils): glucose is converted to lipids for: energy storage (in seeds — e.g. sunflower oil), making cell membranes (phospholipid bilayer), and waterproof coatings (e.g. on leaves).
Key relationships:
  • Photosynthesis produces glucose during the day (needs light)
  • Respiration happens ALL the time (day and night) to release energy
  • During the day, photosynthesis rate is usually higher than respiration rate → net glucose production
  • At night, only respiration occurs → glucose is used up
Worked Example

"List five uses of glucose in plants." Answer: (1) Used in respiration to release energy. (2) Converted to starch for storage. (3) Converted to cellulose for cell walls. (4) Combined with nitrate ions to make amino acids. (5) Converted to lipids for energy storage and cell membranes.

Common Misconception

"Plants only photosynthesise and never respire"

Plants respire ALL the time — day and night. Respiration occurs in every living cell and releases the energy needed for all metabolic processes. During the day, the rate of photosynthesis usually exceeds the rate of respiration, so there is a net gain of glucose. At night, only respiration occurs.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a simple list: glucose is used for (1) energy (respiration), (2) storage (starch), (3) structure (cellulose), (4) proteins (amino acids), (5) fats (lipids). Match each to a function.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Discuss why starch is better for storage than glucose (insoluble, doesn't affect water potential). Research the difference between starch storage in monocots (endosperm) and dicots (cotyledons).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Five Uses Recall

Students list all five uses of glucose from memory. Compare with a partner and add any missed.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can list all five uses and explain each with biological reasoning
  • Getting there: Can list three uses but cannot explain the biological significance
  • Not yet: Cannot list the main uses of glucose in plants

Lesson 2: Starch Testing and Photosynthesis-Respiration Balance

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Starch Test Demo

Perform an iodine test on a piece of potato. Show that iodine turns blue-black in the presence of starch. Ask: "Why does the potato contain starch? Where did it come from?" (Photosynthesis → glucose → starch.)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The iodine test for starch:
  • Iodine solution is amber/brown in colour
  • In the presence of starch, it turns blue-black
  • Procedure: place a drop of iodine on the cut surface of a food sample. If it turns blue-black, starch is present. If it stays amber, starch is absent.
  • This can be used to test leaves for starch (e.g. to show that light is needed for photosynthesis)
The starch test on a leaf — showing light is needed:
  1. De-starch a plant by placing it in the dark for 48 hours (all existing starch is used up in respiration)
  2. Cover part of a leaf with aluminium foil (blocks light). Leave the rest exposed.
  3. Place the plant in sunlight for several hours
  4. Remove the leaf, boil in ethanol to remove chlorophyll (so the colour change is visible)
  5. Rinse and add iodine solution
  6. Result: the exposed part turns blue-black (starch present); the covered part stays amber (no starch)
  7. Conclusion: light is needed for photosynthesis
    8. Photosynthesis-respiration balance:
    • During the day: photosynthesis rate > respiration rate → plant gains mass (net glucose production)
    • At night: only respiration → plant loses mass (glucose is used up)
    • In continuous darkness: respiration continues but no photosynthesis → plant loses mass and eventually dies
    • The "compensation point" is when photosynthesis rate = respiration rate (no net gas exchange)
Worked Example

"Explain why a plant placed in the dark for 48 hours before an experiment is described as 'de-starched.'" Answer: In the dark, photosynthesis cannot occur (no light), but respiration continues. The plant uses up its stored starch by converting it back to glucose for respiration. After 48 hours, all the starch has been used, so any starch found after the experiment must have been newly produced during the experiment.

Common Misconception

"Plants don't need to respire because they photosynthesise"

Photosynthesis produces glucose; respiration releases energy from glucose. Plants need BOTH processes. Respiration provides the energy for all cellular activities including active transport, protein synthesis, and growth. Without respiration, a plant would have glucose but no usable energy.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Simplify the starch test: "Iodine turns blue-black if starch is there." Provide a step-by-step card for the de-starching experiment. Focus on the conclusion: "light is needed for photosynthesis."
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Design a complete investigation to show that CO₂ is needed for photosynthesis (using sodium hydroxide to absorb CO₂). Discuss the controls needed and predict results.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Balance Check

Students draw a simple graph showing the rates of photosynthesis and respiration over 24 hours. Mark the times when photosynthesis exceeds respiration and vice versa.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the iodine test, the de-starching experiment, and explain the photosynthesis-respiration balance
  • Getting there: Can perform the iodine test but cannot explain the de-starching experiment
  • Not yet: Cannot describe the starch test or explain the balance between photosynthesis and respiration

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a labelled diagram showing the five uses of glucose in plants
  • Write a step-by-step guide for testing a leaf for starch using iodine
  • Explain in your own words why plants need both photosynthesis and respiration
  • Research how farmers use knowledge of photosynthesis and respiration to maximise crop yield
