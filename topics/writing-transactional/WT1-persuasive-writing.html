WT1: Persuasive Writing

Foundation Higher All Boards

Write effective transactional texts for different purposes, audiences, and formats, using appropriate style, structure, and persuasive techniques.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Write for a specified purpose, audience, and format
  • Use persuasive and rhetorical techniques effectively
  • Adapt register and tone for different contexts
  • Structure arguments logically with clear sequencing
  • Include subject-specific features (layout, conventions)

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of audience and purpose
  • Confidence with varied vocabulary and sentence structures
  • Basic understanding of persuasive techniques

Materials & Equipment

  • Examples of each text type (letters, speeches, articles, reviews)
  • Persuasive techniques reference sheet
  • Writing frames for different text types
  • Peer assessment checklists

Lesson 1: Persuasive Techniques

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Advert Analysis

Show a TV or print advert. Ask: what is it trying to persuade you to do? How? Students identify techniques: celebrity endorsement, repetition, emotional appeal, statistics.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach the main persuasive techniques:
  • Rhetorical questions — engage the reader and imply the answer you want
  • Rule of three — list three items for memorability: "It's fast, it's reliable, it's affordable"
  • Repetition — repeat key words or phrases for emphasis
  • Emotive language — words that trigger emotional responses (devastating, heartwarming, crisis)
  • Statistics and facts — provide evidence for claims
  • Testimonials — quotes from "real people" to build credibility
  • Direct address — "you" involves the reader personally
  • Imperatives — commands create urgency: "Act now", "Don't miss out"
  • Inclusive language — "we" and "us" create shared identity
  • Hyperbole — exaggeration for effect: "the best ever", "a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity"
Key exam skill: AQA Paper 2 Q5 asks students to write a transactional text. The question specifies purpose, audience, and format. Students must include features appropriate to the text type.
Worked Example

Task: Write a persuasive speech arguing for longer school holidays.
Model excerpt: "How many of you have felt exhausted by the end of term? [rhetorical question] Look around you — every face in this room tells the same story. We are tired, we are stressed, and we are running on empty. [inclusive language + emotive language] Studies show that student wellbeing drops by 40% in the final weeks of term. Forty percent. [statistics + repetition] We deserve better. Our teachers deserve better. [rule of three] It is time to give students the rest they need — and the holidays they deserve. [imperative + call to action]"

Practice Task

Write a 100-word persuasive paragraph arguing that homework should be abolished. Include at least four different persuasive techniques.

"Picture this: it is Friday evening, and instead of relaxing after a week of hard work, your child is hunched over a maths worksheet, tears of frustration in their eyes. [emotive language + direct address] Does that seem fair? [rhetorical question] Research from Finland — consistently one of the top-performing education systems in the world — shows that countries with less homework achieve equal or better results. [statistics + authority appeal] We are exhausting our children for no measurable gain. [emotive language] It is time to trust teachers to teach in school and let children be children at home. [inclusive language + imperative]"

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a persuasive techniques bingo card. Students must use at least four techniques in their writing.
Stretch:
Write a persuasive text that also addresses and rebuts a counterargument.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Technique Spot

Display a persuasive paragraph. Students highlight each technique they can identify and name it.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Uses a range of persuasive techniques purposefully and effectively
  • Getting there: Uses some techniques but they feel forced or unvaried
  • Not yet: Makes statements without using persuasive techniques

Lesson 2: Text Types and Layout

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Format Detective

Display four texts without labels. Students identify: letter, speech, article, review. Discuss: how do you know? What features give it away?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach the conventions of each text type:
  • Letter: address (sender and recipient), date, salutation (Dear…), sign-off (Yours faithfully/sincerely), paragraphs for each point
  • Speech: direct address (ladies and gentlemen), rhetorical questions, repetition for emphasis, sign-off (thank you), numbered points
  • Article: catchy headline, engaging opening, subheadings (optional), varied paragraphs, call to action or thought-provoking ending
  • Review: headline, summary judgement, specific details, comparison, recommendation
Key exam skill: even if the question does not specify a format, students must adapt their writing to suit the implied audience and purpose. A letter to a newspaper needs formal register; a speech to classmates can be informal.
Worked Example

Letter opening: "Dear Sir or Madam, I am writing to express my concern about the proposed closure of our local library."
Speech opening: "Good morning, everyone. I want you to imagine a world without libraries. No books to borrow, no quiet spaces to study, no free internet access for those who cannot afford it at home. That is the world we are sleepwalking towards."
Article opening: "The library on Elm Street has been a cornerstone of this community for thirty years. Next month, it may cease to exist. This is the story of what we stand to lose — and what we can still do to save it."

Practice Task

Write a formal letter to your headteacher arguing that the school canteen should offer more vegetarian options. Include all letter conventions and at least three persuasive techniques.

"Dear Headteacher,

I am writing to request that the school canteen expands its range of vegetarian meal options. Currently, vegetarian students have a choice of just two meals per week, compared to six options for meat-eaters. This sends an unfortunate message: that vegetarianism is an afterthought rather than a valid dietary choice.

I believe this change would benefit the whole school. Research shows that a wider range of meal options leads to higher canteen uptake, reduced food waste, and improved student wellbeing. Furthermore, many students — vegetarian or not — would appreciate more variety in their daily meals.

I have spoken to several students who share this view, and I would be happy to help organise a survey to gauge wider interest. I would be grateful if you could consider this proposal at your next meeting.

Yours faithfully,
[Student name]"

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a layout template for each text type. Students fill in the blanks rather than starting from scratch.
Stretch:
Write the same argument as a letter, a speech, and an article — adapting register and features for each format.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Feature Checklist

Students write a 3-item checklist of essential features for their chosen text type. Swap with a partner and check.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Includes all essential features of the text type with appropriate register and audience awareness
  • Getting there: Includes most features but register is inconsistent
  • Not yet: Writing does not match the required format

Lesson 3: Structuring Arguments

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Argument Map

Display a randomised list of arguments for and against school uniforms. Students put them in logical order. Discuss: what makes a convincing argument sequence?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach argument structure:
  • Opening: state your position clearly. Hook the reader.
  • Argument 1: strongest point with evidence
  • Argument 2: second point with evidence
  • Counterargument: acknowledge the opposing view, then rebut it
  • Conclusion: restate position, call to action or thought-provoking ending
Key exam technique: for AQA Paper 2 Q5, plan for 5 minutes before writing. Identify purpose (persuade/inform/advise), audience (who are you writing to?), and format (letter/speech/article). Then plan your arguments. Teach signposting language: "Furthermore", "In addition", "However", "On the other hand", "Nevertheless", "To summarise".
Worked Example

Signposting in an article:
Opening: "The case for longer holidays has never been stronger."
Argument 1: "First and foremost, student wellbeing demands it."
Argument 2: "Furthermore, the evidence from international comparisons is compelling."
Counterargument: "Critics argue that longer holidays widen the attainment gap for disadvantaged students."
Rebuttal: "However, this concern — while valid — can be addressed through targeted holiday programmes rather than shorter breaks for everyone."

Practice Task

Plan and write a 250-350 word article arguing that the voting age should be lowered to 16. Include: an engaging headline, a clear position, at least two arguments with evidence, one counterargument with rebuttal, and a strong conclusion.

Headline: "If They Can Work and Pay Tax, They Should Vote"
Opening: "Sixteen-year-olds can work, pay taxes, join the army, and get married — but they cannot vote. This is not just inconsistent. It is undemocratic.
Argument 1: "The most fundamental argument is one of representation. Young people are directly affected by government decisions on education, climate change, and housing. To deny them a vote is to silence the generation with the most at stake."
Argument 2: "Moreover, the evidence from Scotland is encouraging. When 16 and 17-year-olds were given the vote in the 2014 independence referendum, turnout among this age group was higher than among 18-24 year olds. They did not waste the opportunity — they seized it."
Counterargument + rebuttal: "Some argue that 16-year-olds lack the maturity to vote. But maturity is not something that magically appears on your 18th birthday. If we trust young people to make life-changing decisions about work and relationships, we should trust them to place a cross in a box."
Conclusion: "Democracy is not a privilege to be earned — it is a right to be extended. Lower the voting age. Trust our young people. They have earned it."

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a planning template with sections for each part of the argument. Reduce to 200 words.
Stretch:
Write a piece that addresses TWO counterarguments and rebuts both.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Strongest Argument

Students read each other's articles and identify the single most convincing argument. Discuss: what makes it work?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Writes a structured argument with clear signposting, evidence, counterargument, and conclusion
  • Getting there: Argument is structured but lacks counterargument or strong conclusion
  • Not yet: Arguments are listed without logical sequence

Homework Suggestions

  • Write a persuasive letter to a local politician about an issue you care about
  • Analyse a political speech for persuasive techniques and structural choices
  • Write the same argument as a speech and an article — compare the differences
  • Find an advert and identify every persuasive technique used, explaining its effect
