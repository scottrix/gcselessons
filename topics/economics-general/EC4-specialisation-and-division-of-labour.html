Lesson 2: Core Concepts: specialisation and division of labour

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on specialisation and division of labour. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Specialisation means focusing on producing a limited range of goods or services — individuals, firms, and countries can all specialise.
Key Fact: Division of labour breaks production into separate tasks, with each worker performing one task. Adam Smith identified this as a key source of productivity gains.
Key Fact: Benefits: increased productivity, time saved (no switching tasks), specialist equipment, higher output and lower unit costs.
Key Fact: Costs: worker boredom and demotivation, over-dependence on one product or market, skills become obsolete if the industry changes.
Key Fact: Specialisation leads to exchange because no one produces everything they need — workers earn wages from their specialism and buy other goods, creating interdependence.
Economics Exam Tips: When evaluating specialisation, use the PBR framework: Productivity gains, Boredom costs, Resilience (how vulnerable is the specialist to change?).

Individual workerHigh skill, higher pay, career progressionBoredom, deskilling, redundancy risk

Global tradeComparative advantage, greater total outputTrade disputes, supply chain disruption

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Explain three benefits and two costs of the division of labour for workers and firms.

Answer: Benefits: (1) Increased productivity — workers become skilled at one task. (2) Time saved — no switching between tasks. (3) Specialist equipment increases efficiency. Costs: (1) Boredom and demotivation from repetitive tasks. (2) Over-dependence — if demand falls, workers and firms have limited alternatives.

Lesson 3: Application: specialisation and division of labour

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Economics Exam Tips. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain three benefits and two costs of the division of labour for workers and firms.

Answer: Benefits: (1) Increased productivity — workers become skilled at one task. (2) Time saved — no switching between tasks. (3) Specialist equipment increases efficiency. Costs: (1) Boredom and demotivation from repetitive tasks. (2) Over-dependence — if demand falls, workers and firms have limited alternatives.

Q2: Analyse why specialisation leads to exchange and interdependence.

Answer: Specialisation means people produce only what they are best at, but need many goods they don't produce. A baker specialises in bread but needs clothes and housing — so they exchange bread (or wages) for these goods. This creates interdependence: we all rely on others. Without exchange, specialisation would be impossible.

Q3: Evaluate whether the benefits of specialisation always outweigh the costs.

Answer: Benefits are significant: higher productivity, lower costs, greater output, and economic growth. However, costs can be severe: regions that specialised in declining industries suffered mass unemployment. The 2008 crisis showed over-specialisation in banking created systemic risk. Conclusion: specialisation brings substantial benefits but requires flexibility — education and retraining are essential.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: specialisation and division of labour

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Evaluate whether a country should specialise in producing the goods it is best at, or diversify its economy. <div class="

A grade 9 response will: argue for specialisation (comparative advantage, higher productivity, more trade); argue for diversification (protection from price shocks, broader skills base, resilience); conclude: some specialisation is beneficial but over-specialisation is risky. A diversified economy with strong sectors is more resilient.

Exam Tips: Always give BOTH benefits AND costs of specialisation. | Link specialisation to TRADE: specialisation creates the need for exchange. | Adam Smith's pin factory is the classic example of division of labour.
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often make mistakes here. Wrong: Specialisation always benefits workers because it makes them more productive. Correct: While specialisation increases productivity, it can harm workers: narrowing skills (vulnerability to industry decline), causing monotony, reducing bargaining power. During deindustrialisation, millions of specialised manufacturing workers lost jobs and struggled because their skills were too narrow.
