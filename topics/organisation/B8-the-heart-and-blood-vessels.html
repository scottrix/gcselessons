B8: The Heart and Blood Vessels

Foundation Higher All Boards

Study the structure and function of the human heart, the types of blood vessels, and how double circulation efficiently delivers oxygen and nutrients to body tissues.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the structure of the heart and explain how it pumps blood
  • Name and describe the three types of blood vessels
  • Explain double circulation and its advantages
  • Describe how the heart rate is controlled (Higher)

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of the circulatory system at a basic level (B6)
  • Knowledge of cells and tissues

Materials & Equipment

  • Annotated diagram of the human heart
  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Diagram of blood vessel types (artery, vein, capillary)
  • Optional: sheep's heart for dissection (with appropriate safety measures)

Lesson 1: Structure of the Heart

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Heartbeat Count

Students take their pulse at the wrist (radial artery) for 15 seconds and multiply by 4 to get beats per minute. Record results. Ask: "What is making your heart beat? How fast does it need to beat?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The heart is a muscular organ slightly larger than a fist, located in the chest. It has four chambers:
  • Right atrium: receives deoxygenated blood from the body via the vena cava
  • Right ventricle: pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs via the pulmonary artery
  • Left atrium: receives oxygenated blood from the lungs via the pulmonary veins
  • Left ventricle: pumps oxygenated blood to the body via the aorta. Has the thickest muscular wall because it pumps blood to the whole body.
Valves prevent backflow of blood:
  • AV valves (tricuspid on right, bicuspid/mitral on left): between atria and ventricles
  • Semi-lunar valves: at the exits of the ventricles into the arteries
Blood flow through the heart: Deoxygenated blood → vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs (gas exchange) → pulmonary veins → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body.
Worked Example

"Why does the left ventricle have a thicker muscular wall than the right ventricle?" Answer: The left ventricle pumps blood to the entire body through the aorta, requiring more force and higher pressure. The right ventricle only pumps blood to the lungs, which are nearby and have low resistance, so less force is needed.

Common Misconception

"Blood passes through the heart from right to left directly"

Blood does NOT pass directly from the right side to the left side. It goes from the right side → lungs → left side. The two sides are completely separated by a septum. This ensures oxygenated and deoxygenated blood do not mix.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a pre-drawn heart diagram with labels to trace. Use two colours: blue for deoxygenated blood, red for oxygenated. Trace the path with arrows.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
If available, examine a real sheep's heart. Identify the four chambers, valves, and major blood vessels. Compare the thickness of the left and right ventricle walls and explain why.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Blood Flow Sequence

Give students cards with parts of the heart on them. Students arrange them in the correct order of blood flow. Check with a partner.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the four chambers, valves, and major blood vessels with the flow of blood
  • Getting there: Can name the chambers but confuses the flow of blood
  • Not yet: Cannot describe the structure of the heart

Lesson 2: Blood Vessels

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Compare and Contrast

Show diagrams of an artery, a vein, and a capillary cross-section. Ask students to write down as many differences as they can spot.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Three types of blood vessels:
FeatureArteryVeinCapillary
Carries bloodAway from heartTowards heartBetween arteries and veins
PressureHighLowVery low
Wall thicknessThick, muscular, elasticThin, less muscularOne cell thick
LumenSmallLargeVery small (one cell wide)
ValvesNoYes (prevent backflow)No
Blood typeUsually oxygenated*Usually deoxygenated*Exchange occurs here
*Exception: pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood; pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood. Capillaries are the site of exchange — their walls are one cell thick, allowing diffusion of oxygen, glucose, and waste products between blood and tissues.
Worked Example

"Explain why arteries have thick, muscular, elastic walls." Answer: Blood is pumped from the heart at high pressure. Thick muscular walls withstand this pressure. Elastic tissue stretches with each heartbeat and then recoils, helping to maintain blood pressure between beats.

Common Misconception

"Arteries always carry oxygenated blood"

Arteries carry blood AWAY from the heart. Most arteries carry oxygenated blood, but the pulmonary artery is an exception — it carries deoxygenated blood from the heart to the lungs. Veins carry blood towards the heart; most carry deoxygenated blood, but the pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on three key differences between arteries and veins: direction, wall thickness, and presence of valves. Use a simplified table. Provide a diagram for labelling.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research atherosclerosis and how it affects arteries. Explain how high blood pressure damages blood vessels and increases the risk of heart disease.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Vessel Identification

Show cross-section diagrams of different blood vessels. Students identify each one and give two reasons for their answer.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can compare all three vessel types in detail with correct biological reasoning
  • Getting there: Can distinguish arteries from veins but struggles with capillaries
  • Not yet: Cannot differentiate between blood vessel types

Lesson 3: Double Circulation and Heart Rate Control

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Exercise Pulse

Students do 20 star jumps, then immediately take their pulse. Compare with resting pulse from Lesson 1. Ask: "Why did your heart rate increase?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Double circulation:
  • The human circulatory system is a double circulation — blood passes through the heart twice for each complete circuit of the body
  • Pulmonary circuit: right side of heart → lungs → left side of heart (picks up oxygen)
  • Systemic circuit: left side of heart → body → right side of heart (delivers oxygen, picks up CO₂)
Advantages of double circulation:
  • Blood goes to the lungs at lower pressure (gentle gas exchange)
  • Blood goes to the body at high pressure (fast delivery of oxygen and nutrients)
  • More efficient than single circulation (fish have single circulation)
Heart rate control (Higher):
  • The heart has its own pace maker — the sinoatrial node (SAN) in the right atrium. It sends electrical impulses that cause the heart to beat regularly.
  • Heart rate is controlled by the medulla oblongata in the brain, which sends signals via the autonomic nervous system.
  • During exercise: medulla sends signals via the sympathetic nervous system → heart rate increases → more oxygen delivered to muscles.
  • During rest: medulla sends signals via the parasympathetic nervous system → heart rate decreases.
  • Hormones also affect heart rate: adrenaline (from adrenal glands) increases heart rate during "fight or flight."
Worked Example

"Explain why a double circulation is more efficient than a single circulation." Answer: In a double circulation, blood passes through the heart twice. This means blood can be pumped to the lungs at low pressure (protecting delicate capillaries) while being pumped to the body at high pressure (ensuring rapid delivery of oxygen). A single circulation (like in fish) can only have one pressure — high enough for the body would damage lung capillaries.

Common Misconception

"The heart beats because the brain tells it to"

The heart has its own natural pacemaker (SAN) that generates electrical impulses to cause rhythmic beating. The brain can speed up or slow down the heart rate, but the heart can beat independently — this is why a transplanted heart works.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Use a diagram showing the two loops of double circulation. Focus on: "blood goes through the heart twice." Simplify heart rate control to: "brain speeds up heart during exercise."
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research how a heart transplant works and why the SAN is not replaced. Investigate artificial pacemakers and how they regulate heart rhythm.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Summary Diagram

Students draw a simple diagram of double circulation showing the two circuits and label where oxygen is picked up and where it is delivered.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain double circulation and how heart rate is controlled by the nervous system and hormones
  • Getting there: Understands double circulation but cannot explain heart rate control mechanisms
  • Not yet: Cannot describe double circulation or its advantages

Homework Suggestions

  • Draw and label a detailed diagram of the human heart showing all four chambers, valves, and major blood vessels
  • Create a comparison table of arteries, veins, and capillaries with at least five features
  • Research coronary heart disease and write a paragraph explaining how lifestyle factors increase risk
  • Measure resting heart rate each morning for a week and record in a table — look for patterns
