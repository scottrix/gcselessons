B11: The Effect of Lifestyle on Non-Communicable Diseases

Higher All Boards

Analyse how lifestyle choices such as smoking, diet, and lack of exercise contribute to non-communicable diseases, and evaluate strategies to reduce their incidence.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Higher only
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the biological mechanisms by which smoking causes disease
  • Explain how diet and exercise affect the risk of coronary heart disease
  • Interpret epidemiological data to identify correlations between lifestyle and disease
  • Evaluate strategies to reduce the incidence of non-communicable diseases

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of communicable and non-communicable diseases (B10)
  • Basic knowledge of the circulatory system (B8)

Materials & Equipment

  • Epidemiological data sets (graphs, tables)
  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Case study cards for group work
  • Calculator for data analysis

Lesson 1: Smoking and Coronary Heart Disease

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Correlation Question

Show a graph of cigarette sales per capita vs death rates from coronary heart disease in different countries. Ask: "Does this graph prove that smoking causes heart disease? What else might explain the pattern?" Introduce the concept of correlation vs causation.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
How smoking damages the body — three main harmful substances:
  • Tar: deposited in the lungs, paralyses cilia (tiny hair-like structures that clear mucus and pathogens). This leads to chronic bronchitis and increases risk of lung cancer. Also enters the bloodstream and damages artery walls.
  • Carbon monoxide: binds to haemoglobin more strongly than oxygen (forms carboxyhaemoglobin). Reduces oxygen-carrying capacity of blood. The heart has to work harder to supply tissues with enough oxygen.
  • Nicotine: a stimulant that increases heart rate and blood pressure. Constricts (narrows) arteries, increasing the risk of atherosclerosis.
Coronary heart disease (CHD) mechanism:
  1. Smoking damages the inner lining of coronary arteries
  2. Fatty deposits (atheroma) build up at the damage sites (atherosclerosis)
  3. Arteries become narrower, restricting blood flow to the heart muscle
  4. If a blood clot forms (thrombosis), it can block the narrowed artery completely
  5. This causes a heart attack — the heart muscle is starved of oxygen
Risk factors for CHD:
  • Smoking (increases risk 2-4 times)
  • High blood pressure (damages artery walls)
  • High blood cholesterol (increases fatty deposits)
  • Obesity (increases strain on the heart)
  • Lack of exercise (weakens the heart, increases obesity risk)
  • Diabetes (damages blood vessels)
  • Genetic predisposition (family history)
  • Stress (increases blood pressure)
Worked Example

"Explain, step by step, how smoking increases the risk of a heart attack." Answer: (1) Tar and chemicals in cigarette smoke damage the endothelium (inner lining) of coronary arteries. (2) Cholesterol and fatty deposits accumulate at damage sites, forming atheroma. (3) The artery wall becomes thicker and narrower (atherosclerosis). (4) Blood flow to the heart muscle is reduced. (5) A blood clot (thrombus) may form at the narrowed site, completely blocking the artery. (6) The heart muscle is deprived of oxygen, causing a myocardial infarction (heart attack).

Common Misconception

"Only heavy smokers get heart disease"

Even light smoking (1-4 cigarettes per day) significantly increases the risk of CHD. There is no safe level of smoking. The risk increases with the number of cigarettes smoked per day and the duration of smoking.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a simplified flow diagram: Smoking → damages arteries → fatty deposits build up → arteries narrow → blood clot → heart attack. Focus on the key sequence.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the concept of "pack years" and how doctors quantify smoking exposure. Discuss why some long-term smokers never develop CHD (genetic variation in repair mechanisms).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Chain Explanation

Students write the chain of events from smoking to heart attack on a mini-whiteboard. Peer assess for completeness.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the mechanism of CHD caused by smoking with clear biological reasoning
  • Getting there: Knows smoking causes heart disease but cannot explain the biological mechanism
  • Not yet: Cannot link smoking to heart disease or describe atherosclerosis

Lesson 2: Diet, Exercise, and Evaluating Prevention Strategies

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Data Analysis

Show a table comparing death rates from CHD in countries with different dietary habits. Students identify patterns and write a hypothesis about the relationship between diet and CHD.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Diet and CHD:
  • Saturated fat: increases blood cholesterol levels → more fatty deposits in arteries → atherosclerosis
  • Salt (sodium): increases blood pressure → more strain on the heart and arteries
  • Sugar: excess intake leads to obesity → increases risk of type 2 diabetes and CHD
  • Fibre: reduces cholesterol absorption, aids digestion, helps maintain healthy weight
Exercise and CHD:
  • Strengthens the heart muscle, making it more efficient
  • Increases HDL ("good") cholesterol and decreases LDL ("bad") cholesterol
  • Helps maintain a healthy weight, reducing obesity risk
  • Lowers blood pressure
  • Improves insulin sensitivity, reducing diabetes risk
Evaluating prevention strategies:
  • Public health campaigns: e.g. anti-smoking adverts, sugar tax, 5-a-day campaigns. Can change behaviour but expensive and not equally effective for all groups.
  • Legislation: e.g. ban on smoking in public places, mandatory food labelling,限制 advertising of junk food to children. Effective but can be seen as infringing personal freedom.
  • Screening: e.g. blood pressure checks, cholesterol tests. Early detection but costs money and may cause anxiety.
  • Education: e.g. teaching about healthy eating in schools. Long-term benefits but takes time to show effects.
When evaluating, students should consider: effectiveness, cost, practicality, and ethical considerations.
Worked Example

"Evaluate the effectiveness of a sugar tax in reducing obesity." Strengths: evidence from Mexico shows sugar tax reduced sugary drink purchases. A relatively simple policy to implement. Generates revenue that can fund health programmes. Weaknesses: may disproportionately affect low-income families. Manufacturers may reformulate products rather than reduce sugar content overall. Does not address other causes of obesity (lack of exercise, portion sizes).

Common Misconception

"Exercise only helps with weight control"

Right: While exercise helps maintain a healthy weight, it also directly strengthens the heart, lowers blood pressure, improves cholesterol levels, and increases insulin sensitivity — all independently reducing CHD risk even without weight loss.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a template for evaluation: "This strategy is effective because... However, it has limitations because..." Focus on one strategy in depth rather than covering all.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Compare CHD rates between countries with different health policies (e.g. Japan vs UK). Discuss why cultural factors make it difficult to apply successful strategies from one country to another.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Top Three

Students rank the prevention strategies (campaigns, legislation, screening, education) from most to least effective and justify their top choice in two sentences.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain how diet and exercise affect CHD risk and evaluate prevention strategies with balanced reasoning
  • Getting there: Can describe diet/exercise effects but cannot evaluate strategies effectively
  • Not yet: Cannot link diet or exercise to CHD or evaluate prevention strategies

Homework Suggestions

  • An epidemiological data set on smoking and lung cancer: identify the correlation, explain why correlation does not prove causation, and suggest how causation could be demonstrated
  • Design a public health campaign to reduce one non-communicable disease. Include target audience, key message, and medium of communication
  • Write a balanced essay: "Should governments ban smoking completely?" with arguments for and against
  • Calculate BMI for different individuals and classify them as underweight, normal, overweight, or obese. Discuss limitations of BMI as a health indicator.
