B9: Plant Organisation

Foundation Higher All Boards

Explore how plants are organised into tissues and transport systems, including the structure of leaves and how water and nutrients move through xylem and phloem.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the structure of a leaf and how it is adapted for photosynthesis
  • Explain how water is transported through xylem by transpiration
  • Describe how sugars are transported through phloem by translocation
  • Investigate the rate of transpiration using a potometer

Prerequisites

  • Knowledge of specialised plant cells (B4)
  • Understanding of xylem and phloem

Materials & Equipment

  • Leaf cross-section diagrams
  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Potometer (if available) or images/video of potometer experiment
  • Fresh leaves for examination
  • Hand lens / magnifying glass

Lesson 1: Structure of the Leaf

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Observe a Leaf

Give each student a fresh leaf. Ask them to observe it with a hand lens. What can they see? (Veins, waxy surface, different shades of green.) Ask: "Why do leaves need to be flat and thin?" (To capture light and allow gas exchange.)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The leaf is the main organ of photosynthesis. Its structure is adapted for this function:
  • Upper epidermis: single layer of cells, transparent to let light through. Covered by a waxy cuticle to reduce water loss.
  • Palisade mesophyll: column-shaped cells packed with chloroplasts (up to 100 per cell). This is the main site of photosynthesis. Located near the upper surface to maximise light absorption.
  • Spongy mesophyll: loosely packed cells with large air spaces between them. Allows gas diffusion (CO₂ in, O₂ out). Fewer chloroplasts than palisade layer.
  • Lower epidermis: contains guard cells that surround stomata (pores). Stomata open and close to control gas exchange and water loss. More stomata on lower surface to reduce water loss from direct sunlight.
  • Vascular bundles (veins): contain xylem (transports water and minerals up from roots) and phloem (transports sugars from leaves to other parts of the plant). Veins also provide structural support to the leaf.
Draw and label a cross-section of a leaf. Include all layers.
Worked Example

"Explain why the palisade mesophyll is located near the upper surface of the leaf." Answer: The palisade layer contains the most chloroplasts and is the main site of photosynthesis. Being near the upper surface maximises exposure to sunlight, which provides the light energy needed for photosynthesis.

Common Misconception

"All cells in a leaf contain chloroplasts"

Not all leaf cells contain chloroplasts. The epidermal cells (upper and lower) are transparent and generally lack chloroplasts (except guard cells). The vascular bundle cells (xylem and phloem) also do not contain chloroplasts. Only mesophyll cells and guard cells have significant numbers of chloroplasts.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a pre-drawn leaf cross-section with labels to add. Focus on three key layers: palisade (photosynthesis), spongy (gas exchange), and stomata (gas exchange and water loss).
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Compare the leaf structure of a dicotyledon (broad leaf) with a monocotyledon (grass leaf). Discuss why monocots have parallel veins and dicots have branching veins.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Label

Show an unlabeled leaf cross-section. Students label as many parts as they can from memory in two minutes.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe all layers of the leaf and explain how each is adapted for photosynthesis
  • Getting there: Can name the main layers but cannot explain their functions
  • Not yet: Cannot identify the layers of a leaf or their roles

Lesson 2: Transpiration and Xylem

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Prediction

Show a plant in a sealed plastic bag with condensation on the inside. Ask: "Where did the water come from?" (It evaporated from the leaves.) This introduces transpiration.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Transpiration: the evaporation of water from the surfaces of mesophyll cells in the leaf, through the stomata, and out into the atmosphere. The transpiration stream:
  • Water evaporates from mesophyll cell surfaces into the air spaces in the spongy mesophyll
  • Water vapour diffuses out through the open stomata
  • This creates a "pull" (transpiration pull) that draws water up through the xylem from the roots
  • Water enters the root hair cells by osmosis from the soil
  • Water moves from cell to cell through the root cortex until it reaches the xylem
Xylem tissue:
  • Dead cells joined end to end with no end walls — forms hollow tubes
  • Cell walls strengthened with lignin (waterproof, rigid)
  • Transports water and dissolved mineral ions from roots to leaves
  • Provides structural support (keeps the plant upright)
Factors affecting transpiration rate:
  • Temperature (higher → faster evaporation)
  • Humidity (lower → faster evaporation)
  • Wind (blows away water vapour, maintains gradient)
  • Light intensity (more light → stomata open wider)
Worked Example

"Explain why transpiration is faster on a hot, dry, windy day." Answer: Higher temperature increases the rate of evaporation from mesophyll cells. Low humidity means there is a steeper water concentration gradient between the leaf and the outside air. Wind blows away water vapour from around the leaf, maintaining the concentration gradient. All three factors increase the rate of transpiration.

Common Misconception

"Transpiration is the same as photosynthesis"

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from leaves. Photosynthesis is the process of making glucose using light energy. Transpiration is a consequence of having open stomata for gas exchange — water loss is unavoidable.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Use a simple diagram showing water entering roots, travelling up the stem, and evaporating from leaves. Focus on: "water is pulled up the plant by evaporation from leaves."
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Investigate using a potometer how different environmental conditions affect the rate of water uptake. Design a fair test to investigate one factor.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Factor Match

Match each factor to its effect on transpiration: increased temperature → faster, increased humidity → slower, increased wind → faster, darkness → slower (stomata close).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the transpiration stream and explain how environmental factors affect its rate
  • Getting there: Understands that water is pulled up but cannot explain the mechanism
  • Not yet: Cannot describe transpiration or its causes

Lesson 3: Translocation and Phloem

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Think About It

Ask: "Photosynthesis happens in the leaves. But roots and growing tips also need glucose. How does glucose get from the leaves to the rest of the plant?" Introduce translocation.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Translocation: the movement of sucrose and amino acids (dissolved food) through the phloem from where they are made (sources) to where they are needed (sinks). Phloem tissue:
  • Sieve tube elements: living cells joined end to end, perforated end walls (sieve plates), no nucleus at maturity
  • Companion cells: lie alongside sieve tubes, have a nucleus and many mitochondria, provide ATP and proteins to the sieve tubes
Sources and sinks:
  • Source: where sugars are made or stored (leaves during summer, storage organs like potato tubers)
  • Sink: where sugars are used or stored (roots, growing shoots, fruits, storage organs)
How translocation works (simplified):
  • At the source, sucrose is loaded into the sieve tubes by active transport
  • This lowers the water potential inside the sieve tubes → water enters by osmosis from nearby xylem
  • The increased pressure pushes the sucrose solution towards the sinks
  • At the sinks, sucrose is removed by active transport and water follows by osmosis
Translocation can occur in both directions (up and down the plant), unlike transpiration which only goes up.
Worked Example

"Explain how glucose produced in the leaves in summer reaches the roots for storage." Answer: Glucose is converted to sucrose and loaded into the phloem sieve tubes at the leaves (source) by active transport. Water enters by osmosis, creating pressure that pushes the sucrose solution down to the roots (sink), where it is removed and stored as starch.

Common Misconception

"Phloem transports water"

Phloem transports dissolved food (sucrose and amino acids), not water. Xylem transports water and dissolved mineral ions. Water moves into phloem by osmosis, but this is a mechanism for creating pressure to move the sugar solution — the phloem's function is food transport.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Compare xylem and phloem in a simple table: what they transport, direction, alive/dead. Focus on the key difference: xylem = water up, phloem = food up and down.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the pressure flow hypothesis in detail. Explain how a plant could transport sugars from roots (storage) to growing tips (sink) in spring.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Xylem vs Phloem

Students complete a comparison table of xylem and phloem from memory: what they transport, direction, structure, and whether cells are alive or dead.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain translocation, describe phloem structure, and compare xylem and phloem
  • Getting there: Knows phloem transports food but cannot explain the mechanism
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between xylem and phloem functions

Homework Suggestions

  • Draw and label a leaf cross-section showing all tissue layers
  • Create a comparison table of xylem and phloem with at least six features
  • Investigate transpiration at home: place a cut celery stalk in coloured water and observe after 24 hours (the coloured water shows xylem transport)
  • Research deforestation and explain how removing trees affects the water cycle
