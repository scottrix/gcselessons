B7: The Digestive System

Foundation Higher All Boards

Learn how the digestive system breaks down food using mechanical and chemical digestion, enzymes, and absorption through the small intestine.

Lesson Overview
Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the structure and function of the organs of the digestive system
  • Explain the difference between mechanical and chemical digestion
  • Name the key digestive enzymes and their substrates and products
  • Describe how absorption occurs in the small intestine

Prerequisites

  • Basic knowledge that food provides energy and nutrients
  • Understanding of cells and tissues

Materials & Equipment

  • Diagram of the digestive system
  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Properties of materials for testing (e.g. test tubes, food samples, iodine, Biuret reagent, Benedict's solution) if doing practical
  • Enzyme summary table handout

Lesson 1: The Digestive System Structure

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Food Journey

Ask: "What happens to a piece of bread from the moment it enters your mouth to when it leaves your body?" Students write a quick sequence. Discuss and introduce the organs involved.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Trace the path of food through the digestive system:
  • Mouth: teeth break food into smaller pieces (mechanical digestion). Saliva contains the enzyme amylase, which starts breaking down starch into maltose. Tongue mixes food into a bolus.
  • Oesophagus: muscular tube that carries food to the stomach by peristalsis (rhythmic muscle contractions).
  • Stomach: muscular bag that churns food (mechanical digestion). Produces gastric juice containing hydrochloric acid (kills bacteria, provides acidic pH for enzymes) and the enzyme protease (rennin and pepsin), which begins protein digestion. Food becomes a semi-liquid called chyme.
  • Small intestine: where most chemical digestion and absorption occurs. Receives bile from the liver (emulsifies fats) and digestive enzymes from the pancreas (lipase, protease, amylase). The inner wall has villi and microvilli to increase surface area for absorption.
  • Liver: produces bile (stored in gallbladder). Bile emulsifies fats — breaks large fat droplets into smaller droplets, increasing surface area for lipase to work.
  • Pancreas: produces digestive enzymes (lipase, protease, amylase) and bicarbonate ions to neutralise stomach acid.
  • Large intestine: absorbs remaining water and forms faeces.
  • Rectum and anus: stores and eliminates faeces (egestion).
Worked Example

"Trace the path of a piece of protein from mouth to absorption." Answer: Mouth (no protein digestion) → Oesophagus → Stomach (protease begins breaking protein into amino acids) → Small intestine (pancreatic protease completes digestion; amino acids absorbed through villi into blood) → Large intestine (remaining waste).

Common Misconception

"The stomach absorbs most nutrients"

The stomach primarily breaks down food mechanically and begins protein digestion. Most chemical digestion and nearly all absorption occurs in the small intestine, where villi and microvilli provide a huge surface area.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a cut-out diagram of the digestive system. Students label the organs in order. Focus on mouth, stomach, small intestine as the three key organs.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the role of the liver in detail — what happens if the liver fails? Discuss liver transplants and artificial liver machines.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Journey Narrative

Students write a first-person account of a molecule of glucose travelling through the digestive system, from bread to absorption.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the journey of food through the digestive system with functions of each organ
  • Getting there: Can name the organs but confuses some functions
  • Not yet: Cannot sequence the organs of the digestive system

Lesson 2: Enzymes in Digestion

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Analogy

Compare enzymes to scissors: they cut large molecules into smaller pieces but are not used up in the process. Ask: "What might happen if we didn't have these molecular scissors?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Enzymes are biological catalysts — they speed up chemical reactions without being permanently changed. They are proteins with a specific shape that fits their substrate (lock and key model). Key digestive enzymes:
EnzymeProduced bySubstrateProductsWhere
AmylaseSalivary glands, pancreasStarchMaltose (sugar)Mouth, small intestine
Protease (pepsin)Stomach liningProteinAmino acidsStomach
Protease (trypsin)PancreasProteinAmino acidsSmall intestine
LipasePancreasFats/lipidsFatty acids + glycerolSmall intestine
Key points:
  • Enzymes work best at specific temperatures and pH values
  • Amylase works best at neutral pH (mouth, small intestine) and 37°C
  • Protease (pepsin) works best at pH 2 (acidic stomach environment)
  • Lipase works best at slightly alkaline pH (small intestine)
  • At temperatures above 40-45°C, enzymes are denatured (their shape changes and they no longer work)
Bile is NOT an enzyme — it is an emulsifier that breaks fats into smaller droplets mechanically, increasing surface area for lipase.
Worked Example

"State the enzyme, substrate, and products for the digestion of starch in the mouth." Answer: Enzyme = amylase, Substrate = starch, Product = maltose (a sugar). Amylase is secreted by the salivary glands and works at neutral pH in the mouth.

Common Misconception

"Bile is an enzyme"

Bile is NOT an enzyme. It is produced by the liver and stored in the gallbladder. It is an emulsifier — it breaks large fat droplets into smaller ones by a mechanical process (not chemical). This increases the surface area for lipase (the actual enzyme) to work on.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a completed enzyme table for students to learn. Focus on three key enzymes: amylase, protease, lipase. Use colour coding: substrate in blue, enzyme in red, products in green.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research enzyme inhibitors and how some poisons work by denaturing enzymes. Investigate how the stomach lining protects itself from its own protease (mucus layer).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Speed Quiz

Teacher calls out a substrate (e.g. "Starch!"), students must name the enzyme ("Amylase!"), where it's made ("Salivary glands and pancreas!"), and the products ("Maltose!"). Quick fire round.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can name all key enzymes with substrates, products, and locations
  • Getting there: Can name some enzymes but mixes up substrates and products
  • Not yet: Cannot name digestive enzymes or their substrates

Lesson 3: Absorption in the Small Intestine

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Surface Area Question

Ask: "Why is the small intestine about 6 metres long when the small intestine is called 'small'?" Explain that "small" refers to the diameter, not the length. The huge surface area is key for absorption.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The small intestine is adapted for absorption:
  • Villi: finger-like projections on the inner wall (about 0.5-1mm tall). Each villus contains a network of blood capillaries and a lacteal (lymph vessel). Villi increase surface area by about 10 times.
  • Microvilli: tiny projections on the surface of each villus cell (enterocyte). Further increase surface area by about 20 times. Together with villi, the total surface area is enormous — roughly the size of a tennis court.
  • Thin walls: villi have very thin walls (one cell thick) for rapid diffusion/absorption.
  • Rich blood supply: capillaries in each villus quickly carry away absorbed nutrients, maintaining the concentration gradient.
What is absorbed and where:
  • Glucose and amino acids → absorbed into blood capillaries → hepatic portal vein → liver
  • Fatty acids and glycerol → absorbed into lacteals → lymphatic system → bloodstream
  • Water → absorbed by osmosis throughout the small and large intestine
  • Mineral ions → absorbed by active transport
The large intestine absorbs remaining water and some vitamins produced by gut bacteria.
Worked Example

"Explain how the structure of the small intestine is adapted for efficient absorption." Answer: Villi increase surface area; thin walls (one cell thick) allow fast diffusion; rich blood supply maintains concentration gradient; microvilli further increase surface area; lacteals absorb fatty acids. All these features maximise the rate of absorption.

Common Misconception

"The small intestine is called 'small' because it is short"

The small intestine is about 6 metres long — it is called "small" because it has a small diameter (about 2.5 cm). The large intestine is called "large" because it has a larger diameter (about 7.5 cm), even though it is shorter (about 1.5 metres).

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a diagram of villi for labelling. Use a practical demonstration: scrunch a piece of paper into a ball vs. flatten it — which has more surface area? Relate to why villi increase absorption.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Calculate the surface area of the small intestine given villi dimensions. Research coeliac disease and how damage to villi affects absorption. Compare absorption in the small and large intestine.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Adaptation Challenge

Students list as many adaptations of the small intestine as they can in two minutes. Compare with a partner and create a combined list.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain how villi and microvilli increase absorption with clear structure-function links
  • Getting there: Knows that villi increase surface area but cannot explain other adaptations
  • Not yet: Cannot describe the adaptations of the small intestine

Homework Suggestions

  • Create an enzyme revision table with columns for enzyme name, substrate, products, and where it works
  • Draw and label a villus showing the blood capillaries, lacteal, and microvilli
  • Research what happens when someone has their gallbladder removed — how does this affect fat digestion?
  • Write a paragraph explaining why denaturation of enzymes is irreversible
