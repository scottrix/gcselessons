B10: Health and Disease

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand the difference between communicable and non-communicable diseases, the role of lifestyle factors, and how diseases are prevented and treated.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish between communicable and non-communicable diseases
  • Give examples of diseases caused by different types of pathogens
  • Describe how lifestyle factors increase the risk of non-communicable diseases
  • Explain the terms epidemic and pandemic

Prerequisites

  • Basic knowledge of microorganisms as pathogens

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Printed disease classification cards for sorting activities
  • Graphs showing disease prevalence data

Lesson 1: Communicable and Non-Communicable Diseases

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Name That Disease

Write disease names on the board: flu, cancer, malaria, heart disease, diabetes, HIV, asthma. Ask students to sort them into two groups. Discuss the difference between diseases that spread between people and those that don't.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Key definitions:
  • Disease: a disorder of structure or function that causes signs and symptoms
  • Pathogen: a microorganism that causes disease
  • Communicable disease: caused by pathogens that can spread from person to person (infectious diseases)
  • Non-communicable disease: NOT caused by pathogens and cannot be spread between people (e.g. cancer, heart disease, diabetes)
Types of pathogens:
Pathogen TypeExamplesDiseases Caused
BacteriaSalmonella, StreptococcusFood poisoning, throat infections
VirusesInfluenza, HIV, EbolaFlu, AIDS, viral haemorrhagic fever
FungiCandidaThrush, athlete's foot
Protists (Protoctists)PlasmodiumMalaria
How communicable diseases spread:
  • Direct contact (touching, kissing, sexual contact)
  • Droplet spread (coughing, sneezing)
  • Airborne (tiny particles in air)
  • Waterborne (contaminated water)
  • Vectors (mosquitoes, ticks, flies)
  • Contaminated food or surfaces (fomites)
Non-communicable diseases are caused by a combination of:
  • Genetic factors (inherited genes)
  • Lifestyle factors (diet, exercise, smoking, alcohol)
  • Environmental factors (pollution, UV radiation)
Worked Example

"Classify each disease as communicable or non-communicable and give a reason: malaria, lung cancer, tuberculosis, type 2 diabetes." Answer: Malaria = communicable (spread by mosquito vector). Lung cancer = non-communicable (caused by smoking/chemicals, not a pathogen). Tuberculosis = communicable (spread by airborne bacteria). Type 2 diabetes = non-communicable (related to lifestyle and genetics).

Common Misconception

"All diseases are caused by germs"

Non-communicable diseases are NOT caused by pathogens. They are caused by a combination of genetic, lifestyle, and environmental factors. Examples include heart disease, cancer, and type 2 diabetes. Most deaths in developed countries are from non-communicable diseases.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a simple sorting activity: disease cards into "catching" and "not catching" piles. Focus on the key vocabulary: communicable = spreads, non-communicable = does not spread.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the difference between epidemic and pandemic. Discuss why non-communicable diseases are increasing in developing countries. Analyse data on global causes of death.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Fire Classification

Read out 8 diseases. Students hold up a card: C for communicable, NC for non-communicable. Discuss any disagreements.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can classify diseases correctly and explain the role of pathogens and lifestyle factors
  • Getting there: Can classify most diseases but cannot explain why
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between communicable and non-communicable diseases

Lesson 2: Lifestyle Factors and Prevention

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Graph Analysis

Show a graph of smoking rates vs lung cancer rates over the past 50 years. Ask: "What do you notice about the relationship?" Discuss correlation and how it suggests a link.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Key lifestyle factors and their associated non-communicable diseases:
  • Smoking: increases risk of lung cancer, heart disease, COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), stroke. Tar damages cilia in the lungs, carbon monoxide reduces oxygen-carrying capacity of blood, nicotine increases heart rate and blood pressure.
  • Poor diet: high fat/sugar intake increases risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, heart disease, some cancers. Lack of fruit and vegetables reduces fibre and vitamin intake.
  • Lack of exercise: increases risk of obesity, heart disease, type 2 diabetes. Exercise strengthens the heart, improves circulation, helps maintain healthy weight.
  • Excessive alcohol consumption: increases risk of liver disease (cirrhosis), some cancers, heart disease. Alcohol is a toxin that damages liver cells.
  • UV radiation (sun exposure): increases risk of skin cancer. UV damages DNA in skin cells.
Prevention strategies:
  • Public health campaigns (e.g. anti-smoking, healthy eating)
  • Regular exercise (at least 150 minutes moderate activity per week)
  • Balanced diet (5-a-day, reduce salt and sugar)
  • Wearing sunscreen and avoiding excessive sun exposure
  • Regular health screening (blood pressure, cholesterol)
Epidemics and pandemics:
  • Epidemic: a disease that spreads rapidly and affects many people in a community or region at the same time
  • Pandemic: an epidemic that spreads across multiple countries or continents (e.g. COVID-19, Spanish flu)
  • Endemic: a disease that is constantly present in a particular population or region (e.g. malaria in tropical regions)
Worked Example

"Explain how smoking increases the risk of heart disease." Answer: (1) Tar sticks to artery walls, narrowing them (atherosclerosis). (2) Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin, reducing oxygen-carrying capacity. (3) Nicotine increases heart rate and blood pressure. Together, these increase the risk of blood clots, heart attack, and stroke.

Common Misconception

"Only old people get non-communicable diseases"

While risk increases with age, lifestyle factors mean non-communicable diseases can affect younger people too. Type 2 diabetes is increasingly diagnosed in teenagers. Heart disease risk factors begin accumulating from childhood.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on three key lifestyle factors: smoking, diet, exercise. For each, name one disease it causes. Provide a simple infographic to complete.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the social and economic factors that influence lifestyle choices (e.g. poverty, education, advertising). Discuss whether governments should intervene to change people's behaviour.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Risk Factor Match

Match each lifestyle factor to the non-communicable disease it increases the risk of. Students write on mini-whiteboards.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain how at least three lifestyle factors increase disease risk with specific biological mechanisms
  • Getting there: Can name lifestyle factors and associated diseases but lacks biological detail
  • Not yet: Cannot link lifestyle factors to non-communicable diseases

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a poster showing the health effects of smoking on different organs of the body
  • Research one communicable disease and create an information sheet covering: pathogen type, how it spreads, symptoms, treatment, and prevention
  • Analyse a dataset on disease prevalence in different countries and write a paragraph explaining patterns
  • Write a persuasive letter to a teenager about the importance of a healthy lifestyle
