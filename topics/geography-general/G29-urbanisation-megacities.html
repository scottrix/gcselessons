Lesson 2: Core Concepts: urbanisation and megacities

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on urbanisation and megacities. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Definition: Urbanisation is the increase in the proportion of people living in urban areas (towns and cities) compared to rural areas (countryside). It is caused by rural-urban migration and natural increase within cities.
Key Fact: HICs (High-Income Countries) are already highly urbanised (over 75%). The fastest urbanisation is happening in LICs (Low-Income Countries) and NEEs (Newly Emerging Economies), where rates of urban growth can exceed 4% per year.
Key Concept: Rural-urban migration is driven by push factors (negative conditions that drive people away from rural areas) and pull factors (positive attractions that draw people to cities). People weigh up both sets of factors when deciding to move.
Definition: Natural increase is the growth of the urban population through births exceeding deaths. It accounts for roughly 60% of urban growth in LICs and NEEs, with migration accounting for the remaining 40%.
Definition: A megacity is an urban area with a population of 10 million or more. In 2020, there were approximately 34 megacities worldwide, most located in LICs and NEEs.

Lesson 3: Application: urbanisation and megacities

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Concept. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Define the term urbanisation. (2 marks)

Answer: Urbanisation is the increase in the proportion of a country's population living in urban areas (towns and cities) rather than rural areas (1 mark). It is caused by rural-urban migration (people moving from countryside to city) and natural increase (births exceeding deaths within urban populations) (1 mark).

Q2: Explain how push and pull factors contribute to rural-urban migration. (4 marks)

Answer: Push factors drive people away from rural areas - low agricultural wages, lack of services (healthcare, education, electricity), drought and famine, and lack of employment opportunities (1 mark). For example, in many LICs, rural farmers earn less than $2 per day, pushing them to seek better incomes in cities (1 mark). Pull factors attract people to urban areas - better employment prospects in industry and services, higher wages, better healthcare and education, and access to services like electricity and clean water (1 mark). Migrants weigh up both sets of factors and decide that the benefits of city life outweigh the disadvantages of staying in rural areas (1 mark).

Q3: Why are most megacities now located in LICs and NEEs? (6 marks)

Answer: Most megacities are now in LICs and NEEs because these countries are experiencing the most rapid urbanisation (1 mark). HICs are already highly urbanised (over 75%) and their urban growth has slowed, meaning their cities grow slowly if at all (1 mark). LICs and NEEs have high rates of rural-urban migration as people are pushed from the countryside by poverty and pulled to cities by job opportunities (1 mark). Natural increase is high in LIC/NEE cities because migrants are young adults with high birth rates, and improved urban healthcare has reduced death rates (1 mark). Economic growth in NEEs like China and India has concentrated industrial jobs in cities, attracting millions of workers (1 mark). Globalisation has directed foreign investment to large cities in NEEs, creating employment that fuels further urban growth (1 mark).

Q4: "Natural increase is a more significant cause of urban growth than migration." To what extent do you agree? (9 marks)

Answer: I agree that natural increase is very significant, accounting for approximately 60% of urban population growth in LICs and NEEs (1 mark). This is because migrants are predominantly young adults of childbearing age, leading to high urban birth rates (1 mark). Improved healthcare in cities reduces death rates, widening the gap between births and deaths and increasing natural growth (1 mark). In Lagos, natural increase alone adds 2.6% to the population annually (1 mark). However, migration is also a crucial driver, accounting for about 40% of urban growth (1 mark). Without migration, cities would not have the young populations that generate high natural increase - migration and natural increase are interlinked (1 mark). In some cities, migration is the dominant cause - Dhaka's explosive growth from 1.5 million to 22 million was initially driven by mass rural-urban migration (1 mark). Migration also contributes indirectly to natural increase by bringing young adults to cities (1 mark). The relative importance varies between cities and over time - migration may dominate during early urbanisation, while natural increase becomes more significant once a large urban population is established (1 mark). In conclusion, while natural increase contributes more to overall urban growth numerically, migration is the catalyst that creates the conditions for natural increase, making both essential and interdependent causes of urban growth (1 mark).

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: urbanisation and megacities

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on urbanisation and megacities from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Always define urbanisation before discussing its causes or impacts | Use specific facts and statistics for megacities (population, growth rates) | For push-pull questions, give equal attention to both sets of factors | Know the difference between migration and natural increase as causes of urban growth | Explain that natural increase and migration are INTERLINKED, not separate | Use LIC/NEE examples for megacities - examiners expect Dhaka, Lagos, Mumbai
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often write vague answers without specific geographical evidence. Wrong: Writing generalised statements like 'it causes problems' Correct: Using specific data and named examples, e.g. 'the 2010 Haiti earthquake killed over 200,000 people due to poor building quality' Students often confuse causes and effects. Wrong: Mixing up what caused the event with what resulted from it Correct: Clearly separate causes (why it happened) from effects (what happened as a result) Students often describe rather than evaluate. Wrong: Listing strategies without assessing their effectiveness Correct: Weighing up strengths and weaknesses of each approach and reaching a supported judgement
