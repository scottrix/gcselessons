Lesson 2: Core Concepts: glacial tourism and management

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on glacial tourism and management. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Concept: Glaciated upland landscapes are attractive for tourism, recreation and economic development because of their dramatic scenery, distinctive landforms and sense of wilderness. However, their fragile environments and limited accessibility create challenges.
Key Fact: The Lake District receives approximately 18 million visitors per year, generating an estimated £1.4 billion for the local economy and supporting around 18,000 jobs. Tourism accounts for roughly 40% of the Lake District's economy.
Key Concept: The multiple uses of glaciated uplands create conflicts between different interest groups. Tourists, farmers, conservationists, quarrying companies, foresters and local residents all want different things from the same landscape.
Key Facts: The Lake District National Park was established in 1951 and covers 2,362 km². It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2017. It receives approximately 18 million visitors per year and has around 42,000 residents.
Exam Tip: When evaluating management in glaciated uplands, always consider whether strategies are sustainable (can they continue long-term?), whether they address all stakeholders' needs, and whether they create new problems. The best approaches are partnerships between different groups.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Describe two economic activities in glaciated upland areas. (4 marks)

Answer: Tourism is the largest economic activity, with 18 million visitors per year to the Lake District generating £1.4 billion and 18,000 jobs through accommodation, outdoor activities and retail (2 marks). Hill farming (sheep rearing) is the traditional land use, maintaining the open landscape with hardy breeds like Herdwick sheep, though incomes are low and dependent on subsidies (2 marks).

Lesson 3: Application: glacial tourism and management

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Concept, Key Concept, Key Facts. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Describe two economic activities in glaciated upland areas. (4 marks)

Answer: Tourism is the largest economic activity, with 18 million visitors per year to the Lake District generating £1.4 billion and 18,000 jobs through accommodation, outdoor activities and retail (2 marks). Hill farming (sheep rearing) is the traditional land use, maintaining the open landscape with hardy breeds like Herdwick sheep, though incomes are low and dependent on subsidies (2 marks).

Q2: Explain why land use conflicts arise in glaciated uplands. (4 marks)

Answer: Land use conflicts arise because multiple groups want different things from the same landscape (1 mark). Tourists want access and facilities, creating footpath erosion, traffic congestion and litter that damages the environment (1 mark). Second homes drive up house prices, forcing local people out of communities (1 mark). Quarrying provides employment but creates visual and noise pollution that reduces the area's appeal for tourism and conservation (1 mark).

Q3: Using a named glaciated upland area, describe the management strategies used to reduce conflict. (6 marks)

Answer: In the Lake District National Park, the LDNPA manages conflicts through several strategies. The Fix the Fells programme repairs eroded footpaths using stone pitching and geotextile membranes, addressing the conflict between tourist access and landscape protection (1 mark). Park and ride schemes and improved bus services reduce traffic congestion while still allowing visitor access (1 mark). Environmental Stewardship schemes pay farmers for conservation management, balancing farming needs with environmental protection (1 mark). Planning controls prevent inappropriate development while allowing necessary economic activity (1 mark). The Sandford Principle ensures conservation takes priority when purposes conflict (1 mark). Partnerships between LDNPA, National Trust, farmers and local businesses help find balanced solutions (1 mark).

Q4: "Tourism brings more problems than benefits to glaciated upland areas." To what extent do you agree? (9 marks)

Answer: I disagree that tourism brings more problems than benefits. Tourism generates enormous economic benefits - in the Lake District, 18 million visitors create £1.4 billion of income and support 18,000 jobs, providing livelihoods in an area with few alternative employers (1 mark). Tourism supports local services like pubs, shops and buses that permanent residents also depend on (1 mark). The Lake District's UNESCO World Heritage status was partly based on its cultural landscape shaped by visitors (1 mark). However, tourism does create significant problems. Footpath erosion costs around £500,000 per year to repair through Fix the Fells (1 mark). Traffic congestion on narrow roads affects both visitors and residents (1 mark). Second homes and holiday lets price out local people, hollowing out communities (1 mark). Litter, noise and wildlife disturbance degrade the very environment visitors come to enjoy (1 mark). Seasonal employment is often low-paid and insecure (1 mark). On balance, tourism's economic benefits are essential for the Lake District's viability, but they must be managed sustainably to prevent environmental and social damage (1 mark). The key is integrated management that maximises benefits while minimising problems - through initiatives like off-peak visiting, car-free tourism, affordable housing policies and conservation partnerships (1 mark).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on glacial tourism and management from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Use the Lake District as your main case study - know specific facts (18 million visitors, £1.4 billion, 42,000 residents) | Always consider BOTH sides of tourism (benefits and problems) | Know specific management strategies: Fix the Fells, park and ride, Environmental Stewardship, Sandford Principle | For conflict questions, identify specific groups and explain WHY they disagree | Link management strategies to the conflicts they address | Remember the National Park's dual purpose: conservation AND enjoyment
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often write vague answers without specific geographical evidence. Wrong: Writing generalised statements like 'it causes problems' Correct: Using specific data and named examples, e.g. 'the 2010 Haiti earthquake killed over 200,000 people due to poor building quality' Students often confuse causes and effects. Wrong: Mixing up what caused the event with what resulted from it Correct: Clearly separate causes (why it happened) from effects (what happened as a result) Students often describe rather than evaluate. Wrong: Listing strategies without assessing their effectiveness Correct: Weighing up strengths and weaknesses of each approach and reaching a supported judgement
