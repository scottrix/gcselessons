Lesson 2: Core Concepts: measuring development

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on measuring development. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Definition: Development is the process of improvement in a country's economic and social conditions. It involves changes in wealth, health, education, and standard of living.
Definition: GNI per head is the total value of goods and services produced by a country in a year, plus income from abroad, divided by the population. It is measured in US dollars.
Key Link: Social and economic indicators are closely connected. Higher GNI means more money for healthcare and education, which leads to lower infant mortality, higher life expectancy, and better literacy rates.
Definition: The HDI is a composite indicator created by the United Nations that combines three measures of development: GNI per head (PPP), life expectancy at birth, and mean years of schooling.
Definition: The DTM describes how population changes as a country develops economically. It shows the relationship between birth rate, death rate, and total population over five stages.
Exam Tip: Always evaluate indicators - no single measure gives a complete picture. A country may have high GNI but low life expectancy (e.g. some oil-rich Gulf states with migrant worker populations), or low GNI but good social development (e.g. Sri Lanka, Kerala state in India).

Lesson 3: Application: measuring development

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Link, Exam Tip, Key Point. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Define the term 'GNI per head'.

Answer: GNI per head is the total value of goods and services produced by a country plus net income from abroad, divided by the total population, measured in US dollars.

Q2: Describe two limitations of using GNI per head as a measure of development.

Answer: First, GNI per head hides inequality within a country - wealth may be concentrated among a small elite while the majority live in poverty. Second, it does not account for the informal economy (e.g. subsistence farming or street trading) which is significant in many LICs and NEEs.

Q3: Explain why the HDI is considered a better measure of development than GNI alone.

Answer: The HDI is a composite measure that combines economic (GNI per head), health (life expectancy), and education (mean years of schooling) indicators. This gives a more complete picture of development than GNI alone, which only measures economic wealth. For example, Equatorial Guinea has relatively high GNI from oil but low HDI because wealth doesn't translate into better health or education for most people.

Q4: Outline the key characteristics of Stage 3 of the Demographic Transition Model.

Answer: In Stage 3, birth rates begin to fall while death rates remain low. This is due to increased access to contraception, better education (especially for women), urbanisation, and lower infant mortality. The population continues to grow but at a slowing rate.

Q5: Suggest reasons why death rates fall before birth rates as a country develops.

Answer: Death rates fall first because improvements in healthcare, sanitation, and food supply have an immediate impact on mortality. Birth rates remain high initially due to cultural norms, lack of contraception access, and because high infant mortality encourages families to have more children. It takes time for social attitudes and access to family planning to change.

Q6: Compare the development indicators you would expect to find in an LIC and a HIC.

Answer: In an LIC you would expect: high birth rate (30-45), high infant mortality (40-80 per 1,000), low life expectancy (50-60 years), low literacy rate (40-70%), and low GNI per head (below $1,045). In a HIC you would expect: low birth rate (8-12), low infant mortality (2-5 per 1,000), high life expectancy (78-84 years), high literacy rate (99%+), and high GNI per head (above $12,746).

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: measuring development

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on measuring development from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Always name specific indicators when discussing development - don't just say "it's more developed" | Use data to support your answers (approximate figures for GNI, life expectancy, etc.) | Remember that no single indicator gives a full picture - always evaluate limitations | When describing the DTM, always refer to specific stages and explain the reasons for changing birth and death rates | Use the terms LIC, NEE, and HIC correctly in your answers | If asked to compare countries, use specific named examples with data | Common error: confusing GDP and GNI - GDP is produced within borders, GNI includes income from abroad
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often write vague answers without specific geographical evidence. Wrong: Writing generalised statements like 'it causes problems' Correct: Using specific data and named examples, e.g. 'the 2010 Haiti earthquake killed over 200,000 people due to poor building quality' Students often confuse causes and effects. Wrong: Mixing up what caused the event with what resulted from it Correct: Clearly separate causes (why it happened) from effects (what happened as a result) Students often describe rather than evaluate. Wrong: Listing strategies without assessing their effectiveness Correct: Weighing up strengths and weaknesses of each approach and reaching a supported judgement
