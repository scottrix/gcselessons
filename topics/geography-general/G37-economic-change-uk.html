Lesson 2: Core Concepts: economic change in the uk

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on economic change in the uk. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Definition: De-industrialisation is the decline in the importance of the secondary sector (manufacturing) in a country's economy, measured by output or employment.
Definition: A post-industrial economy is one where the service sector (tertiary) and knowledge-based industries (quaternary) dominate, having replaced manufacturing as the main source of employment and GDP.
Key Concept: Science parks are located near universities and focus on research and high-tech industries. Business parks are developments offering modern office space for service-sector companies. Both are typically located on the edge of cities where land is cheaper and transport links are good.
Key Concept: The north-south divide refers to the perceived economic and cultural differences between southern England (wealthier, service-based, London-centric) and northern England (historically industrial, lower average incomes, higher unemployment).
Post-Brexit Challenges: Leaving the EU single market has created trade friction (customs checks, regulatory divergence), labour shortages (especially in agriculture, hospitality, and logistics), and reduced EU research funding. However, the UK has signed trade deals with Australia, Japan, and CPTPP (Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership). The long-term economic impact remains debated.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Explain the causes of de-industrialisation in the UK.

Answer: De-industrialisation in the UK was caused by: globalisation (cheaper labour abroad made UK manufacturing uncompetitive); mechanisation (technology replaced human workers); government policy (privatisation and closure of unprofitable industries like coal under Thatcher); rising service sector wages attracting workers from manufacturing; and foreign competition (countries like China producing goods more cheaply). Manufacturing employment fell from 8.5 million in 1970 to 2.7 million in 2020.

Lesson 3: Application: economic change in the uk

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Concept, Key Concept, Post-Brexit Challenges. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain the causes of de-industrialisation in the UK.

Answer: De-industrialisation in the UK was caused by: globalisation (cheaper labour abroad made UK manufacturing uncompetitive); mechanisation (technology replaced human workers); government policy (privatisation and closure of unprofitable industries like coal under Thatcher); rising service sector wages attracting workers from manufacturing; and foreign competition (countries like China producing goods more cheaply). Manufacturing employment fell from 8.5 million in 1970 to 2.7 million in 2020.

Q2: Describe the characteristics of a post-industrial economy using the UK as an example.

Answer: A post-industrial economy is dominated by the tertiary (services, 69% of GDP) and quaternary (knowledge-based, 20% of GDP) sectors. In the UK, financial services (London as a global hub), technology (valued at $1 trillion), and creative industries (£115 billion) have replaced manufacturing. Science parks like Cambridge Science Park drive innovation in biotechnology and AI. Manufacturing now accounts for only 10% of GDP and 8% of employment.

Q3: What is the north-south divide? Describe two ways the government has tried to reduce it.

Answer: The north-south divide is the economic disparity between southern England (wealthier, service-based, higher wages, more investment) and northern England (lower average incomes, higher unemployment, legacy of de-industrialisation). Government strategies: the Northern Powerhouse aimed to boost northern cities through devolution and transport investment; the Levelling Up Fund allocated £4.8 billion to deprived areas for local infrastructure projects.

Q4: Explain the advantages and disadvantages of science parks for local economies.

Answer: Advantages: Science parks create high-skilled, high-wage jobs; attract investment to an area; benefit from proximity to universities for research and talent; raise the area's profile as a centre of innovation. Cambridge Science Park employs 7,000+ people in high-tech industries. Disadvantages: Jobs require high qualifications, excluding local people without degrees; may widen inequality within the area; land and property prices rise, displacing existing residents; economic benefits may not spread to surrounding deprived communities.

Q5: "De-industrialisation has had only negative impacts on the UK." How far do you agree?

Answer: While de-industrialisation caused significant job losses and community decline (e.g. coal mining communities losing their primary employer), it also brought positive changes. The environment improved as polluting factories closed; the service and knowledge sectors offer higher wages and cleaner working conditions; science parks have driven innovation; and flexible working patterns have increased. However, the benefits have been uneven - the south gained service jobs while the north suffered disproportionately. The overall impact depends on location, skills, and the ability of communities to adapt.

Q6: Assess the UK's role in the global economy after Brexit.

Answer: After Brexit, the UK faces challenges including trade friction with the EU (its largest trading partner), labour shortages in key sectors, and reduced research funding. However, the UK remains the 5th largest economy, is the 4th largest recipient of FDI, and has signed new trade deals (CPTPP, Australia, Japan). London remains a global financial centre, and the tech sector continues to grow. The long-term impact depends on how the UK adapts its trade relationships, attracts skilled workers, and invests in innovation. The picture is mixed - some sectors have struggled while others have adapted successfully.
