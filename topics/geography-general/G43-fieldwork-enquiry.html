Lesson 2: Core Concepts: fieldwork: enquiry process

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on fieldwork: enquiry process. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Concept: Geographical enquiry is a structured investigation into a geographical question or issue. It follows a clear sequence: Question → Hypothesis → Data Collection → Data Presentation → Analysis → Conclusion → Evaluation. AQA requires two fieldwork investigations: one in a physical environment and one in a human environment.
Definition: A research question is an open-ended question that guides your enquiry. It should be specific, measurable, and linked to geographical theory.
Definition: A hypothesis is a testable statement that predicts the expected outcome of the enquiry. It is often based on geographical theory and can be supported or rejected by evidence.
Key Concept: You must choose data collection methods that are appropriate for your hypothesis. The evidence should be sufficient, relevant, and reliable. Consider both primary data (collected by you) and secondary data (collected by others).
Key Factors: A good fieldwork location must be accessible, safe, relevant to the hypothesis, and representative of the geographical process being studied.
Definition: A risk assessment identifies potential hazards in the fieldwork environment, assesses the likelihood and severity of harm, and describes measures to reduce or eliminate risks.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Explain the difference between a research question and a hypothesis.

Answer: A research question is an open-ended question that guides the enquiry (e.g. "How does environmental quality vary across the city?"). A hypothesis is a testable statement that predicts the expected outcome (e.g. "Environmental quality decreases with distance from the CBD"). A hypothesis is more specific and directional, allowing you to clearly support or reject it with evidence, whereas a research question invites broader exploration.

Lesson 3: Application: fieldwork: enquiry process

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Concept, Key Concept, Key Factors. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain the difference between a research question and a hypothesis.

Answer: A research question is an open-ended question that guides the enquiry (e.g. "How does environmental quality vary across the city?"). A hypothesis is a testable statement that predicts the expected outcome (e.g. "Environmental quality decreases with distance from the CBD"). A hypothesis is more specific and directional, allowing you to clearly support or reject it with evidence, whereas a research question invites broader exploration.

Q2: Write a suitable hypothesis for a study investigating how river characteristics change downstream.

Answer: "River velocity, width, and depth all increase with distance downstream from the source" - based on the Bradshaw Model which predicts these changes as the river is joined by more tributaries and the channel becomes more efficient through erosion and deposition processes.

Q3: Describe three factors you would consider when choosing a fieldwork location.

Answer: Factor 1: Accessibility - the site must be reachable safely by the group, with road or public transport access and manageable terrain. Factor 2: Relevance - the site must show the geographical process being studied (e.g. a river must have enough variation downstream to test the hypothesis). Factor 3: Safety - the site must be free from unacceptable hazards, such as fast-flowing water or unstable cliffs.

Q4: Identify two hazards of river fieldwork and explain how you would minimise the risk from each.

Answer: Hazard 1: Deep water / drowning - minimise by never entering water above knee height, wearing buoyancy aids, working in pairs, and avoiding sites near weirs or waterfalls. Hazard 2: Slippery river banks - minimise by wearing appropriate footwear with good grip, avoiding steep or muddy banks, and using a walking pole for stability.

Q5: Explain why it is important to use both primary and secondary data in a geographical enquiry.

Answer: Primary data is collected firsthand by the researcher, ensuring it is specific to the study location and up-to-date. Secondary data provides wider context, historical comparison, and larger datasets that would be impossible to collect personally (e.g. census data, Environment Agency records). Using both allows triangulation - checking that findings from one source are supported by another, increasing the reliability and validity of conclusions.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: fieldwork: enquiry process

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on fieldwork: enquiry process from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: You must know your own fieldwork in detail - the examiner will ask about YOUR specific investigation | Be able to state your hypothesis clearly and explain the geographical theory behind it | Know exactly where your fieldwork took place (named locations, grid references) | Be prepared to explain and justify your data collection methods | Risk assessment is a common exam question - know specific hazards and mitigations for your environment | Remember you need TWO fieldwork investigations: one physical, one human | Practice explaining why each method was chosen - link it to your hypothesis
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often write vague answers without specific geographical evidence. Wrong: Writing generalised statements like 'it causes problems' Correct: Using specific data and named examples, e.g. 'the 2010 Haiti earthquake killed over 200,000 people due to poor building quality' Students often confuse causes and effects. Wrong: Mixing up what caused the event with what resulted from it Correct: Clearly separate causes (why it happened) from effects (what happened as a result) Students often describe rather than evaluate. Wrong: Listing strategies without assessing their effectiveness Correct: Weighing up strengths and weaknesses of each approach and reaching a supported judgement
