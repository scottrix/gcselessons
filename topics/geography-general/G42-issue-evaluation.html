Lesson 2: Core Concepts: issue evaluation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on issue evaluation. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Concept: The Issue Evaluation is Section A of AQA GCSE Geography Paper 3 (Geographical Applications). It tests your ability to use geographical skills, critical thinking, and knowledge to analyse a contemporary geographical issue. You receive pre-release resources 12 weeks before the exam.
Step-by-Step Approach: Read all resources carefully at least twice Annotate key information, patterns, and links between resources Identify the geographical issue and its key components Locate the issue spatially - use maps to understand place context Connect the resources to your wider geographical knowledge Evaluate the reliability and bias of each source
Definition: Stakeholders are individuals, groups, or organisations that have an interest in or are affected by a geographical issue. Different stakeholders often have conflicting views because they have different priorities, values, and interests.
Exam Technique: When asked about stakeholder viewpoints: (1) Identify who the stakeholders are; (2) Explain their specific interest/concern; (3) Use evidence from the resource booklet; (4) Show how and why viewpoints conflict; (5) Consider which viewpoints are better supported by evidence.
Key Skill: The final question in the Issue Evaluation section (typically worth 9 marks + 3 SPaG) asks you to make and justify a decision. You must present a clear conclusion supported by evidence and reasoning, considering alternative viewpoints.
Geography Exam Tips — Issue Evaluation: 1. For Issue Evaluation questions, always name specific case studies with factual detail 2. Use geographical terminology precisely (e.g. specific processes, not vague descriptions) 3. Consider social, economic and environmental perspectives in your evaluations 4. Support your points about Issue Evaluation with data, statistics or named examples 5. For 'assess' or 'evaluate' questions, reach a clear judgement supported by evidence

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Why is it important to consider the reliability of sources in the pre-release booklet?

Answer: Source reliability is important because different sources may be biased or have limited perspective. A newspaper article may use emotive language or omit facts; a developer's report may emphasise benefits and downplay costs; a resident's view may be based on personal impact rather than wider evidence. Evaluating reliability helps you assess how much weight to give each source and identify which are most trustworthy, leading to better-informed conclusions.

Lesson 3: Application: issue evaluation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Concept, Step-by-Step Approach, Exam Technique. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Why is it important to consider the reliability of sources in the pre-release booklet?

Answer: Source reliability is important because different sources may be biased or have limited perspective. A newspaper article may use emotive language or omit facts; a developer's report may emphasise benefits and downplay costs; a resident's view may be based on personal impact rather than wider evidence. Evaluating reliability helps you assess how much weight to give each source and identify which are most trustworthy, leading to better-informed conclusions.

Q2: Explain why stakeholders often have conflicting viewpoints about geographical issues.

Answer: Stakeholders have conflicting viewpoints because they have different priorities and interests. Local residents may prioritise quality of life and property values, while developers prioritise profit and government prioritises policy targets. Environmental groups value sustainability, while businesses value economic growth. These different values lead to different conclusions about what should happen. For example, a wind farm developer sees economic opportunity; a nearby resident sees visual intrusion; an environmentalist sees climate action.

Q3: Describe the PEEL structure and explain how it helps you answer evaluation questions.

Answer: PEEL stands for Point, Evidence, Explain, Link. You start by stating your argument (Point), then support it with data from the resource booklet (Evidence), then show how the evidence supports your argument (Explain), and finally connect back to your overall decision (Link). This structure ensures every point is properly supported and explained, which is essential for accessing the higher mark bands where reasoning must be clear and evidence-based.

Q4: What six factors should you consider when evaluating alternative solutions to a geographical problem?

Answer: The six factors are: (1) Effectiveness - how well does it solve the problem? (2) Cost - what are the financial costs and benefits? (3) Sustainability - is it environmentally sustainable long-term? (4) Social impact - who benefits and who loses? (5) Feasibility - is it practical and achievable? (6) Timescale - how quickly can it be implemented?

Q5: Explain how you would approach the final 9-mark justification question.

Answer: For the 9-mark justification question: (1) State your decision clearly at the beginning; (2) Present your strongest evidence from the resource booklet; (3) Link to geographical knowledge beyond the booklet; (4) Acknowledge counter-arguments and explain why your view is stronger; (5) Consider multiple perspectives and explain why you prioritise some; (6) Conclude firmly. Use PEEL paragraphs to structure each point. Remember that 3 additional SPaG marks are available for spelling, punctuation, grammar, and use of geographical terminology.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: issue evaluation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on issue evaluation from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Study the pre-release booklet thoroughly - annotate it, make notes, discuss it in class | You cannot bring your annotated booklet into the exam - a clean copy is provided | Always refer to specific figures and sources from the booklet (e.g. "Figure 3 shows...") | For the 9-mark question, you MUST take a position - don't be indecisive | Use geographical terminology correctly (sustainability, stakeholder, mitigation, trade-off) | Consider short-term AND long-term impacts in your evaluations | Remember SPaG marks (3 marks) - write in full sentences, use paragraphs, spell correctly | Practice timing: spend about 60 minutes on Section A
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often write vague answers without specific geographical evidence. Wrong: Writing generalised statements like 'it causes problems' Correct: Using specific data and named examples, e.g. 'the 2010 Haiti earthquake killed over 200,000 people due to poor building quality' Students often confuse causes and effects. Wrong: Mixing up what caused the event with what resulted from it Correct: Clearly separate causes (why it happened) from effects (what happened as a result) Students often describe rather than evaluate. Wrong: Listing strategies without assessing their effectiveness Correct: Weighing up strengths and weaknesses of each approach and reaching a supported judgement
