Key Facts: Lagos is a megacity in Nigeria, West Africa. It is built on the coast of the Gulf of Guinea and on islands in Lagos Lagoon. With a population of approximately 15 million (metropolitan area), it is the largest city in sub-Saharan Africa and Nigeria's economic capital. Lagos grows by an estimated 77 people per hour.
Key Fact: Lagos was a small town of approximately 300,000 in 1950. By 2025, the metropolitan population is approximately 15 million - a 50-fold increase in 75 years. Projections suggest it could reach 25 million by 2035.
Exam Tip: When assessing urban planning, always evaluate whether strategies benefit ALL residents or just the wealthy. Eko Atlantic is often criticised for ignoring the needs of the 60% who live in slums. The best answers show understanding of inequality in urban planning.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Explain why Lagos has grown so rapidly. (4 marks)

Answer: Lagos has grown rapidly due to rural-urban migration driven by push factors (poverty, Boko Haram insurgency, desertification in rural Nigeria) and pull factors (employment in oil and manufacturing, higher incomes, education and healthcare) (1 mark). Natural increase also contributes significantly - the young migrant population has high birth rates (36 per 1,000) and improved healthcare reduces death rates (1 mark). Lagos's economic importance (generating 25% of Nigeria's GDP) creates a cycle of growth as more jobs attract more migrants (1 mark). Net migration adds 500,000 people annually, while natural increase adds a similar number, giving a total growth rate of over 3% per year (1 mark).

Lesson 3: Application: urban growth in lics and nees

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain why Lagos has grown so rapidly. (4 marks)

Answer: Lagos has grown rapidly due to rural-urban migration driven by push factors (poverty, Boko Haram insurgency, desertification in rural Nigeria) and pull factors (employment in oil and manufacturing, higher incomes, education and healthcare) (1 mark). Natural increase also contributes significantly - the young migrant population has high birth rates (36 per 1,000) and improved healthcare reduces death rates (1 mark). Lagos's economic importance (generating 25% of Nigeria's GDP) creates a cycle of growth as more jobs attract more migrants (1 mark). Net migration adds 500,000 people annually, while natural increase adds a similar number, giving a total growth rate of over 3% per year (1 mark).

Q2: Describe two challenges of urban growth in a named LIC or NEE city. (4 marks)

Answer: In Lagos, water and sanitation is a major challenge - only 10% of residents have piped water and 60% lack adequate sanitation (1 mark). This leads to waterborne diseases like cholera, especially in slum areas like Makoko where open defecation contaminates water sources (1 mark). Traffic congestion is another challenge - over 5 million cars on limited roads create commutes of over 2 hours (1 mark). This costs the economy $8.5 billion per year and causes air pollution, stress and lost productivity (1 mark).

Q3: Using a named example, assess the opportunities and challenges of urban growth in LICs and NEEs. (6 marks)

Answer: Lagos offers significant opportunities - it generates 25% of Nigeria's GDP, providing employment in oil, manufacturing, tech and services (1 mark). The informal economy supports 65% of workers through street trading and transport services (1 mark). However, challenges are severe - 60% live in slums with overcrowding and poor sanitation (1 mark). Traffic congestion costs $8.5 billion per year, and unemployment is estimated at 20-30% (1 mark). Urban planning attempts like the BRT system and Eko Atlantic address some issues but often benefit wealthier residents more than the poor majority (1 mark). The opportunities of economic growth and social mobility must be weighed against the reality of slum conditions and inadequate services for most residents (1 mark).

Q4: "Urban planning in LICs and NEEs benefits the wealthy more than the poor." To what extent do you agree? Use a named example. (9 marks)

Answer: I agree that urban planning often benefits the wealthy more than the poor. Eko Atlantic is a prime example - a new city built on reclaimed land designed for 250,000-500,000 residents with modern infrastructure, reliable electricity and clean water (1 mark). However, the cost of living there excludes the 60% of Lagos residents who live in informal settlements, making it effectively a "city for the rich" (1 mark). When slums are demolished for development, poor residents are displaced without adequate alternatives (1 mark). The BRT transport system primarily benefits those commuting along major corridors, while slum residents in remote areas still rely on expensive informal transport (1 mark). However, some planning does benefit the poor - community toilet blocks in slums improve sanitation, and LAWMA's waste collection reduces disease (1 mark). The Lagos Light Rail could eventually improve access for lower-income commuters if fares are affordable (1 mark). The fundamental problem is that urban planning in LICs and NEEs is often underfunded and unable to keep pace with rapid population growth (1 mark). When resources are limited, they tend to flow to projects that generate revenue or attract investment, rather than to basic services for the poorest (1 mark). In conclusion, while some planning benefits reach the poor, the overall pattern in Lagos shows that wealthier residents gain disproportionately from urban development, perpetuating inequality (1 mark).

Exam Tips: Use Lagos as your named LIC/NEE case study - know specific facts and statistics | Balance opportunities and challenges in your answers | Always evaluate urban planning - who benefits and who is left out? | Know the difference between formal and informal sector employment | Link challenges to specific statistics (60% in slums, 10% with piped water, $8.5B congestion cost) | For evaluation, discuss whether planning is sustainable and equitable
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often write vague answers without specific geographical evidence. Wrong: Writing generalised statements like 'it causes problems' Correct: Using specific data and named examples, e.g. 'the 2010 Haiti earthquake killed over 200,000 people due to poor building quality' Students often confuse causes and effects. Wrong: Mixing up what caused the event with what resulted from it Correct: Clearly separate causes (why it happened) from effects (what happened as a result) Students often describe rather than evaluate. Wrong: Listing strategies without assessing their effectiveness Correct: Weighing up strengths and weaknesses of each approach and reaching a supported judgement
