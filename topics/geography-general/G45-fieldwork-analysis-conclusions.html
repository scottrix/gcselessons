Lesson 2: Core Concepts: fieldwork: analysis, conclusions and evaluation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on fieldwork: analysis, conclusions and evaluation. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Concept: Data analysis means identifying patterns, trends, and relationships in your data. It involves describing what the data shows, calculating statistics, and explaining the geographical reasons behind the patterns you find.
Definition: The IQR measures the spread of the middle 50% of data, eliminating the influence of outliers. It is calculated as Q3 − Q1, where Q1 is the 25th percentile and Q3 is the 75th percentile.
Definition: Spearman's Rank measures the strength and direction of the relationship between two variables. It produces a value between -1 and +1. This is the most important statistical test for GCSE Geography fieldwork.
Key Concept: To determine if your Spearman's Rank result is statistically significant (not just due to chance), compare your calculated rs value to the critical value table at the 95% confidence level (p = 0.05). If your calculated value exceeds the critical value, the result is significant and you can reject the null hypothesis.
Key Skill: A conclusion directly answers the original question or hypothesis, using evidence from your data and analysis. It should state whether the hypothesis is supported or rejected, and explain why with reference to geographical theory.
Reliability: Can the results be replicated? If you repeated the investigation, would you get similar results? Validity: Do the results actually measure what you intended to measure? Do they accurately answer the research question?

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Explain the difference between the mean and the median, and when each might be more appropriate.

Answer: The mean is the sum of all values divided by the number of values; the median is the middle value when data is arranged in order. The mean is more appropriate when data has no extreme outliers and you want to use all data values (e.g. average river velocity at a site). The median is more appropriate when there are outliers that would distort the mean (e.g. environmental quality scores where one extremely high or low score would pull the mean but not affect the median). In the example with scores including a 45 outlier, the median (20) better represents the typical score than the mean (22).

Lesson 3: Application: fieldwork: analysis, conclusions and evaluation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Concept, Key Concept, Key Skill. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain the difference between the mean and the median, and when each might be more appropriate.

Answer: The mean is the sum of all values divided by the number of values; the median is the middle value when data is arranged in order. The mean is more appropriate when data has no extreme outliers and you want to use all data values (e.g. average river velocity at a site). The median is more appropriate when there are outliers that would distort the mean (e.g. environmental quality scores where one extremely high or low score would pull the mean but not affect the median). In the example with scores including a 45 outlier, the median (20) better represents the typical score than the mean (22).

Q2: A Spearman's Rank calculation gives a result of +0.82 with 10 pairs of data. The critical value at p=0.05 for n=10 is 0.65. Explain what this result means.

Answer: The result of +0.82 indicates a strong positive correlation between the two variables. Since 0.82 exceeds the critical value of 0.65 at the 95% confidence level (p=0.05), the result is statistically significant. This means there is less than a 5% probability that the correlation occurred by chance. Therefore, you can reject the null hypothesis and conclude that there is a genuine positive relationship between the two variables.

Q3: Describe three factors that could affect the reliability of fieldwork data.

Answer: Factor 1: Sample size - too few sites makes results unrepresentative and more affected by anomalies. Collecting data at only 3 river sites, for example, would not reliably show downstream trends. Factor 2: Measurement error - human errors in reading instruments (e.g. misreading a flow meter) or recording data (e.g. writing the wrong number) reduce reliability. This can be improved by taking repeat readings. Factor 3: Timing - results vary with conditions; river discharge after heavy rain would be much higher than during a dry period, making comparisons unreliable.

Q4: Explain how you would write a conclusion for a geographical enquiry.

Answer: A conclusion should: (1) State whether the hypothesis is supported or rejected; (2) Summarise the key evidence using specific data values; (3) Link findings to geographical theory (e.g. the Bradshaw Model); (4) Explain any anomalies that didn't fit the pattern; (5) Acknowledge limitations that reduce certainty. The conclusion must directly answer the original question and be fully supported by evidence from the data and analysis.

Q5: What is the difference between reliability and validity in fieldwork? Why are both important?

Answer: Reliability means the results can be replicated - if you repeated the investigation under the same conditions, you would get similar results. It depends on consistent methods, adequate sample size, and accurate measurements. Validity means the results actually measure what was intended and genuinely answer the research question. Both are important because: unreliable results cannot be trusted (they may be random); invalid results may be reliable but measure the wrong thing (e.g. a well-conducted study that measures footfall instead of environmental quality). An investigation must be both reliable AND valid to produce meaningful conclusions.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: fieldwork: analysis, conclusions and evaluation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on fieldwork: analysis, conclusions and evaluation from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Know how to calculate and interpret Spearman's Rank - it appears frequently on exams | Always compare your calculated rs to the critical value to test significance | When describing data, quote specific figures - don't just say "it increased" | In conclusions, explicitly state "the hypothesis is supported/rejected" | Evaluation questions are common - prepare by identifying specific limitations of your methods | Higher mark questions: suggest specific improvements and explain how they would increase reliability or validity | Remember: correlation does not equal causation | Use statistical language: significant, correlation, anomaly, confidence level
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often write vague answers without specific geographical evidence. Wrong: Writing generalised statements like 'it causes problems' Correct: Using specific data and named examples, e.g. 'the 2010 Haiti earthquake killed over 200,000 people due to poor building quality' Students often confuse causes and effects. Wrong: Mixing up what caused the event with what resulted from it Correct: Clearly separate causes (why it happened) from effects (what happened as a result) Students often describe rather than evaluate. Wrong: Listing strategies without assessing their effectiveness Correct: Weighing up strengths and weaknesses of each approach and reaching a supported judgement
