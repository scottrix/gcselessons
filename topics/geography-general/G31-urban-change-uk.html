Lesson 3: Application: urban change in the uk

Q1: Explain how international migration has affected a named UK city. (4 marks)

Answer: In Bristol, international migration has brought cultural diversity - approximately 16% of the population was born outside the UK, with significant Somali, Polish and South Asian communities (1 mark). Migrants fill labour shortages in sectors like healthcare and construction, and bring entrepreneurial skills, establishing businesses particularly in areas like Easton and St Paul's (1 mark). Polish migrants have contributed to the construction and service sectors, while the Somali community has established cultural institutions (1 mark). However, migration has also created some community tensions and placed pressure on housing and services in areas of concentration (1 mark).

Q2: Describe two challenges of urban change in a named UK city. (4 marks)

Answer: In Bristol, housing affordability is a major challenge - average house prices of £350,000 are over 11 times the median salary, creating a severe shortage of affordable homes and over 16,000 on the housing waiting list (1 mark). This leads to homelessness, with over 200 people sleeping rough, and gentrification displacing lower-income residents (1 mark). Traffic congestion is another challenge - Bristol has the worst congestion outside London, costing £300 million per year and causing air pollution that exceeds WHO limits (1 mark). A Clean Air Zone was introduced in 2022 to address this, but transport remains a significant problem affecting the economy and quality of life (1 mark).

Q3: Using a named UK city, assess the impact of urban regeneration. (6 marks)

Answer: Bristol's Harbourside regeneration transformed the former docklands (closed as a port in the 1970s) into a cultural and leisure destination with museums, apartments, restaurants and public spaces (1 mark). The Temple Quarter Enterprise Zone is creating 17,000 new jobs focused on creative and digital industries, plus 10,000 new homes (1 mark). South Bristol regeneration has brought a new community hospital, leisure centre and improved transport to the city's most deprived areas (1 mark). However, regeneration has been criticised for providing insufficient affordable housing and potentially benefiting wealthier newcomers more than existing deprived communities (1 mark). The 10-year life expectancy gap between Clifton and Lawrence Hill shows that regeneration has not yet addressed deep-rooted inequality (1 mark). While regeneration has brought significant investment and improved amenities, its benefits are unevenly distributed across the city (1 mark).

Q4: "Urban regeneration benefits some groups more than others." To what extent do you agree? Use a named UK city. (9 marks)

Answer: I agree that urban regeneration benefits some groups more than others. In Bristol, the Harbourside redevelopment created attractive apartments, restaurants and cultural venues (1 mark), but the new housing was predominantly expensive, excluding lower-income residents and effectively creating a wealthy enclave alongside deprived areas (1 mark). The Temple Quarter Enterprise Zone promises 17,000 jobs and 10,000 homes, but without strong affordable housing targets, most homes will be unaffordable for residents of nearby Lawrence Hill - England's most deprived ward (1 mark). Gentrification in areas like Stokes Croft has increased property values, benefiting homeowners and landlords but displacing private renters who can no longer afford the area (1 mark). However, some regeneration has targeted deprived areas more directly - South Bristol's new community hospital, leisure centre and MetroBus routes provide services that benefit existing lower-income residents (1 mark). The University of Bristol's Temple Quarter campus could bring educational opportunities to the area (1 mark). The issue is that market-led regeneration naturally favours those with purchasing power, while public investment in deprived areas is often insufficient to close inequality gaps (1 mark). A more equitable approach would require stronger affordable housing requirements, community benefit agreements and targeted employment programmes for existing residents (1 mark). In conclusion, while regeneration brings overall economic benefits, its distribution is heavily skewed toward wealthier groups unless deliberate policies ensure inclusive growth (1 mark).

Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often write vague answers without specific geographical evidence. Wrong: Writing generalised statements like 'it causes problems' Correct: Using specific data and named examples, e.g. 'the 2010 Haiti earthquake killed over 200,000 people due to poor building quality' Students often confuse causes and effects. Wrong: Mixing up what caused the event with what resulted from it Correct: Clearly separate causes (why it happened) from effects (what happened as a result) Students often describe rather than evaluate. Wrong: Listing strategies without assessing their effectiveness Correct: Weighing up strengths and weaknesses of each approach and reaching a supported judgement
