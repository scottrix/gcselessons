Lesson 2: Core Concepts: myths of the underworld

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on myths of the underworld. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: The Greek Underworld (Hades) was the realm of the dead, ruled by the god Hades and his queen Persephone
Key Fact: All souls went to the Underworld — it was not simply a place of reward or punishment
Key Fact: Charon the ferryman transported souls across the River Styx — the dead were buried with a coin for payment
Key Fact: The Underworld had distinct regions: Asphodel Meadows for ordinary souls, Elysium for heroes, and Tartarus for the wicked
Key Fact: The three judges of the dead (Minos, Rhadamanthus and Aeacus) decided where souls would go
Key Fact: Persephone's abduction by Hades explained the seasons — she spent part of the year in the Underworld

Extended question: Full-Mark Response How far do Greek myths of the Underworld reveal a pessimistic view of death? (15 marks) <div class="

Greek Underworld myths reveal a fundamentally pessimistic view of death, though they also contain elements of hope. The earliest and most influential depiction — Homer's Odyssey Book 11 — is deeply bleak. Achilles tells Odysseus he would rather be a landless peasant on Earth than king of the dead, declaring that death strips all meaning from existence. The shades in Homer are witless, feeble remnants of their living selves. The Asphodel Meadows, where most ordinary souls dwelt, was a place of neutral nothingness — neither reward nor punishment, merely a joyless continuation. This reflects a view that death is the end of everything worthwhile about life. However, there were more hopeful traditions. Elysium offered a paradise for heroes and the favoured, suggesting that exceptional deeds or divine favour could be rewarded. The Eleusinian Mysteries promised initiates a blessed afterlife, indicating that Greek religion developed more optimistic beliefs over time. The myth of Persephone also introduces hope: she returns from the Underworld each year, symbolising the cycle of death and rebirth.
