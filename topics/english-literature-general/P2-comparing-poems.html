Lesson 2: Core Concepts: comparing poems

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on comparing poems. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Point: Don't just write about one poem then the other. Weave them together - compare as you go.
Why Alternating Works: Examiners can see you're comparing throughout. It's easier to track similarities and differences. You cover both poems equally.
Top Tip: Comparison questions are typically worth 30 marks (AO1/AO2/AO3). Spend 45 minutes total: 5 mins planning your comparison points, 35 mins writing, 5 mins checking. Every paragraph must reference both poems — if you write a full paragraph on only one poem, you have already lost marks.

ThemesWhat ideas do both poems explore? How differently?
MethodsStructure, language, form - how do poets achieve effects?

NatureIs nature beautiful or threatening? What's the human relationship to nature?

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: What's the difference between alternating and integrated comparison?

Answer: Alternating: discuss one aspect, then compare both poems on it, then move to next aspect. Integrated: discuss both poems in every sentence. Alternating is clearer; integrated shows stronger comparison skills.

Lesson 3: Application: comparing poems

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Point, Why Alternating Works, Top Tip. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What's the difference between alternating and integrated comparison?

Answer: Alternating: discuss one aspect, then compare both poems on it, then move to next aspect. Integrated: discuss both poems in every sentence. Alternating is clearer; integrated shows stronger comparison skills.

Q2: Name three elements you should compare between poems.

Answer: Elements to compare: themes, methods (language/form/structure), tone, context, perspective, setting, imagery.

Q3: Why should you include both similarities and differences?

Answer: Both are required for a full comparison. Similarities show shared understanding; differences show nuanced analysis. A comparison that only lists similarities is incomplete.

Q4: Give an example of a comparative connective for similarity and one for difference.

Answer: Similarity: "Similarly", "Both", "Likewise", "In the same way". Difference: "However", "In contrast", "Whereas", "Unlike", "On the other hand".

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response 30 marks: Compare how poets present ideas about power in "Ozymandias" and "London." <div class="

Both Shelley and Blake present power as corrosive and ultimately destructive, yet they diverge in their treatment of time: Shelley shows power already collapsed, while Blake shows it actively suffocating the present. Shelley's "Ozymandias" dramatises the fall of tyrannical power through the ruined statue — "Half sunk, a shattered visage lies" — where the adjective "shattered" and participle "sunk" together enact the collapse Ozymandias sought to prevent. The ironic inscription "Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair" is hollowed by the surrounding "nothing… bare" — the very nothingness becomes the poem's final image, a desert that has outlasted all human authority. The sonnet form, traditionally used for love, is subverted to show the opposite: the death of legacy. Similarly, Blake presents power as destructive in "London," yet here power is not past but present. The repetition of "chartered" — "chartered street… chartered Thames" — suggests that even nature is owned and controlled, a claustrophobic vision of institutional power. Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often write about each poem separately, then add a comparison sentence at the end. Wrong: Writing 3 paragraphs on Poem A, then 3 paragraphs on Poem B, then "Both poems are about war." Correct: Weaving comparison throughout — each paragraph discusses both poems on one element (e.g., tone, imagery, structure). Students often state differences without explaining why they exist. Wrong: "Poem A uses nature imagery but Poem B uses urban imagery." Correct: "While Poem A draws on Romantic nature imagery to idealise the past, Poem B's urban metaphors reflect a modern, industrialised reality — a difference rooted in their contrasting historical contexts."
