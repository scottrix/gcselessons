Lesson 2: Core Concepts: modern context

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Priestley's Vision: "An Inspector Calls" argues for exactly these changes. Inspector's final speech - "we are members of one body" - advocates the social solidarity that led to the welfare state.
The Inspector as Socialist Voice: Inspector's final speech is pure socialist message. He argues that privilege brings responsibility, that we must care for each other, that ignoring the vulnerable has consequences.
"An Inspector Calls" - Genre Blend: Starts as realistic drawing-room drama, becomes expressionist. The Inspector seems supernatural. Time is distorted. These theatrical choices make the audience think beyond realism.
Context Must Be Integrated, Not Bolted On: Examiners award AO3 marks for context that is woven into your argument, not dumped in a separate paragraph. Always link historical, social, or political context directly to the writer's choices and the text's meaning. A disconnected "life in 1912" paragraph scores poorly; a sentence that explains why Priestley chose 1912 to create dramatic irony scores highly.

Lesson 3: Application: modern context

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Priestley&#x27;s Vision, The Inspector as Socialist Voice, &quot;An Inspector Calls&quot; - Genre Blend. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: How does World War II context affect "An Inspector Calls"?

Answer: WWII context means the audience has experienced catastrophe. They know what Birling doesn't - that war is coming. The play argues that pre-war attitudes (selfishness, inequality) led to disaster and must change.

Q2: Why did Priestley set his play in 1912?

Answer: Setting in 1912 creates dramatic irony. The Birlings don't know about the wars, the Titanic sinking, the Russian Revolution. Their confidence in progress and stability is shown to be wrong. Priestley critiques the past from a knowing present.

Q3: What does the Inspector represent in terms of post-war politics?

Answer: The Inspector represents post-war socialist values: collective responsibility, care for the vulnerable, equality. His final speech advocates the social solidarity that led to the welfare state. He is the voice of the new Britain Priestley wants.

Q4: How does women's changing role affect the play's meaning?

Answer: Eva Smith's exploitation shows how women were treated. Sheila's development shows the new independent woman emerging after suffrage and war work. The contrast critiques old attitudes and shows hope for change.

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response 30 marks: How does Priestley use the historical and social context of 1912 and 1945 to shape meaning in "An Inspector Calls"? <div class="

Priestley exploits the gap between 1912 and 1945 as his most powerful dramatic device. By setting the play in the complacent pre-war era but writing for a post-war audience, he transforms every confident pronouncement Birling makes into a devastating irony. When Birling dismisses the possibility of war — "there isn't a chance of war" — and declares the Titanic "unsinkable, absolutely unsinkable," the 1945 audience recognises immediately that his capitalist certainties are hollow. This dramatic irony is not merely comic; it is Priestley's central argument that the individualist, hierarchical attitudes of 1912 led directly to global catastrophe. The social context of 1912 further sharpens Priestley's critique. Eva Smith's exploitation — sacked for asking for "twenty-five shillings a week" — reflects the absence of welfare provision, employment rights, or collective bargaining. Mrs Birling's refusal of charity to a "girl of that sort" exposes how voluntary welfare was subject to class prejudice.
