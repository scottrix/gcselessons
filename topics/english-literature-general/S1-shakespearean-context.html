Lesson 2: Core Concepts: shakespearean context

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on shakespearean context. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Context: "Macbeth" was written in 1606, early in James I's reign. The play reflects James's interests: witchcraft, kingship, and the dangers of treason.
Theatre was for everyone: Shakespeare wrote for all classes - high drama for nobility, jokes and action for groundlings. Plays needed to engage diverse audiences.
Relevance: "Macbeth" explores treason, regicide, and the betrayal of a king by a trusted subject. The Gunpowder Plot was fresh in the audience's minds.
Golden Rule: Link context to specific moments in the play. Don't just dump historical facts - explain how context deepens understanding of the text.
Shakespeare Context Strategy: Be specific — say "Jacobean" not "Shakespeare's time." Know your monarchs: Elizabeth I (1558-1603) vs James I (1603-1625). For Macbeth, focus on three key context areas: the Divine Right of Kings and the Gunpowder Plot; James I's obsession with witchcraft; and Jacobean gender expectations. Always link context to specific moments in the play.

Lesson 3: Application: shakespearean context

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Key Context, Theatre was for everyone, Relevance. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What is the Divine Right of Kings and how does it affect "Macbeth"?

Answer: Divine Right of Kings meant monarchs were appointed by God. Killing a king was a sin against divine order. In "Macbeth," regicide triggers chaos in nature and leads to Macbeth's downfall, reinforcing this belief.

Q2: Why did Shakespeare include witches in "Macbeth"?

Answer: James I was obsessed with witchcraft and wrote a book about identifying witches. Including witches appealed to James, established the play's evil atmosphere, and reflected genuine Jacobean fears.

Q3: How did the Globe Theatre shape Shakespeare's writing?

Answer: The Globe had no sets or artificial lighting, so Shakespeare used language to create setting and atmosphere. The thrust stage meant audience surrounded actors, creating intimacy. All-male casts meant boys played women.

Q4: What was the Gunpowder Plot and why is it relevant?

Answer: The Gunpowder Plot (1605) was a failed Catholic attempt to blow up Parliament and kill James I. "Macbeth" (1606) explores treason and regicide by a trusted subject, resonating with audiences still shaken by the plot.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response 25 marks: "How does Shakespeare use Jacobean context to create meaning in the opening scenes of Macbeth?" <div class="

Shakespeare's opening scenes are steeped in Jacobean anxiety, exploiting the cultural and political fears of 1606 to establish the play's atmosphere of moral chaos. The witches' declaration "Fair is foul, and foul is fair" inverts moral categories entirely — for an audience conditioned by James I's Protestant theology to see witchcraft as demonic agency, these figures represent genuine evil, not theatrical spectacle. Their paradoxical language establishes a world where moral absolutes have collapsed, prefiguring Macbeth's own moral disorientation. When Duncan arrives at Macbeth's castle and praises its "pleasant seat," the dramatic irony operates on both textual and contextual levels: the audience knows Macbeth plans murder, but for a Jacobean audience who believed in the Divine Right of Kings, Duncan's trust makes the coming regicide not merely criminal but sacrilegious — a violation of God's appointed order. Lady Macbeth's invocation "unsex me here" directly contravenes Jacobean gender ideology, which insisted on feminine submission and domesticity; her rejection of femininity to embrace cruelty would have been read as further evidence of the supernatural's corrupting influence.

Exam Tips: Know the difference between Elizabethan and Jacobean periods | Link context to specific quotations | Remember James I's influence on "Macbeth" | Consider theatrical conditions (Globe, all-male cast) | Understand beliefs: divine right, witches, Great Chain of Being | Use context to explain audience reactions
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often use vague period labels. Wrong: "In Shakespeare's time, people believed in witches." Correct: "In Jacobean England, King James I's publication of 'Daemonologie' (1597) legitimised belief in witchcraft — Shakespeare's witches would have evoked genuine terror in an audience whose king actively persecuted accused witches." Students often ignore theatrical context. Wrong: "Shakespeare wrote plays for people to read." Correct: "Shakespeare wrote for the Globe Theatre — a thrust stage with no scenery and daylight performances — meaning atmosphere had to be created through language, and soliloquies were spoken directly to groundlings standing feet from the actor."
