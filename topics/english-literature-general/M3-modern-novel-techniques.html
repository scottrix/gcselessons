Lesson 3: Application: modern novel techniques

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Why Use Unreliable Narration?. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What is stream of consciousness and why do modern authors use it?

Answer: Stream of consciousness captures the continuous flow of thoughts without logical organisation. Authors use it to create psychological realism, show inner experience, and immerse readers in character's mind.

Q2: How can you tell if a narrator is unreliable?

Answer: Signs of unreliable narration: contradictions in their story; other characters disagree; events don't match description; they admit to lying or forgetting; excessive self-justification.

Q3: What is the difference between symbolism and allegory?

Answer: Symbolism: objects representing ideas (conch = order). Allegory: entire story represents abstract ideas or political situations (island = world, characters = aspects of human nature).

Q4: What effects can fragmented narrative create?

Answer: Fragmented narrative creates: mystery (reader pieces together truth); reflects trauma (memory is fragmented); challenges reader (requires active engagement); shows modern experience (life isn't neatly organised).

Extended question: Full-Mark Response 30 marks: How does Golding use narrative techniques to present the descent into savagery in "Lord of the Flies"? <div class="

Golding constructs the descent into savagery not as a sudden event but as an insidious erosion, using narrative technique to ensure the reader feels the loss of civilisation before the characters recognise it. The novel's symbolic architecture is established from the opening: the island's "scar" caused by the crashed plane foreshadows the damage the boys will inflict, while the conch's "deep, harsh note" initially promises democratic order. Golding's choice of third-person omniscient narration allows the reader to witness patterns the boys miss — we see Jack's obsession with hunting intensify across chapters while Ralph still believes "we can keep the fire going." Golding's manipulation of narrative distance is central to the descent. Early chapters use close third-person to build identification with Ralph's rational perspective, but as Jack's tribe gains power, the narrative increasingly inhabits the mob's consciousness: "the chant rose ritually" and "the circle began to twist" — the reader is drawn into the hypnotic rhythm of collective violence.
