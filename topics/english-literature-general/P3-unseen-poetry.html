Lesson 2: Core Concepts: unseen poetry

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on unseen poetry. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Good News: Unseen poetry tests skills, not knowledge. You can't revise the poems, so focus on developing analysis techniques.
Form Significance: Why did the poet choose this form? A sonnet about love connects to tradition. Free verse about trauma suggests breaking from conventions.
Top Tip: For unseen poetry, the 24-mark analysis question gets roughly 30 minutes and the 8-mark comparison gets 10-15 minutes. Always spend 5 minutes reading and annotating before writing anything — a well-planned 25-minute answer will always outperform a rushed 30-minute one.

Lesson 3: Application: unseen poetry

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Good News, Form Significance, Top Tip. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What should you do in your first reading of an unseen poem?

Answer: First reading: understand meaning - what's happening, who's speaking, what's the mood. Don't analyse yet - just comprehend the poem's basic content.

Q2: Why is it important to identify the form of the poem?

Answer: Identifying form helps you understand context and intent. A sonnet suggests love/serious topic; free verse suggests modern/conversational approach.

Q3: What's the difference between basic and developed interpretation?

Answer: Basic: identifies what (e.g., "the poem is about loss"). Developed: explains why and how (e.g., "the poem explores how loss echoes through memory").

Q4: How should you structure your response to the comparison question?

Answer: For comparison: Identify shared theme, find one similarity, find one difference, use comparative connectives. Keep it concise - only 8 marks.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Review

Review any questions answered incorrectly. Identify whether the error was knowledge, method, or reading the question.

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response 24 marks: In "The Lamplighter," how does the poet present the speaker's feelings about change? <div class="

The poet presents the speaker's feelings about change as deeply ambivalent — simultaneously yearning for progress and mourning what is lost. The opening stanza's regular ABAB rhyme scheme and iambic tetrameter establish a sense of order and familiarity, enacting the stable world the speaker inhabits before change arrives. However, as the poem progresses, the rhyme becomes increasingly unsettled — the half-rhyme of "grow" and "now" in stanza three creates a sense of dissonance, structurally mirroring the speaker's growing discomfort with transformation. The central metaphor of the lamplighter — a figure made obsolete by "electric hum" — encapsulates the speaker's anxiety: the lamplighter is not merely a worker replaced but a way of seeing the world that is vanishing. The sibilance in "soft, sure sweep of flame" creates a gentle, almost reverential tone for the old light, contrasting sharply with the "harsh click" of the new — the plosive "c" sound enacting the brutal efficiency that replaces tenderness. Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often panic and write about what the poem is "about" without analysing how it's written. Wrong: "The poem is about a woman who feels sad about her childhood." Correct: "The short, fragmented lines mirror the speaker's fractured memory, while the repeated 'was' creates a sense of distance from a lost past." Students often waste the first paragraph retelling the poem's content. Wrong: "First the speaker walks to the river, then she remembers her mother, then she feels sad." Correct: Open with your interpretation: "The poem presents memory as both sustaining and destructive, using water imagery to show how the past both nourishes and engulfs the speaker."
