Lesson 2: Core Concepts: shakespearean language

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on shakespearean language. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Why Soliloquies Matter: They give direct access to a character's psychology. In "Macbeth," soliloquies show his internal conflict, ambition, guilt, and eventual nihilism.
Track language changes: How a character speaks at the beginning vs the end shows their development (or deterioration).
Shakespeare Language Strategy: Focus on three layers: verse form (iambic pentameter, blank verse, prose), imagery patterns (blood, darkness, clothing), and rhetorical devices (repetition, antithesis, soliloquy). When the rhythm breaks, something significant is happening — always ask why. Track how a character's language changes across the play as evidence of development.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: What is iambic pentameter and why does Shakespeare use it?

Answer: Iambic pentameter is 10-syllable verse with alternating stressed/unstressed beats (da-DUM da-DUM). Shakespeare uses it for nobility and serious moments - it elevates language, creates rhythm, and signals importance. Breaking the rhythm shows emotional disturbance.

Lesson 3: Application: shakespearean language

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Why Soliloquies Matter, Track language changes, Shakespeare Language Strategy. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: What is iambic pentameter and why does Shakespeare use it?

Answer: Iambic pentameter is 10-syllable verse with alternating stressed/unstressed beats (da-DUM da-DUM). Shakespeare uses it for nobility and serious moments - it elevates language, creates rhythm, and signals importance. Breaking the rhythm shows emotional disturbance.

Q2: What's the difference between a soliloquy and an aside?

Answer: A soliloquy is a long speech with the character alone, revealing inner thoughts. An aside is a brief comment that other characters don't hear. Soliloquies = deep psychological insight; asides = quick revelations.

Q3: What does the blood imagery represent in "Macbeth"?

Answer: Blood represents guilt that cannot be washed away. Macbeth asks if "Neptune's ocean" can wash the blood, but it will "the multitudinous seas incarnadine." Lady Macbeth sleepwalks, trying to wash imaginary blood. Blood stains represent permanent moral corruption.

Q4: Why do the witches speak in rhyming couplets?

Answer: Witches speak in rhyming couplets and trochaic meter (opposite of iambic) to sound supernatural, unsettling, and different from noble characters. Their chanting quality associates them with spells and evil. This would have frightened Shakespeare's audience.

Extended question: Full-Mark Response 25 marks: "How does Shakespeare use language to present Macbeth's psychological deterioration?" <div class="

Shakespeare charts Macbeth's psychological collapse through a linguistic degeneration that moves from complex argument to nihilistic fragmentation. In Act 1, Macbeth's soliloquies demonstrate sophisticated reasoning: the extended metaphor of "vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself / And falls on th'other" reveals a mind still capable of self-analysis, the equestrian imagery showing he can identify and evaluate his own flaw. By the banquet scene, language has begun to fracture — the imperative "Thou canst not say I did it; never shake / Thy gory locks at me" combines denial with accusation in a single sentence, the semicolon splitting a mind at war with itself. The personal pronoun "I" attempts assertion, but the negation "never" reveals desperation rather than control. In the final soliloquy, all argument has collapsed: "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow" reduces existence to mechanical repetition, the triple anaphora stripping the word of meaning through overuse.
