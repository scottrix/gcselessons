P10: Density and States of Matter

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understanding the particle model of matter, the three states, and how density relates mass to volume. Measuring density of regular and irregular objects.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the arrangement, motion, and spacing of particles in solids, liquids, and gases
  • Define density and use the equation ρ = m/V
  • Measure the density of regular and irregular solids and liquids
  • Explain how density changes during state changes
  • Interpret density graphs and identify anomalies

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of atoms from KS3 science
  • Knowledge of mass, volume, and units
  • Ability to rearrange formulae

Materials & Equipment

  • Scientific calculator
  • Balance (to measure mass)
  • Ruler and Vernier callipers (for regular solids)
  • Measuring cylinder and displacement can (for irregular solids)
  • Various solids and liquids (metal blocks, stone, oil, water, alcohol)

Lesson 1: The Particle Model

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

State Sort

Show pictures of ice, water, and steam. Ask: "What is the same about all three? What is different? What do you think is happening to the particles in each state?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The particle model helps us understand the three states of matter:

Solids:
- Particles are closely packed in a regular arrangement
- Particles vibrate about fixed positions
- Strong forces of attraction between particles
- Fixed shape and volume
- Incompressible

Liquids:
- Particles are close together but in random positions
- Particles can move past each other (slide)
- Weaker forces of attraction than solids
- Fixed volume but takes the shape of its container
- Nearly incompressible

Gases:
- Particles are far apart in random positions
- Particles move quickly in all directions
- Very weak forces of attraction (negligible)
- No fixed shape or volume — fills the container
- Easily compressed

Changes of state:
Melting: solid → liquid (particles gain energy, break free from fixed positions)
Boiling/evaporation: liquid → gas (particles gain enough energy to escape completely)
Condensing: gas → liquid (particles lose energy, come closer together)
Freezing: liquid → solid (particles lose energy, become locked in position)

Sublimation: solid → gas without passing through liquid state (e.g., dry ice, iodine)
Deposition: gas → solid without passing through liquid state (e.g., frost forming)
Worked Example

A sealed container holds water at room temperature. Some water evaporates from the surface, and some steam condenses back into water. At equilibrium, the rate of evaporation equals the rate of condensation. Explain this using the particle model.

At the surface, the most energetic particles escape into the gas phase (evaporation). Meanwhile, gas particles hit the surface and lose energy, becoming liquid again (condensation). At equilibrium, both rates are equal, so the amount of liquid and gas remains constant.

Common Misconception: Students think particles get bigger when a substance melts or boils. The particles stay the same size — it is the spacing and motion that change.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a Venn diagram comparing solids, liquids, and gases. Students complete it using the particle model descriptions.
Stretch:
Explain why a solid expands when heated, using the particle model. Discuss what happens to the spacing and vibration of particles.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Particle Description

Students write a paragraph describing what happens to particles when ice melts, using key terms: arrangement, motion, spacing, forces of attraction, energy.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe particle arrangement, motion, and spacing in all three states and explain changes of state
  • Getting there: Can describe the three states but struggles with changes of state or particle motion
  • Not yet: Cannot describe the particle model accurately for any state

Lesson 2: Density

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Float or Sink?

Show a block of wood, a stone, and an aluminium block. Ask: "Which will float on water? Why?" Students predict and explain using ideas about density.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Density is the mass per unit volume of a substance. It tells us how tightly packed the particles are.

Equation: ρ = m/V
- ρ (rho) = density in kg/m³ or g/cm³
- m = mass in kg or g
- V = volume in m³ or cm³

Unit conversions:
1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³
1 kg/m³ = 0.001 g/cm³

Typical densities:
- Water: 1000 kg/m³ (1 g/cm³)
- Aluminium: 2700 kg/m³ (2.7 g/cm³)
- Iron: 7800 kg/m³ (7.8 g/cm³)
- Gold: 19 300 kg/m³ (19.3 g/cm³)
- Air: 1.2 kg/m³
- Cork: 240 kg/m³ (floats on water)

Floating rule:
- If an object's density is less than the fluid's density, it floats
- If an object's density is greater than the fluid's density, it sinks

Measuring density of a regular solid:
1. Measure mass using a balance
2. Measure dimensions using Vernier callipers or a ruler
3. Calculate volume (e.g., V = length × width × height for a rectangular block)
4. Calculate density: ρ = m/V

Measuring density of an irregular solid:
1. Measure mass using a balance
2. Fill a displacement can with water until it overflows
3. Place the object in the can; collect the displaced water in a measuring cylinder
4. The volume of displaced water = volume of the object
5. Calculate density: ρ = m/V

Measuring density of a liquid:
1. Measure mass of empty measuring cylinder
2. Pour in a known volume of liquid
3. Measure mass of cylinder + liquid
4. Mass of liquid = (mass of cylinder + liquid) − (mass of empty cylinder)
5. Calculate density: ρ = m/V
Worked Example

A metal block has mass 135 g and dimensions 5 cm × 3 cm × 3 cm.
(a) Volume = 5 × 3 × 3 = 45 cm³
(b) Density = m ÷ V = 135 ÷ 45 = 3.0 g/cm³ = 3000 kg/m³
(c) The block sinks in water (density of water = 1.0 g/cm³) because 3.0 > 1.0

Key Equation:
ρ = m/V

ρ = density (kg/m³ or g/cm³)
m = mass (kg or g)
V = volume (m³ or cm³)

1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a step-by-step method card for measuring density. Students follow each step and record results in a table.
Stretch:
A gold crown has mass 0.85 kg. When placed in a full beaker of water, 50 ml of water overflows. Is the crown pure gold? (Density of gold = 19 300 kg/m³)

Plenary (5 minutes)

Density Ranking

Students rank these materials by density (lowest to highest): gold, aluminium, water, air, iron, cork. Then check against a reference table.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can measure density of regular and irregular objects, convert units, and explain floating/sinking
  • Getting there: Can calculate density from given values but struggles with practical measurement
  • Not yet: Cannot rearrange ρ = m/V or measure volume of irregular objects

Lesson 3: Density and State Changes

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Ice floats!

Show a glass of water with ice cubes floating. Ask: "Ice is a solid form of water. Why does it float on liquid water? What does this tell us about the density of ice compared to water?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
How density changes during state changes:

1. Melting (solid → liquid):
- Particles move further apart (for most substances)
- Volume increases, mass stays the same
- Density decreases
- Exception: water — ice is LESS dense than liquid water (ice floats!)

2. Boiling (liquid → gas):
- Particles move much further apart
- Volume increases enormously (by about 1600× for water)
- Density decreases dramatically
- Gas is much less dense than liquid

Why water is special:
- Water molecules form hydrogen bonds that create an open, hexagonal structure in ice
- This makes ice less dense (917 kg/m³) than liquid water (1000 kg/m³)
- This is why ice floats — and why lakes freeze from the top down
- If ice sank, lakes would freeze solid from the bottom, killing all aquatic life

Density graphs:
A density graph shows how the density of a substance changes with temperature. For most substances, density decreases as temperature increases (thermal expansion). Water has an anomaly — its density increases from 0°C to 4°C, then decreases above 4°C.
Worked Example

A container holds 2 kg of water at 4°C (density = 1000 kg/m³). The water is heated to 80°C (density = 972 kg/m³).
(a) Volume at 4°C: V = m/ρ = 2/1000 = 0.002 m³ = 2 litres
(b) Volume at 80°C: V = m/ρ = 2/972 = 0.002058 m³ = 2.058 litres
(c) The volume has increased by 0.058 litres (58 ml) — this is thermal expansion
(d) The water is now less dense, so it has expanded

Key Equation:
V = m/ρ (rearranged from ρ = m/V)
Common Misconception: Students think water always expands when heated. Water actually contracts when heated from 0°C to 4°C (density increases). This is why 4°C water sinks to the bottom of a lake.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a table showing the density of water at different temperatures. Students identify the anomaly and explain it using the particle model.
Stretch:
Research the density of air at sea level and at the top of Mount Everest. Explain why air density decreases with altitude using the particle model.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

"Explain why ice floats on water, using ideas about density and the particle model." Students write a 3-mark answer.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain how density changes during state changes and describe the water anomaly
  • Getting there: Can describe density changes but cannot explain the water anomaly
  • Not yet: Cannot link density to the particle model or state changes

Homework Suggestions

  • Measure the density of five different objects around your home (e.g., apple, stone, coin, eraser, cork)
  • Create a bar chart comparing the densities of 10 common materials
  • Explain why a steel ship floats even though steel is denser than water
  • Research why hot air balloons work, using ideas about density and the particle model
