P11: Particle Motion and Pressure

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understanding gas pressure, the relationship between temperature and particle motion, and how pressure changes with volume and temperature. Absolute temperature and the Kelvin scale.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Explain how gas pressure arises from particle collisions
  • Describe the relationship between temperature, pressure, and volume for gases
  • Use the equation p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ for pressure-volume changes
  • Convert between Celsius and Kelvin
  • Explain the relationship between temperature and average kinetic energy

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of the particle model (P10)
  • Knowledge of the three states of matter
  • Ability to rearrange equations

Materials & Equipment

  • Scientific calculator
  • Syringe with sealed end (for Boyle's Law demonstration)
  • Temperature probe or thermometer
  • Balloon (for Charles's Law demonstration)
  • Ice, warm water, and beakers

Lesson 1: Gas Pressure and Temperature

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Syringe Challenge

Give each student a sealed syringe. Ask them to push the plunger in and then release it. Discuss: "What pushes the plunger back out? What happens if you push harder?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
How gas pressure arises:
Gas particles move randomly at high speeds. They collide with the walls of their container. Each collision exerts a small force on the wall. The total force from billions of collisions per second creates gas pressure.

Pressure = Force ÷ Area
Pressure is measured in pascals (Pa), where 1 Pa = 1 N/m²

Factors affecting gas pressure:

1. Temperature:
- When a gas is heated, particles gain kinetic energy
- They move faster and collide with walls more frequently and with greater force
- Pressure increases (if volume is constant)

2. Volume:
- When a gas is compressed (volume decreases), particles are in a smaller space
- They collide with walls more frequently
- Pressure increases (if temperature is constant)

3. Number of particles:
- More particles means more collisions per second
- Pressure increases (if volume and temperature are constant)

Absolute temperature (Kelvin scale):
- Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of particles
- At 0 K (absolute zero), particles have minimum kinetic energy
- 0 K = −273.15°C
- To convert: K = °C + 273
- Temperature in Kelvin is proportional to average kinetic energy
- We MUST use Kelvin for gas law calculations
Worked Example

A gas has a temperature of 27°C. Convert to Kelvin and explain what this means for particle motion.
K = 27 + 273 = 300 K
At 300 K, particles have a certain average kinetic energy. At 600 K, they would have double the average kinetic energy and move twice as fast on average.

Key Equations:
Pressure = Force ÷ Area (p = F/A)
K = °C + 273
0 K = −273.15°C (absolute zero)
Common Misconception: Students think temperature measures "how hot something is." Temperature is actually a measure of the average kinetic energy of particles. Two objects at the same temperature have the same average kinetic energy per particle, even if one has more total thermal energy (because it has more particles).

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a table for converting between °C and K. Students practise converting 10 different temperatures.
Stretch:
A sealed container of gas is heated from 20°C to 227°C. If the original pressure was 100 000 Pa, what is the new pressure? (Assume volume is constant.) Answer: p₂ = p₁ × T₂/T₁ = 100 000 × 500/293 = 170 649 Pa

Plenary (5 minutes)

Kelvin Conversions

Students convert: 0°C, 100°C, −273°C, 37°C (body temperature), −40°C to Kelvin. Discuss: "What is special about −273°C?"

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain how gas pressure arises, convert between °C and K, and describe the effect of temperature on pressure
  • Getting there: Can convert temperatures but cannot explain pressure in terms of particle collisions
  • Not yet: Cannot convert between °C and K or explain gas pressure

Lesson 2: Boyle's Law (Pressure and Volume)

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Syringe Investigation

Students use a sealed syringe to explore how pressure changes when they push the plunger. They record the position of the plunger and their observations. Discuss: "What happens to the pressure as you decrease the volume?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Boyle's Law: For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, pressure is inversely proportional to volume.

p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ (or pV = constant)

This means:
- If volume is halved, pressure doubles
- If volume is doubled, pressure halves
- If volume is reduced to one-third, pressure triples

Why does this happen?
When a gas is compressed into a smaller volume:
1. Particles are in a smaller space
2. They travel shorter distances before hitting a wall
3. They collide with walls more frequently
4. More collisions per second = higher pressure

Graphical representation:
- p vs V graph: hyperbola (curved line approaching axes)
- p vs 1/V graph: straight line through the origin (shows inverse proportionality)

Assumptions for Boyle's Law:
- Fixed mass of gas (no leaks)
- Temperature is constant
- Gas behaves ideally (particles have negligible volume and no intermolecular forces)
Worked Example

A gas has volume 4 cm³ and pressure 200 kPa. It is compressed to volume 2 cm³ at constant temperature.
p₁V₁ = p₂V₂
200 × 4 = p₂ × 2
p₂ = (200 × 4) ÷ 2 = 400 kPa

The pressure doubles when the volume is halved.

Boyle's Law:
p₁V₁ = p₂V₂

p = pressure (Pa or kPa)
V = volume (m³ or cm³)

pV = constant (at constant temperature)

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a table with some values filled in. Students complete the table using p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ and plot a p-V graph.
Stretch:
A gas has volume 600 cm³ at pressure 100 kPa. It is compressed to 200 cm³. The temperature then increases from 300 K to 450 K. Calculate the final pressure.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Check

A gas has volume 10 cm³ and pressure 300 kPa. What is the pressure when the volume is reduced to 5 cm³? (600 kPa)

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can apply Boyle's Law to solve problems and explain why pressure changes using the particle model
  • Getting there: Can use p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ but cannot explain the physics behind the law
  • Not yet: Cannot apply Boyle's Law or understand inverse proportionality

Lesson 3: Charles's Law and the Pressure Law

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Balloon in Freezer

Show a video of a balloon being placed in liquid nitrogen (or describe what happens). Ask: "Why does the balloon shrink? What happens when it is warmed back up?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Charles's Law: For a fixed mass of gas at constant pressure, volume is directly proportional to absolute temperature (Kelvin).
V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂

This means:
- If temperature doubles (in Kelvin), volume doubles
- If temperature triples (in Kelvin), volume triples
- If temperature halves (in Kelvin), volume halves

Pressure Law (Gay-Lussac's Law): For a fixed mass of gas at constant volume, pressure is directly proportional to absolute temperature (Kelvin).
p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂

This means:
- If temperature doubles (in Kelvin), pressure doubles
- If temperature triples (in Kelvin), pressure triples

Why do these laws work?
When temperature increases:
1. Particles gain kinetic energy
2. They move faster
3. They collide with walls more frequently and with greater force
4. For Charles's Law: the volume expands to keep pressure constant
5. For the Pressure Law: the volume stays constant, so pressure increases

The Ideal Gas Equation (Higher):
pV = nRT
- n = number of moles of gas
- R = gas constant (8.31 J/(mol·K))
- T = temperature in Kelvin
This combines Boyle's Law, Charles's Law, and the Pressure Law into one equation.
Worked Example

A gas has volume 500 cm³ at 20°C. It is heated to 127°C at constant pressure. What is the new volume?
T₁ = 20 + 273 = 293 K
T₂ = 127 + 273 = 400 K
V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂
500/293 = V₂/400
V₂ = (500 × 400) ÷ 293 = 683 cm³ (to 3 s.f.)

Key Equations:
Charles's Law: V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ (constant pressure)
Pressure Law: p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂ (constant volume)
Ideal Gas Equation: pV = nRT

All temperatures MUST be in Kelvin

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a conversion table: °C to K. Students practise identifying which gas law to use for different scenarios (constant pressure vs constant volume).
Stretch:
A gas at 27°C and 100 kPa is heated to 327°C and compressed to half its original volume. Calculate the new pressure using the combined gas law: p₁V₁/T₁ = p₂V₂/T₂.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Three Laws Summary

Students complete a comparison table for Boyle's Law, Charles's Law, and the Pressure Law: what is constant, what is proportional, and the equation for each.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can apply Charles's Law and the Pressure Law, convert temperatures, and explain the physics
  • Getting there: Can use the equations but struggles to identify which law applies in different scenarios
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between the three gas laws or convert temperatures to Kelvin

Homework Suggestions

  • Solve 10 gas law problems: five using Boyle's Law, three using Charles's Law, two using the Pressure Law
  • Convert 20 temperatures between °C and K, including negative Celsius values
  • Research why absolute zero is −273°C and explain what happens to particle motion at this temperature
  • Explain why a car tyre pressure increases on a long journey, using the Pressure Law
