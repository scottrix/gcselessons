Lesson 2: Core Concepts: prosocial behaviour

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on prosocial behaviour. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Prosocial behaviour is any act that benefits others. Altruism is helping with no expectation of personal gain - the debate is whether true altruism exists or whether all helping is ultimately self-serving.
Key Fact: The bystander effect: people are less likely to help when others are present. Latane & Darley's (1968) smoke experiment: 75% helped when alone vs 10-38% when in a group.
Key Fact: Diffusion of responsibility: in a group, each person assumes someone else will help. This was famously linked to the murder of Kitty Genovese (1964), where 38 witnesses allegedly did nothing - though later accounts question this version.
Key Fact: The cost-reward model (Piliavin): people weigh up the costs of helping against the rewards. If costs outweigh rewards, people are less likely to help. This model suggests helping is always rational and self-interested.
Key Fact: Factors increasing prosocial behaviour: similarity to the victim, being in a good mood, having training, and being in a small group or alone.
Psychology Exam Tips: When evaluating prosocial behaviour, use real-world examples: emergency situations, online helping behaviour, and charity giving. Consider whether lab experiments accurately reflect real emergencies.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Describe the bystander effect and explain why the presence of others reduces helping behaviour.

Answer: The bystander effect: more bystanders = less helping. Reasons: diffusion of responsibility, pluralistic ignorance (nobody else seems concerned), and audience inhibition (fear of looking foolish). Latane & Darley's decision-making model: notice -> interpret -> take responsibility -> decide how to help -> implement help.

Lesson 3: Application: prosocial behaviour

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Psychology Exam Tips. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Describe the bystander effect and explain why the presence of others reduces helping behaviour.

Answer: The bystander effect: more bystanders = less helping. Reasons: diffusion of responsibility, pluralistic ignorance (nobody else seems concerned), and audience inhibition (fear of looking foolish). Latane & Darley's decision-making model: notice -> interpret -> take responsibility -> decide how to help -> implement help.

Q2: Evaluate whether true altruism exists, using psychological research.

Answer: True altruism debate: Cialdini's negative state relief model argues that helping reduces our own distress - so all helping is selfish. Batson's empathy-altruism hypothesis argues that empathy motivates genuine altruism. Evidence: high-empathy people help even when they can easily escape the situation.

Q3: Explain how the cost-reward model accounts for prosocial behaviour and evaluate its limitations.

Answer: The cost-reward model explains helping as rational calculation. Strengths: supported by research. Limitations: ignores emotional responses (people often help instinctively), cannot explain impulsive heroism, assumes people always act rationally in emergencies.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: prosocial behaviour

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Evaluate the validity of the bystander effect as an explanation for failing to help in emergencies. <div class="

A grade 9 response will: describe the bystander effect with Latane & Darley's research; explain mechanisms (diffusion of responsibility, pluralistic ignorance, audience inhibition); evaluate using real-world evidence; consider alternative explanations; conclude: the bystander effect is well-established but real emergencies involve more complex processes than lab studies capture.

Exam Tips: More bystanders = LESS helping. This is the bystander effect. | Diffusion of responsibility: 'Someone else will do it.' Pluralistic ignorance: 'Nobody else seems worried.' | Cost-reward model: if COSTS > REWARDS, no helping. If REWARDS > COSTS, helping occurs.
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often make mistakes here. Wrong: People don't help in emergencies because they are apathetic and don't care about others. Correct: Research shows that failing to help is usually a social-cognitive process, not apathy. Bystanders often do care but are inhibited by: pluralistic ignorance, diffusion of responsibility, and audience inhibition. Education about the bystander effect significantly increases helping rates.
