Lesson 2: Core Concepts: perception

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on perception. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Perception is the process of interpreting sensory information to make sense of the world. Sensation is detecting stimuli; perception is interpreting them - they are distinct processes.
Key Fact: Gregory's constructivist theory: perception uses past experience and expectations to interpret sensory input (top-down processing). Visual illusions support this - we misinterpret because we apply assumptions (e.g. the Muller-Lyer illusion).
Key Fact: Gibson's direct theory: perception is entirely determined by sensory input from the environment (bottom-up processing). We perceive affordances - what the environment offers us (e.g. a chair affords sitting). No past experience needed.
Key Fact: Factors affecting perception: culture (Hudson's study showed Western and non-Western people perceive depth cues differently), emotion (anxious people perceive threats more readily), motivation (hungry people are more likely to perceive food-related images).
Key Fact: Visual illusions reveal how perception can be distorted: the Ames Room exploits size constancy; the Kanizsa Triangle shows we perceive shapes that don't physically exist (illusory contours).
Psychology Exam Tips: When evaluating perception theories, consider whether perception is more influenced by nature (Gibson - innate) or nurture (Gregory - learned). Support your argument with specific studies and acknowledge that both processes likely contribute.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Compare Gregory's constructivist theory with Gibson's direct theory of perception.

Answer: Gregory: perception is constructed using past experience and expectations (top-down). Gibson: perception is directly picked up from the environment (bottom-up). Gregory explains illusions and ambiguous figures but struggles with infant perception. Gibson explains accurate perception in novel situations but cannot explain illusions.

Lesson 3: Application: perception

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Psychology Exam Tips. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Compare Gregory's constructivist theory with Gibson's direct theory of perception.

Answer: Gregory: perception is constructed using past experience and expectations (top-down). Gibson: perception is directly picked up from the environment (bottom-up). Gregory explains illusions and ambiguous figures but struggles with infant perception. Gibson explains accurate perception in novel situations but cannot explain illusions.

Q2: Explain how culture can affect perception, using research evidence.

Answer: Hudson (1960) found that Western participants easily perceived depth in 2D pictures using perspective cues, while non-Western participants (who had less exposure to 2D representations) found it harder. This suggests that some perceptual skills are learned through cultural experience.

Q3: Evaluate what visual illusions tell us about the nature of perception.

Answer: Illusions show perception is not a direct copy of reality - the brain interprets and sometimes misinterprets information. This supports Gregory's top-down processing view. However, not all illusions are explained by past experience; some (like the Kanizsa Triangle) may involve innate perceptual processes, supporting Gibson's view partially.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: perception

Duration: 50 minutes

Main Content (35 minutes)

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Evaluate whether perception is primarily a top-down or bottom-up process. <div class="

A grade 9 response will: outline Gregory's top-down theory with evidence (illusions, ambiguous figures, past experience); outline Gibson's bottom-up theory with evidence (affordances, accurate perception in novel situations); evaluate both; conclude: perception involves both processes - bottom-up provides raw data, top-down refines interpretation. Interactionist approaches best explain the evidence.

Exam Tips: Sensation = detecting. Perception = interpreting. They are different processes. | Gregory = top-down (experience-based). Gibson = bottom-up (environment-based). | Always name a SPECIFIC illusion (Muller-Lyer, Ames Room) when discussing perception.
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often make mistakes here. Wrong: Perception is entirely determined by the information available in the environment, so visual illusions cannot exist. Correct: Visual illusions clearly demonstrate that perception is not simply a direct read-out of environmental information - the brain must be interpreting sensory input using assumptions and expectations. Even Gibson acknowledged that illusions exist; he argued they are exceptions caused by unusual environments that trick normally reliable perceptual systems.
