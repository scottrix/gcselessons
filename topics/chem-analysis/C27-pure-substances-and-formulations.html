C27: Pure Substances and Formulations

Foundation All Boards

Distinguish between pure substances and mixtures, describe methods of separation, and explain what a formulation is.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish between pure substances and mixtures
  • Describe methods of separation and when each is appropriate
  • Explain what a formulation is and give examples

Prerequisites

  • Atoms and Elements (C1) — atoms, molecules, elements
  • Crude Oil (C23) — fractional distillation

Lesson 1: Pure Substances and Mixtures

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Pure or Impure?

Is tap water pure? Is distilled water pure? Is air pure? What makes something 'pure' in chemistry?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Pure substance: contains only one type of atom or molecule. Has a fixed melting point and boiling point. Examples: pure water (H₂O only), pure copper (Cu only), pure sodium chloride (NaCl only).

Mixture: contains two or more different substances that are not chemically bonded. Each substance keeps its own properties. Can be separated by physical methods. Examples: air (mixture of gases), salt solution (salt + water), alloy (mixture of metals).

Key differences:
• Pure substances have sharp melting/boiling points
• Mixtures melt/boil over a range of temperatures
• Pure substances have fixed composition
• Mixtures have variable composition

Methods of separation:

1. Filtration: separates insoluble solid from liquid. (e.g. sand from water)
2. Evaporation: separates soluble solid from liquid. (e.g. salt from water — evaporate water, leave salt)
3. Simple distillation: separates solvent from dissolved solid. (e.g. pure water from salt water)
4. Fractional distillation: separates liquids with different boiling points. (e.g. ethanol from water, crude oil fractions)
5. Chromatography: separates dissolved substances based on different solubilities. (e.g. ink components, food dyes)
6. Magnetic separation: separates magnetic materials. (e.g. iron from sulfur)
7. Decanting: separates immiscible liquids (liquids that don't mix). (e.g. oil and water)
8. Centrifuging: separates a solid from a liquid by spinning rapidly.
9. Using a solvent: dissolves one component but not another. (e.g. dissolving salt from a salt/sand mixture)
Pure water vs tap water

Distilled/pure water: only H₂O molecules. Fixed boiling point (100°C) and melting point (0°C).
Tap water: H₂O + dissolved minerals (calcium, magnesium), chlorine, sometimes trace metals. Boils over a range of temperatures. Impure.

Separating sand and salt

1. Add water → salt dissolves, sand doesn't
2. Filter → sand stays on filter paper, salt solution passes through
3. Evaporate salt solution → salt remains
4. (Optional) Distil water to recover pure water

Pure substance: one type of atom/molecule, sharp mp/bp
Mixture: two+ substances, not bonded, variable mp/bp

Separation methods:
Filtration → insoluble solid from liquid
Evaporation → soluble solid from liquid
Simple distillation → solvent from dissolved solid
Fractional distillation → liquids with different bps
Chromatography → dissolved substances by solubility

Common Misconception

Mixtures can be separated by chemical reactions.

Mixtures are separated by physical methods (filtration, distillation, etc.) — no chemical reactions needed. The substances in a mixture are not chemically bonded, so they can be separated without breaking or forming bonds.

Differentiation

Support:
Use physical demonstrations. Filter sand from water. Evaporate salt solution. Distil salt water. Let children observe each method.
Stretch:
Design an experiment to separate a mixture of iron filings, salt, sand, and sawdust. What order would you use the separation methods?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Name the separation method for: (a) oil from water, (b) salt from water, (c) sand from salt solution, (d) ethanol from water, (e) ink components.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Distinguishes pure substances from mixtures and selects appropriate separation methods.
  • Getting there: Knows the difference but cannot always select the right separation method.
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish pure substances from mixtures. Reteach with demonstrations.

Lesson 2: Formulations

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Recipe

A cake recipe is a formulation: specific amounts of flour, sugar, eggs, butter, baking powder. Why can't you just use 'some of each'? What happens if you change the amounts?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
A formulation is a mixture that has been designed to have specific properties. It contains precisely measured amounts of each component. Formulations are usually made by chemists or engineers for a specific purpose.

Examples of formulations:
• Paint: pigment (colour) + binder (glues it to surface) + solvent (controls thickness) + additives (UV protection, drying agents)
• Medicine: active ingredient + filler/bulking agent + flavouring + colouring + coating (to control release)
• Cleaning products: surfactant (removes grease) + water + fragrance + preservative
• Food: ingredients in precise amounts + preservatives + flavourings + colourings
• Fertiliser: nitrogen + phosphorus + potassium compounds in specific ratios

Why formulations matter:
• Each component has a specific job
• The amounts must be precise — too much or too little of one component changes the properties
• They are designed for a specific purpose (not random mixtures)

Formulations vs mixtures:
• A mixture is any combination of substances (random)
• A formulation is a mixture designed for a specific purpose with precise amounts (intentional)
Paint formulation

A typical paint contains: pigment (TiO₂ for white — gives colour), binder (acrylic polymer — adheres to surface), solvent (water or organic — controls viscosity), additives (biocide to prevent mould, UV stabiliser to prevent fading). Each component has a specific role and must be present in the right amount.

Fertiliser formulation

NPK fertiliser: Nitrogen (N) promotes leaf growth, Phosphorus (P) promotes root growth, Potassium (K) promotes flower/fruit development. Different plants need different NPK ratios. A formulation ensures the correct ratio for the target crop.

Formulation = mixture designed for specific purpose
• Precise amounts of each component
• Each component has a specific role
• Designed by scientists/engineers

Examples: paint, medicine, cleaning products, food, fertiliser

Common Misconception

Formulations are the same as mixtures.

All formulations are mixtures, but not all mixtures are formulations. A formulation is a special type of mixture that has been carefully designed with specific amounts of each component to achieve a particular purpose. Random mixtures are not formulations.

Differentiation

Support:
Look at product labels (paint, shampoo, food). Identify the ingredients. What does each component do? Why are the amounts important?
Stretch:
Design a formulation for a sunscreen. What components would you include? What would each do? How would you test if it works?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Explain why medicine is a formulation, not just a mixture. What would happen if you changed the amount of active ingredient?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains what a formulation is, gives examples, and distinguishes from random mixtures.
  • Getting there: Knows formulations are designed mixtures but cannot give detailed examples.
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish formulations from mixtures. Reteach with product label examples.

Homework Suggestions

  • Investigate the formulation of a product (paint, shampoo, etc.): list ingredients and the role of each
  • Practise selecting separation methods for 10 different mixtures
  • Research: why is pure water important in hospitals and laboratories?
