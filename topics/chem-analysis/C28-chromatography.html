C28: Chromatography

Foundation All Boards

Describe how chromatography works, calculate Rf values, and use chromatography to identify substances.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe how paper chromatography separates mixtures
  • Calculate Rf values and explain their significance
  • Use chromatography to identify unknown substances

Prerequisites

  • Pure Substances and Formulations (C27) — mixtures, separation

Lesson 1: Paper Chromatography

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Ink Colours

A black pen draws a line on chromatography paper. When the paper is dipped in water, the ink separates into different colours. Why does this happen? What does it tell you about the ink?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Paper chromatography separates dissolved substances based on their different solubilities in a solvent.

How it works:
1. Draw a pencil line near the bottom of chromatography paper (pencil doesn't dissolve)
2. Spot the mixture (e.g. ink) on the pencil line
3. Place the paper in a solvent (e.g. water) — solvent level below the spot
4. Solvent travels up the paper by capillary action
5. Different substances dissolve in the solvent to different extents
6. Substances that are MORE soluble in the solvent travel FURTHER up the paper
7. Substances that are LESS soluble travel LESS distance
8. Different substances separate into different spots

Key terms:
• Stationary phase: the chromatography paper (stays still)
• Mobile phase: the solvent (moves up the paper)
• Solvent front: how far the solvent has traveled

Rf value (Retardation factor):
Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent
• Rf is always between 0 and 1
• Each substance has a characteristic Rf value in a given solvent
• Same substance on same paper with same solvent → same Rf
• Rf values can be used to identify unknown substances
Chromatography of ink

Black ink separates into 3-4 different coloured spots. Each spot is a different dye. Some dyes are more soluble in water (travel further). Some are less soluble (stay closer to the start). This proves that black ink is a mixture, not a pure substance.

Calculating Rf

If a spot moves 3cm and the solvent front moves 6cm: Rf = 3/6 = 0.5. If another spot moves 1cm: Rf = 1/6 = 0.17. The spot with the higher Rf is more soluble in the solvent.

Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent

Rf is always between 0 and 1
Same substance + same solvent = same Rf

Paper chromatography:
Stationary phase = paper
Mobile phase = solvent
More soluble = travels further = higher Rf

Common Misconception

The substance that travels furthest is the most dense.

The substance that travels furthest is the most soluble in the solvent (mobile phase). It has the weakest attraction to the paper (stationary phase) and the strongest attraction to the solvent.

Differentiation

Support:
Do the practical if possible. Use water-soluble pens and coffee filter paper. Dip in water and watch the colours separate.
Stretch:
Why is pencil used instead of pen for the baseline? What would happen if you used pen for the baseline?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Calculate Rf for a spot that moves 4.5cm when the solvent front moves 7.2cm. What does a high Rf value tell you about a substance?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Describes paper chromatography, calculates Rf values, and explains what they mean.
  • Getting there: Can describe the process but cannot always calculate Rf correctly.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain how chromatography works. Reteach with practical demonstration.

Lesson 2: Using Chromatography to Identify Substances

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Food Dye Detective

A food manufacturer claims their sweets contain only approved food dyes. How could you use chromatography to check this?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Using chromatography to identify substances:
1. Run a chromatogram of the unknown mixture
2. Run known pure substances (standards) on the same paper
3. Compare the Rf values of the spots in the mixture with the Rf values of the standards
4. If Rf values match → the substance is identified

Applications of chromatography:
• Food industry: testing for approved food dyes, detecting illegal colourings
• Pharmaceutical industry: checking purity of drugs, identifying ingredients
• Forensic science: analysing evidence (fibres, inks, drugs)
• Environmental monitoring: detecting pollutants in water
• Sports testing: detecting performance-enhancing drugs

Thin-layer chromatography (TLC):
• Similar to paper chromatography but uses a glass/plastic plate coated with silica gel or alumina
• Faster and more sensitive than paper chromatography
• Used in laboratories for quick analysis

Purity testing:
• A pure substance shows ONE spot on a chromatogram
• A mixture shows MULTIPLE spots
• If a 'pure' substance shows more than one spot, it is impure
Testing food dyes

Run chromatogram of: red food dye (standard), yellow food dye (standard), and the unknown sweet coating. If the unknown shows a spot with the same Rf as the red dye standard, it contains that dye. If it shows extra spots, it contains unlisted dyes.

Testing purity

Pure aspirin: one spot. Impure aspirin: two or more spots (aspirin + degradation products or impurities). Pharmaceutical companies use chromatography to ensure drugs are pure before sale.

Identification: compare Rf of unknown with Rf of known standards

Purity test: pure substance = 1 spot; impure = multiple spots

Applications: food testing, drug testing, forensics, environmental monitoring

Common Misconception

Chromatography can only separate coloured substances.

Chromatography separates any dissolved substance — coloured or not. Colourless substances can be visualised by using UV light (they fluoresce) or by spraying with a developing reagent (e.g. ninhydrin for amino acids).

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a completed chromatogram and ask children to calculate Rf values and identify the unknown substances by comparing with standards.
Stretch:
Why is it important to use the same solvent when comparing Rf values? What would happen if you used a different solvent?

Plenary (5 minutes)

A chromatogram of an unknown mixture shows two spots with Rf = 0.3 and Rf = 0.7. Known standards have Rf = 0.3 (substance X) and Rf = 0.7 (substance Y). What does the mixture contain?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Uses chromatography to identify substances, compares Rf values, and explains purity testing.
  • Getting there: Can calculate Rf but cannot always explain how identification works.
  • Not yet: Cannot use chromatography for identification. Reteach with worked examples comparing standards and unknowns.

Homework Suggestions

  • Practise calculating Rf values from 10 different chromatograms
  • Research: how is gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) used in forensic science?
  • Design an experiment to test whether a drink contains multiple food dyes
