B2: Cell Division

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand how cells divide by mitosis, the cell cycle, and the role of stem cells in growth, repair, and differentiation.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Explain why body cells need to divide by mitosis
  • Describe the stages of the cell cycle (interphase and mitosis)
  • Explain how stem cells differentiate into specialised cells
  • Discuss the ethical issues surrounding embryonic and adult stem cells (Higher)

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of cell structure and organelles (B1)
  • Knowledge that the nucleus contains DNA

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Printed diagrams of the cell cycle
  • String or pipe cleaners (for modelling chromosome behaviour)
  • Coloured cards for sorting activities

Lesson 1: The Cell Cycle and Mitosis

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Think-Pair-Share

Ask: "When you cut your skin, how does it heal?" Students think for 30 seconds, discuss with a partner, then share with the class. Introduce the idea that new cells are made by dividing existing cells.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Explain the reasons for cell division:
  • Growth — organisms grow by increasing cell number
  • Repair — replacing damaged or dead cells
  • Reproduction — in organisms that reproduce asexually
The cell cycle has two main stages:
  • Interphase — the longest part of the cycle. The cell grows, copies its DNA (replication), and prepares to divide. DNA replicates so each chromosome becomes two identical copies joined at the centromere (sister chromatids).
  • Mitosis — the nucleus divides. Four stages: (1) Chromosomes condense and become visible, (2) Chromosomes line up in the middle of the cell, (3) Sister chromatids are pulled apart to opposite poles, (4) Nuclear membranes reform around each set of chromosomes.
  • Cytokinesis — the cytoplasm divides, forming two identical daughter cells.
Key point: mitosis produces two genetically identical daughter cells, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell.
Worked Example

"A human body cell has 46 chromosomes. After mitosis, how many chromosomes will each daughter cell have?" Answer: 46. Mitosis maintains the chromosome number. Draw a simple diagram showing one cell with 4 chromosomes dividing into two cells each with 4 chromosomes.

Common Misconception

"Mitosis produces cells that are half the size of the parent"

Daughter cells are initially smaller than the parent cell but grow to the same size during interphase before the next division.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Use physical models: four pieces of string as chromosomes. Physically demonstrate lining up, pulling apart, and reforming. Focus on "mitosis = two identical cells" as the core concept.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Introduce the concept of the cell cycle checkpoint system. Ask why uncontrolled cell division is dangerous (cancer). Research how many times a human cell can divide before becoming senescent (Hayflick limit, ~50-70 divisions).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Summary Diagram

Students draw a simple diagram of the cell cycle showing interphase → mitosis → two daughter cells. Label what happens at each stage.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the cell cycle in order and explain that mitosis produces identical cells
  • Getting there: Knows mitosis produces two cells but confuses interphase with mitosis
  • Not yet: Cannot sequence the cell cycle or explain why cells divide

Lesson 2: Stem Cells

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Question

Ask: "Can any cell in your body become any other type of cell?" Discuss that most cells are specialised and cannot change, but some unspecialised cells called stem cells can differentiate.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Explain stem cells:
  • Stem cells are unspecialised cells that can differentiate into different cell types
  • Embryonic stem cells — found in the early embryo (blastocyst). Can become any cell type in the body (totipotent/pluripotent). Used in research and some medical treatments.
  • Adult stem cells — found in bone marrow, skin, and other tissues. More limited in what they can become (multipotent). Bone marrow stem cells produce blood cells.
  • Meristems (plants) — regions of unspecialised cells in roots and shoots that can differentiate into any plant cell type. Allow plants to grow throughout their lives.
Applications:
  • Replacing damaged cells (e.g. nerve cells in spinal cord injuries)
  • Treating leukaemia with bone marrow transplants
  • Research into growing organs in the laboratory
Higher content: ethical issues around embryonic stem cells — destroying embryos vs potential to cure diseases.
Worked Example

"Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of using embryonic stem cells in medicine." Advantage: they can become any cell type, so could treat many diseases. Disadvantage: obtaining them requires destroying a human embryo, which raises ethical concerns.

Common Misconception

"Stem cells can only become one type of cell"

Stem cells are defined by their ability to become multiple different cell types. The more types they can become, the more "potent" they are.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a sorting activity: cards with cell types on one side and "stem cell source" on the other. Match red blood cell → bone marrow stem cell. Focus on the definition "unspecialised cells that can become different cell types."
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Stage a mini-debate: "Should embryonic stem cell research be allowed?" Give students statements to evaluate. Research iPS cells (induced pluripotent stem cells) as an alternative to embryonic stem cells.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Three Types

Students write three sentences: one each about embryonic stem cells, adult stem cells, and plant meristems. Each sentence must include what they are and one use.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe three types of stem cells and discuss ethical issues with clear reasoning
  • Getting there: Can describe stem cells but struggles to evaluate ethical issues
  • Not yet: Cannot define stem cells or distinguish between types

Lesson 3: Stem Cells — Applications and Ethics (Higher)

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Opinion Line

Draw a line on the board labelled "Strongly Agree" to "Strongly Disagree." Read the statement: "It is acceptable to use embryonic stem cells for medical research." Students place themselves on the line and explain why.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Deep dive into stem cell applications:
  • Treating diabetes: replacing insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas
  • Treating Parkinson's disease: replacing dopamine-producing neurons
  • Spinal cord injuries: replacing damaged nerve cells
  • Skin grafts: growing new skin from stem cells for burn victims
  • Leukaemia: bone marrow transplants replace cancerous blood cells
Ethical considerations for evaluation:
  • Some religions believe life begins at conception — destroying an embryo is wrong
  • Embryos used are surplus from IVF and would otherwise be destroyed
  • The potential to cure millions of diseases justifies the research
  • Adult stem cells are less controversial but more limited
  • iPS cells (adult cells reprogrammed to behave like embryonic stem cells) may solve the ethical dilemma
For the exam, students need to be able to argue both sides and reach a justified conclusion.
Worked Example

"Evaluate the use of embryonic stem cells to treat medical conditions." Students should discuss: what stem cells are, how they could be used (e.g. replacing nerve cells), ethical concerns (destruction of embryos), and counter-arguments (embryos would be discarded anyway, iPS cells as alternative). Conclude with a balanced judgement.

Common Misconception

"Stem cell therapy is available for all diseases right now"

Most stem cell treatments are still experimental. Only bone marrow transplants for blood disorders are widely established. Many proposed treatments are in clinical trials.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a structured paragraph frame for evaluation: "Stem cells could be used to... However, some people think... On the other hand... In conclusion..." Focus on being able to state one advantage and one disadvantage.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the specific mechanism of iPS cells. Investigate a current clinical trial using stem cells. Write a formal letter to a government minister arguing for or against stem cell research funding.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exit Ticket

Students write on a sticky note: one thing they learned, one thing they found difficult, one question they still have. Collect and review.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can provide a balanced evaluation of stem cell use with scientific reasoning and ethical awareness
  • Getting there: Can state advantages and disadvantages but lacks depth in evaluation
  • Not yet: Cannot discuss applications or ethical issues of stem cells

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a labelled diagram of the cell cycle showing interphase and mitosis stages
  • Research one medical condition that could potentially be treated with stem cells and write a 200-word summary
  • Complete a comparison table of embryonic vs adult vs plant stem cells
  • Write a short ethical argument (for or against) embryonic stem cell research
