B5: Culturing Microorganisms

Higher All Boards

Learn how to grow and study microorganisms using aseptic technique, and understand the factors that affect microbial growth.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Higher only
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the aseptic technique for culturing microorganisms
  • Explain how to calculate the number of microorganisms in a sample using dilution and colony counting
  • Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of different methods of controlling microbial growth
  • Understand the role of agar gel, incubators, and petri dishes in culturing

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of bacteria and their structure (B1)
  • Basic knowledge of microorganisms as pathogens (B12)

Materials & Equipment

  • Petri dishes with nutrient agar (if practical is possible)
  • Inoculating loop (or wooden splint)
  • Bunsen burner or spirit lamp
  • Agar plates (pre-poured)
  • Safety goggles
  • Disinfectant spray
  • Images/videos of aseptic technique if practical is not possible

Lesson 1: Aseptic Technique

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Observation

Show a petri dish with bacterial colonies growing on agar. Ask: "How do you think these colonies were produced? Where did they come from?" Discuss that each colony grew from a single bacterium that divided millions of times.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Explain the aseptic technique step by step:
  • Step 1: Prepare agar plates. Nutrient agar is heated until molten, then poured into sterile petri dishes and left to set. Agar provides nutrients for microbial growth.
  • Step 2: Sterilise equipment. The inoculating loop is sterilised by holding it in a Bunsen burner flame until red hot. Work near the flame (updraught keeps contaminants away).
  • Step 3: Transfer sample. Dip the cooled loop into the sample (e.g. pond water, soil suspension) and streak it across the agar surface in a zig-zag pattern. Replace the lid immediately.
  • Step 4: Seal and incubate. Seal the plate with sticky tape (not completely airtight — gas exchange needed). Incubate at 25°C (or 37°C for human pathogens) for 24-48 hours, upside down to prevent condensation dripping on colonies.
  • Step 5: Count and identify. Count the number of colonies. Each colony = one original bacterium (a colony-forming unit, CFU). Identify by colony shape, colour, size.
Safety: all microorganisms should be treated as pathogenic. Wear goggles, tie back hair, disinfect work surface before and after. Dispose of cultures by autoclaving or placing in disinfectant.
Worked Example

"Explain why the inoculating loop is sterilised before use." Answer: To kill any microorganisms already on the loop, preventing contamination of the agar plate and ensuring that only the intended sample is cultured. This is part of the aseptic technique.

Common Misconception

"One colony = one bacterium"

One colony = one colony-forming unit (CFU). A CFU could be a single bacterium, a pair, or a small cluster that all end up in the same place. We assume each colony came from one original cell, but it could technically be a few cells.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Watch a video of aseptic technique together and have students fill in a numbered sequence of steps. Focus on the key safety points: sterilise, work near flame, seal plate, incubate upside down.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Discuss why incubating at 37°C is appropriate for human pathogens but 25°C is used for environmental samples. Research the dangers of working with unknown environmental microorganisms.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Step Ordering

Give students cards with each step of the aseptic technique written on them. Students arrange them in the correct order. Fastest correct sequence wins.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the aseptic technique in correct order with explanations for each step
  • Getting there: Can sequence the steps but misses some key details
  • Not yet: Cannot sequence the aseptic technique steps

Lesson 2: Counting Microorganisms and Factors Affecting Growth

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Calculation Warm-Up

"If 1 cm³ of pond water is diluted 100 times and 0.1 cm³ of the dilution is placed on an agar plate, and 45 colonies grow after incubation, how many bacteria were in the original 1 cm³?" Answer: 45 × 100 × 10 = 45,000 bacteria per cm³. Walk through the calculation.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Cover two main areas: 1. Counting microorganisms:
  • Serial dilution method: dilute the sample multiple times (e.g. 1:10, 1:100, 1:1000)
  • Spread 0.1 cm³ of each dilution onto separate agar plates
  • Incubate and count colonies on the plate with 20-200 colonies (too many to count = TNTC, too few = statistically unreliable)
  • Calculate: number of bacteria per cm³ = (number of colonies × dilution factor) ÷ volume plated
2. Factors affecting growth:
  • Temperature: most bacteria grow best at 37°C (body temperature). Below 10°C: very slow growth. Above 45°C: many bacteria die.
  • Moisture: bacteria need water for chemical reactions. Dry conditions slow growth.
  • pH: most bacteria prefer neutral pH (6.5-7.5). Very acidic or alkaline conditions inhibit growth.
  • Oxygen: aerobic bacteria need oxygen; anaerobic bacteria are killed by oxygen; facultative anaerobes can survive with or without oxygen.
  • Nutrient availability: bacteria need carbon, nitrogen, and other minerals. Agar provides these.
Worked Example

"A student dilutes a bacterial sample 1000 times. They plate 0.1 cm³ and count 32 colonies. Calculate the number of bacteria per cm³ in the original sample." Answer: 32 × 1000 ÷ 0.1 = 320,000 bacteria per cm³.

Common Misconception

"Bacteria always need oxygen to grow"

Some bacteria are anaerobic and are actually killed by oxygen. Others (facultative anaerobes) can grow with or without oxygen. The oxygen requirement depends on the species.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a template calculation with blanks to fill in. Focus on the formula: bacteria per cm³ = colonies × dilution factor ÷ volume. Practice with simple numbers.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Design an experiment to investigate how temperature affects the rate of bacterial growth. Include independent variable, dependent variable, control variables, and how results would be analysed.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Five Factors

Students write the five factors that affect microbial growth from memory. Check in pairs and add any they missed.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can perform serial dilution calculations and list all factors affecting growth
  • Getting there: Can list factors but makes errors in calculations
  • Not yet: Cannot perform dilution calculations or list factors

Homework Suggestions

  • Design an experiment to test how one factor affects microbial growth (plan only — do not perform without supervision)
  • Research one disease caused by bacteria and explain how culturing helps in diagnosis
  • Complete a serial dilution worksheet with 8 calculation problems
  • Create a flowchart of the aseptic technique with annotations explaining why each step is necessary
