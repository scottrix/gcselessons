B3: Transport in Cells

Foundation Higher All Boards

Learn about the three mechanisms of transport across cell membranes: diffusion, osmosis, and active transport.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe and explain diffusion as the net movement of particles from high to low concentration
  • Define osmosis and explain its effects on plant and animal cells
  • Describe active transport and explain when it is used
  • Investigate the factors affecting the rate of osmosis

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of cell structure and the cell membrane
  • Basic knowledge of solutions and concentrations

Materials & Equipment

  • Visking tubing (dialysis tubing) for diffusion demonstrations
  • Potato cylinders and varying sugar solutions for osmosis investigation
  • Electronic balance
  • Measuring cylinders and rulers
  • Beakers and distilled water

Lesson 1: Diffusion

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Spray Demonstration

Open a marker pen or perfume at one side of the room. Ask students how the smell reaches them. Discuss that molecules are constantly moving and spreading out.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Define diffusion: the net movement of particles from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration, down the concentration gradient, caused by the random motion of particles. Key points:
  • Diffusion happens in gases and liquids (where particles can move freely)
  • The greater the concentration gradient, the faster the rate of diffusion
  • Factors affecting rate: concentration gradient, surface area, temperature, distance
  • In living organisms, diffusion occurs across cell membranes
  • Oxygen diffuses into cells; carbon dioxide diffuses out
  • The cell membrane is partially permeable — it allows some substances through but not others
Higher content: the relationship between surface area and volume — smaller cells have a larger surface area to volume ratio, so diffusion is more efficient. This is why cells are small.
Worked Example

"A cell has a high concentration of oxygen inside and a low concentration outside. In which direction will oxygen diffuse?" Answer: Oxygen diffuses OUT of the cell, from high to low concentration. This is how respiring cells obtain oxygen — the oxygen concentration is always lower inside the cell than in the surrounding capillary.

Common Misconception

"Diffusion requires energy"

Diffusion is a passive process — it does not require energy. Particles move due to their own random kinetic energy. The net movement is simply from where there are more particles to where there are fewer.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Use a practical demonstration: drop food colouring into water and observe spreading. Students describe what they see using the word "diffusion." Provide a definition card to keep.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Calculate surface area to volume ratios for different sized cubes. Explain why large organisms need specialised exchange surfaces rather than relying on diffusion alone.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Quiz

Five questions: What is diffusion? In which direction? Does it need energy? Name two examples in the body. What affects its rate?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can define diffusion accurately and give examples with clear explanations
  • Getting there: Understands the direction of diffusion but cannot explain why it happens
  • Not yet: Confuses diffusion with other transport mechanisms

Lesson 2: Osmosis

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Observation

Show a raisin that has been placed in water overnight (it will be plump) and a grape placed in salt solution (it will be shrivelled). Ask students to explain the difference. Introduce osmosis as the cause.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Define osmosis: the movement of water molecules from a region of higher water concentration to a region of lower water concentration, through a partially permeable membrane, down the water concentration gradient. Key points:
  • A partially permeable membrane allows water through but not dissolved solutes
  • In a dilute solution, there is a higher water concentration → water moves INTO the cell
  • In a concentrated solution, there is a lower water concentration → water moves OUT of the cell
  • Animal cells in pure water: water enters by osmosis → cell swells and may burst (lyse)
  • Animal cells in salt solution: water leaves by osmosis → cell shrivels (crenation)
  • Plant cells in pure water: water enters → cell becomes turgid (swollen but cell wall prevents bursting)
  • Plant cells in salt solution: water leaves → cell becomes flaccid then plasmolysed (membrane pulls away from cell wall)
  • Plant cells in isotonic solution: no net water movement → cell is flaccid
Worked Example

"A plant cell is placed in a very concentrated sugar solution. Describe what happens to the cell." Answer: Water moves out of the cell by osmosis (from higher water concentration inside to lower outside). The vacuole shrinks, cytoplasm pulls away from the cell wall. The cell becomes plasmolysed.

Common Misconception

"Osmosis is the movement of water from concentrated to dilute solutions"

Osmosis is the movement of water from a higher water concentration (dilute solution) to a lower water concentration (concentrated solution). Remember: water follows salt (and other solutes).

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Use the analogy of people moving from a crowded room to an empty room — "water particles want to spread out." Provide pre-drawn diagrams of cells in different solutions for labelling.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Investigate osmosis with potato cylinders in different sugar concentrations. Calculate percentage change in mass and plot a graph. Determine the isotonic concentration.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Diagram Challenge

Students draw three animal cells: one in pure water (labelled "swollen/bursting"), one in isotonic (labelled "normal"), one in salt solution (labelled "shrunken"). Add arrows showing water movement.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can define osmosis precisely and predict what happens to cells in different solutions
  • Getting there: Understands the direction of water movement but confuses plant and animal cell responses
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish osmosis from diffusion or predict water movement

Lesson 3: Active Transport

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Think About It

Ask: "If a plant root needs to absorb mineral ions from soil where the ion concentration is LOWER than inside the root, how can this happen? Diffusion would go the wrong way!" Introduce active transport.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Define active transport: the movement of particles from an area of lower concentration to an area of higher concentration (against the concentration gradient), using carrier proteins and energy from respiration. Key points:
  • Active transport moves substances AGAINST the concentration gradient
  • Requires energy (ATP) from respiration in mitochondria
  • Uses specific carrier proteins in the cell membrane
  • Examples: root hair cells absorbing mineral ions from soil; nerve cells maintaining ion gradients; glucose absorption in the small intestine
  • Compared to diffusion: diffusion is passive and goes with the gradient; active transport uses energy and goes against the gradient
Summary comparison table:
FeatureDiffusionOsmosisActive Transport
SubstanceAny particleWater onlyAny particle
DirectionHigh → Low conc.High → low water conc.Low → high conc.
Membrane needed?NoYes (partially perm.)Yes (carrier protein)
Energy needed?NoNoYes (ATP)
Worked Example

"Explain how a root hair cell absorbs mineral ions from the soil when the soil has a lower concentration of minerals than the cell." Answer: The mineral ion concentration is lower in the soil than in the root hair cell, so diffusion would move ions OUT. Instead, active transport uses carrier proteins in the cell membrane and energy from respiration to pump ions INTO the cell against the concentration gradient.

Common Misconception

"Active transport uses osmosis to move water"

Active transport moves dissolved substances (ions, sugars, amino acids) against their concentration gradient using energy and carrier proteins. Osmosis is specifically about water movement through a partially permeable membrane.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on the key definition: "moving against the gradient, needs energy." Use a physical analogy — pushing a ball uphill requires effort (energy), whereas rolling downhill does not.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research sodium-potassium pumps in nerve cells. Calculate the number of ATP molecules needed for active transport. Compare the three transport mechanisms in a detailed comparison table with examples from the body.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Classification Challenge

Give 8 scenarios. Students classify each as diffusion, osmosis, or active transport. E.g. "Water entering a plant root" (osmosis), "Oxygen moving into a muscle cell" (diffusion), "Glucose absorbed from the gut into blood" (active transport).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can define all three transport mechanisms, compare them, and explain examples from the body
  • Getting there: Can define each mechanism but struggles to apply to unfamiliar examples
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between the three transport mechanisms

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a comparison poster of diffusion, osmosis, and active transport with diagrams
  • Investigate osmosis at home: place an egg (shell dissolved in vinegar) in syrup and observe changes
  • Write an explanation of why active transport is essential for plant nutrition
  • Complete a worksheet on predicting the direction of osmosis in different scenarios
