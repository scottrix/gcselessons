B1: Cell Structure

Foundation Higher All Boards

Explore the structure and function of animal and plant cells, including specialised cells and the use of microscopes to observe cell detail.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the structure and function of cell organelles
  • Compare animal and plant cells, identifying similarities and differences
  • Use a light microscope to observe and draw cells
  • Understand the differences between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells (Higher)
  • Describe the structure of specialised cells and relate structure to function

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding that living things are made of cells
  • Familiarity with the concept of a scientific diagram

Materials & Equipment

  • Light microscope (if available)
  • Prepared slides of plant and animal cells
  • Onion skin or cheek cells for fresh mounts
  • Blunt pencil, ruler, sharp pencil for scientific drawings
  • Coloured pencils (for diagrams)
  • Textbook or printed reference sheet of cell organelles

Lesson 1: Animal and Plant Cells

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Quiz

Ask: "What do all living things have in common?" List responses on a whiteboard. Then ask: "What is the smallest unit of life?" Introduce the cell theory: all living organisms are made of cells, cells are the basic unit of life, and all cells come from pre-existing cells.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Teach the seven key organelles common to both animal and plant cells:
  • Nucleus — contains DNA, controls cell activities. Draw a large circle with a smaller dark circle inside to represent it.
  • Cytoplasm — jelly-like substance where chemical reactions occur. Label the "filling" of the cell.
  • Cell membrane — controls what enters and leaves the cell. Draw it as a thin line around the edge.
  • Mitochondria — site of aerobic respiration, releases energy. Oval-shaped organelles scattered in cytoplasm.
  • Ribosomes — site of protein synthesis. Very small dots in the cytoplasm.
  • Cell wall (plant only) — rigid outer layer, provides support. Drawn as a thick line outside the cell membrane.
  • Chloroplasts (plant only) — site of photosynthesis, contain chlorophyll. Green oval structures.
  • Permanent vacuole (plant only) — filled with cell sap, maintains turgor pressure. Large space in the centre.
Draw a comparison table on the board. Have students copy it and fill in tick/cross for each organelle in animal vs plant cells.
Worked Example

"List four features found in plant cells but not animal cells." Answer: cell wall, chloroplasts, permanent vacuole, and (at higher level) amyloplasts for starch storage. Students should be able to state the function of each.

Common Misconception

"Plant cells always have chloroplasts"

Only plant cells exposed to light contain chloroplasts. Root cells, for example, do not have chloroplasts because they are underground and do not photosynthesise.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a pre-drawn cell diagram with labels to trace. Use a matching activity pairing organelle names with their functions. Reduce the number of organelles to learn (focus on nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane, cell wall, chloroplast).
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Ask students to explain why the nucleus is the "control centre" — what would happen if it were removed? Challenge them to research ribosomes and explain their role in protein synthesis.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Label the Diagram

Show an unlabeled cell diagram on the board. Students write down as many organelle names as they can remember in one minute.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can name all organelles and describe their functions accurately
  • Getting there: Can name most organelles but confuses some functions
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between animal and plant cells or name organelles

Lesson 2: Using a Microscope

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

magnification Challenge

Write "3x" on a piece of paper. Ask students to estimate what the number would look like under 10x magnification. Introduce the idea that microscopes make things appear larger.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Cover the following key points:
  • Light microscopes use lenses to magnify images. Typical magnification: x40 to x1500.
  • Magnification = eyepiece lens magnification × objective lens magnification (e.g. x10 × x40 = x400).
  • Resolution: the ability to distinguish between two close points. Light microscopes have limited resolution (~200nm).
  • Electron microscopes have much higher magnification and resolution (transmission and scanning types).
  • Preparing a microscope slide: (1) Place specimen on slide, (2) Add a drop of stain if needed, (3) Lower cover slip at an angle to avoid air bubbles, (4) Secure with clips.
  • Scientific drawings: Use sharp pencil, single continuous lines (no shading), label with a ruler, labels on the right-hand side, title the drawing, include magnification.
If microscopes are available, prepare a slide of onion epidermis. If not, show high-quality images and walk through the steps.
Worked Example

"A microscope has an eyepiece lens of x10 and an objective lens of x40. What is the total magnification?" Answer: 10 × 40 = x400. Practice with other combinations: x10 × x4 = x40; x10 × x100 = x1000.

Common Misconception

"Magnification makes things clearer"

Magnification makes things appear larger. Resolution determines how clear the image is. You can magnify a blurry image and it stays blurry — you need better resolution.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a step-by-step card for using the microscope. Practice the magnification calculation with simple numbers. Focus on drawing simple cells accurately rather than labelling every organelle.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Compare light and electron microscopes in a table. Ask why electron microscopes are not used for observing living cells (they require a vacuum and the specimen is dead). Research the differences between TEM and SEM.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Calculation

Give five different eyepiece/objective lens combinations. Students calculate total magnification for each. Fastest correct answers win.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can calculate magnification, describe how to prepare a slide, and draw scientific diagrams correctly
  • Getting there: Can calculate magnification but makes errors in drawing or slide preparation steps
  • Not yet: Cannot perform magnification calculations or describe microscope use

Lesson 3: Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Comparison

Show two cell diagrams side by side — a bacterium and a human cell. Ask students to write down three differences they can spot. Discuss responses.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Introduce the two main cell categories:
  • Eukaryotic cells — have a nucleus enclosed by a membrane. Includes all animal and plant cells, fungi, and protists.
  • Prokaryotic cells — no nucleus. DNA is a single circular chromosome in the cytoplasm (nucleoid region). Includes bacteria.
Key features of bacterial (prokaryotic) cells:
  • Single circular DNA chromosome (not in a nucleus)
  • May have small circular DNA molecules called plasmids
  • Cell wall (but not made of cellulose — made of peptidoglycan)
  • Some have a capsule (slime layer) outside the cell wall
  • Some have flagella for movement
  • Size: typically 0.2–5.0 micrometres (much smaller than eukaryotic cells)
Size comparison: bacteria are typically 10–100x smaller than plant/animal cells.
Worked Example

"Give two differences between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells." Answer: (1) Prokaryotes have no nucleus; eukaryotes have a membrane-bound nucleus. (2) Prokaryotes are much smaller than eukaryotic cells. (3) Prokaryotes may have plasmids; eukaryotes do not. (4) Prokaryotes have circular DNA; eukaryotes have linear chromosomes.

Common Misconception

"Bacteria are too small to be cells"

Bacteria are cells — they are prokaryotic cells. They are simply much smaller than eukaryotic cells. They still have all the essential components of life: DNA, cytoplasm, cell membrane, and ribosomes.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a Venn diagram template for sorting features into prokaryotic, eukaryotic, or both. Focus on the key difference (nucleus present/absent) and the size difference.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research antibiotic resistance and the role of plasmids in transferring resistance genes between bacteria. Discuss why prokaryotes reproduce so quickly (binary fission, 20-minute generation time).

Plenary (5 minutes)

True or False

Read statements and students hold up True/False cards: "Bacteria have a nucleus" (F), "Bacteria have DNA" (T), "Bacteria are larger than plant cells" (F), "Plasmids are small circular DNA molecules" (T).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can clearly distinguish between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, listing key structural differences
  • Getting there: Knows the main difference (nucleus) but struggles with additional features
  • Not yet: Cannot differentiate between the two cell types

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a labelled comparison poster of animal and plant cells using different colours for shared and unique organelles
  • Complete a magnification worksheet with 10 calculation problems
  • Research one type of specialised cell (e.g. red blood cell, root hair cell) and prepare a short presentation on its structure and function
  • Write a glossary of all organelle names with definitions
