B4: Cell Specialisation

Foundation Higher All Boards

Examine how cells become specialised for particular functions, and relate the structure of specialised cells to their roles in the body and in plants.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Explain what is meant by cell differentiation and specialisation
  • Describe the structure and function of key specialised animal cells
  • Describe the structure and function of key specialised plant cells
  • Relate cell structure to function in specialised cells

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of cell structure and organelles (B1)
  • Knowledge of stem cells and differentiation (B2)

Materials & Equipment

  • Diagrams or images of specialised cells (red blood cell, sperm cell, nerve cell, root hair cell, etc.)
  • Blank labelled cell templates
  • Coloured pencils
  • Whiteboard and pens

Lesson 1: Animal Specialised Cells

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Matching Game

Show images of five different specialised cells (without labels). Ask students to guess what each cell does based on its shape. Reveal the answers and introduce the concept that structure is related to function.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Explain that cells differentiate to become specialised for particular functions. Key specialised animal cells:
  • Red blood cell: biconcave disc shape (increases surface area for oxygen transport), no nucleus (more room for haemoglobin), contains haemoglobin (binds oxygen). Transports oxygen from lungs to tissues.
  • Sperm cell: has a long tail (flagellum) for swimming, acrosome containing enzymes to penetrate the egg, streamlined head, mitochondria in the midpiece for energy. Designed to reach and fertilise the egg.
  • Nerve cell (neurone): very long axon (can be over 1 metre) to transmit electrical impulses over long distances, dendrites (branching extensions) to receive signals from other neurones, myelin sheath speeds up impulse transmission.
  • Muscle cell (skeletal): cylindrical shape, contains many mitochondria (needs lots of energy for contraction), contains protein fibres (actin and myosin) that slide over each other to cause contraction.
  • Ciliated epithelial cell: cilia (hair-like projections) on the surface that beat in a wave-like motion to move mucus. Found lining the trachea and bronchi.
Emphasise the structure-function relationship for each cell type.
Worked Example

"Explain how the structure of a red blood cell is adapted for its function." Answer: (1) Biconcave disc shape → increases surface area to volume ratio for efficient oxygen diffusion. (2) No nucleus → more space for haemoglobin molecules. (3) Flexible membrane → allows it to squeeze through narrow capillaries. (4) Haemoglobin → binds oxygen reversibly in the lungs and releases it in tissues.

Common Misconception

"All cells in the body are the same"

While all body cells contain the same DNA, different genes are switched on in different cells. This causes them to produce different proteins and become specialised for different functions — a process called differentiation.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a matching activity: cell images on one set of cards, names and functions on another. Students match them. Focus on 3 key cells (red blood, sperm, nerve).
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research neutrophils (white blood cells) and their role in phagocytosis. Compare their structure to lymphocytes. Ask why cancer cells lose their specialised features.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Draw

Students have 3 minutes to draw and label one specialised animal cell from memory. Peer assess for accuracy.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe at least three specialised cells with clear structure-function links
  • Getting there: Can name specialised cells but struggles to explain how structure relates to function
  • Not yet: Cannot name specialised cells or connect structure to function

Lesson 2: Plant Specialised Cells

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Picture Clue

Show a magnified image of a root hair cell. Ask: "Why does this cell have such a long extension?" Discuss that root hair cells are found in the root and need to absorb water and minerals from the soil. The long extension increases surface area.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Key specialised plant cells:
  • Root hair cell: long hair-like extension (root hair) that projects into the soil. Greatly increases surface area for water and mineral ion absorption. Contains many mitochondria for active transport of minerals. No chloroplasts (underground, no light). Has a large permanent vacuole.
  • Xylem vessel: dead cells joined end to end with no end walls (hollow tube). Cell walls strengthened with lignin. Transports water and dissolved minerals from roots to leaves. No living contents (no cytoplasm, nucleus, or organelles).
  • Phloem sieve tube elements: living cells with no nucleus, joined end to end with sieve plates (perforated end walls). Companion cells beside them provide ATP and proteins. Transports sugars (from photosynthesis) from leaves to other parts of the plant.
  • Palisade mesophyll cell: column-shaped cells near the upper surface of the leaf. Contain many chloroplasts (can have 50-100 per cell). Main site of photosynthesis in the leaf.
  • Guard cells: bean-shaped cells that surround the stomata (pores). Control the opening and closing of stomata to regulate gas exchange and water loss. Contain chloroplasts (unlike most epidermal cells).
For each cell, draw the cell and label the key structural features. Ask students to explain why each feature helps the cell do its job.
Worked Example

"Explain why root hair cells have a large surface area but no chloroplasts." Answer: The long root hair extension increases the surface area for absorbing water and minerals from the soil. They have no chloroplasts because they are underground where there is no light, so they cannot photosynthesise.

Common Misconception

"Xylem cells are alive and transport water actively"

Xylem vessels are dead at maturity — they have no cytoplasm or organelles. Water moves through them by a passive process driven by transpiration pull and adhesion/cohesion of water molecules, not by active transport.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a cloze passage: "The root hair cell has a long _____ to increase _____ for absorbing _____." Students fill in the blanks. Focus on root hair cell and xylem vessel as the two key examples.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the structure of the leaf in detail (palisade, spongy mesophyll, epidermis, guard cells). Create an annotated cross-section diagram showing how each cell type contributes to photosynthesis.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Function to Structure

Read out a function, students name the cell: "Absorbs water from the soil" (root hair), "Transports water up the stem" (xylem), "Carries out photosynthesis in the leaf" (palisade mesophyll), "Controls water loss from the leaf" (guard cells).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe plant specialised cells with clear structure-function relationships
  • Getting there: Can name plant cells and some adaptations but misses key links
  • Not yet: Cannot identify specialised plant cells or explain their adaptations

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a revision poster showing 6 specialised cells (3 animal, 3 plant) with labelled diagrams and functions
  • Write a "day in the life" narrative from the perspective of a red blood cell, describing its journey through the body
  • Research one specialised cell not covered in class (e.g. ciliated cell, adipocyte) and present findings
  • Complete a comparison of xylem and phloem in a table format
