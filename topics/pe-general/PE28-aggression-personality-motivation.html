Lesson 2: Core Concepts: aggression, personality and motivation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on aggression, personality and motivation. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Aggression: Behaviour that is intended to harm another person or object. In sport, aggression must be distinguished from assertion - assertion is forceful but legitimate play within the rules, while aggression goes beyond the rules with intent to harm.
Direct Aggression: Aggression that is aimed directly at another person, involving physical contact or confrontation with the intent to cause harm.
Indirect Aggression: Aggression that is not aimed directly at another person. It may involve verbal abuse, psychological intimidation, or aggression directed at objects rather than people.
Personality: The unique combination of characteristics, traits and behaviours that make an individual distinct. In sport, personality influences the type of activity a performer is drawn to and how they respond to different situations.
Evaluating Personality Theory: While personality type can suggest which sports a person may enjoy, it is not deterministic. Many introverts succeed in team sports and many extroverts excel in individual events. Personality is one factor among many that influences sports preference, alongside opportunity, coaching, and social influences.
Motivation: The drive, desire and internal mechanism that stimulates a person to act or behave in a certain way. Motivation determines the direction, intensity and persistence of effort in sport.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Explain the difference between direct and indirect aggression, giving a sporting example of each.

Answer: Direct aggression is aimed directly at another person with physical contact and intent to harm, e.g. a rugby player punching an opponent during a maul. Indirect aggression does not involve direct physical contact with the victim - it may be verbal abuse, psychological intimidation, or aggression towards objects, e.g. a tennis player smashing their racket or verbally abusing an opponent from across the net.

Lesson 3: Application: aggression, personality and motivation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Aggression, Direct Aggression, Indirect Aggression. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain the difference between direct and indirect aggression, giving a sporting example of each.

Answer: Direct aggression is aimed directly at another person with physical contact and intent to harm, e.g. a rugby player punching an opponent during a maul. Indirect aggression does not involve direct physical contact with the victim - it may be verbal abuse, psychological intimidation, or aggression towards objects, e.g. a tennis player smashing their racket or verbally abusing an opponent from across the net.

Q2: Why are introverts more likely to choose individual sports while extroverts prefer team sports?

Answer: Introverts prefer individual sports because they enjoy solitary, focused activities where they can concentrate in a quiet, controlled environment without social pressure. They are comfortable with sustained internal focus and lower arousal. Extroverts prefer team sports because they are energised by social interaction, enjoy the dynamic environment of team play, and thrive on higher arousal levels that come from competition and physical contact with others.

Q3: Describe the difference between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. Which is more effective for long-term participation and why?

Answer: Intrinsic motivation comes from within - the performer participates for enjoyment, satisfaction and the inherent pleasure of the activity. Extrinsic motivation comes from outside - the performer participates for external rewards such as trophies, medals, money or praise. Intrinsic motivation is more effective for long-term participation because it is self-sustaining; if a performer enjoys the activity itself, they will continue even when external rewards are absent. Extrinsic motivation may disappear when rewards are removed.

Q4: Explain the difference between assertion and aggression in sport. Why is this distinction important?

Answer: Assertion is forceful but legitimate play within the rules of the sport, e.g. a strong, fair shoulder charge in rugby where the player is going for the ball. Aggression is behaviour with intent to harm that goes beyond the rules, e.g. deliberately elbowing an opponent off the ball. The distinction is important because assertion is a positive and legal part of sport, while aggression is harmful, illegal and should be penalised. Coaches and players must channel aggressive feelings into assertive, legal play.

Q5: A parent offers their child money for every goal they score. Using psychological theory, evaluate the potential positive and negative effects of this approach.

Answer: Positive effects: The money reward may increase the child's initial motivation to score goals, encourage them to work harder in training, and provide a clear, measurable target. Negative effects: The child may become solely focused on scoring (ignoring teamwork and passing), their intrinsic motivation for the game may decrease (overjustification effect), and if the money reward is removed, their motivation may collapse. The child may also cheat or behave selfishly to score. A better approach would be to praise effort and improvement, supporting intrinsic motivation.

Q6: How might an introvert's personality affect their choice of sport and their optimal arousal level? Give specific examples.

Answer: An introvert is likely to choose individual sports such as archery, golf, swimming or long-distance running, where they can focus inwardly without the pressure of social interaction. Their optimal arousal level would be lower - introverts perform best in calm, controlled environments where they can concentrate without the stimulation of a noisy crowd or team dynamic. For example, an introvert would perform best in archery with low arousal (steady hand, focused mind) rather than the high-arousal environment of a rugby match.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: aggression, personality and motivation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on aggression, personality and motivation from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Always distinguish between aggression and assertion - examiners test this distinction frequently | Give specific sporting examples for each type of aggression, personality and motivation | Remember: introvert ≠ shy; extrovert ≠ confident - focus on social energy and arousal preference | When evaluating motivation, always consider both intrinsic and extrinsic together | Know the overjustification effect - too much extrinsic reward can reduce intrinsic motivation | Most performers have a mix of both motivation types - don't present them as either/or
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often confuse assertion with aggression. Wrong: "A hard tackle in rugby is aggressive behaviour." Correct: "A hard but fair tackle within the rules is assertion. It only becomes aggression if the intent is to harm the opponent rather than win the ball." Students often say extrinsic motivation is "bad." Wrong: "Extrinsic motivation is bad and should be avoided." Correct: "Extrinsic motivation can be effective for initial engagement and short-term goals, but over-reliance on it is problematic as motivation may disappear when rewards are removed. A combination of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation is ideal."
