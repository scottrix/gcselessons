Lesson 2: Core Concepts: engagement patterns in physical activity and sport

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on engagement patterns in physical activity and sport. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Engagement patterns: The ways in which different groups of people participate (or don't participate) in physical activity and sport. These patterns vary significantly based on personal, social and cultural factors.
Key Point: The process of socialisation means that children learn attitudes and behaviours from their family. A child raised in a sporty family is far more likely to participate in sport than one whose family has no interest in physical activity. This influence is especially strong in early childhood.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Explain how gender stereotyping can limit participation in sport for both males and females.

Answer: Gender stereotyping limits participation by creating expectations about which sports are appropriate for each gender. Girls may be discouraged from playing "masculine" sports like rugby or football, while boys may avoid "feminine" sports like dance or netball. This reduces the range of opportunities explored. Stereotyping also affects media coverage and funding, with women's sport receiving less investment and visibility, which limits role models and aspiration for girls.

Lesson 3: Application: engagement patterns in physical activity and sport

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Engagement patterns, Key Point. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain how gender stereotyping can limit participation in sport for both males and females.

Answer: Gender stereotyping limits participation by creating expectations about which sports are appropriate for each gender. Girls may be discouraged from playing "masculine" sports like rugby or football, while boys may avoid "feminine" sports like dance or netball. This reduces the range of opportunities explored. Stereotyping also affects media coverage and funding, with women's sport receiving less investment and visibility, which limits role models and aspiration for girls.

Q2: Describe how religion can be a barrier to participation in sport and suggest how this barrier could be overcome.

Answer: Religion can be a barrier through: (1) Fasting during Ramadan reduces energy and hydration for Muslim athletes, making training and competition difficult. (2) Modesty requirements may prevent participation in activities requiring revealing clothing, such as swimming. (3) Religious observance on holy days may conflict with training or competition schedules. Barriers can be overcome by: scheduling around fasting times, providing facilities that allow modest dress (e.g. burkinis for swimming), offering single-sex sessions, and respecting holy day commitments when planning fixtures.

Q3: Explain the term 'socialisation' and how family influences can affect a young person's participation in sport.

Answer: Socialisation is the process by which a child learns the norms, values and behaviours of their family and community. In sport, a child whose parents are active and enthusiastic about physical activity is likely to adopt similar attitudes through copying their behaviour and receiving encouragement. Parents who pay for club membership, provide transport and watch matches enable participation. Conversely, a child from a family with no interest in sport is less likely to see physical activity as normal or desirable.

Q4: How does socio-economic status affect engagement in physical activity? Give three specific barriers.

Answer: Socio-economic status affects engagement through: (1) Cost - low-income families cannot afford membership fees, specialist kit, equipment or transport to facilities, limiting access to many sports. (2) Access - deprived areas typically have fewer and poorer-quality sports facilities, reducing opportunities for local participation. (3) Time - people in low-paid or irregular employment may work long or unsociable hours, leaving less time available for sport compared to those with more flexible working conditions.

Q5: Explain how the Paralympic Games and classification systems have helped increase participation in disability sport.

Answer: The Paralympic Games have increased participation by raising the profile and visibility of disability sport, inspiring disabled people to take up sport, and challenging negative stereotypes about disability. Classification systems have helped by grouping athletes by the type and severity of their impairment, ensuring fair competition. This means athletes with different disabilities can compete meaningfully against others with similar limitations, which encourages participation by making competition achievable and fair.

Q6: Suggest and explain three strategies that could increase teenage girls' participation in physical activity.

Answer: Three strategies: (1) Girls-only sessions - provide a non-judgemental environment where girls can participate without feeling self-conscious or intimidated, building confidence. (2) Female role models and ambassadors - using high-profile female athletes (e.g. Lionesses) to inspire girls and show that women's sport is valued. (3) Social media campaigns (e.g. This Girl Can) - challenge stereotypes about women in sport and promote the idea that sport is for everyone, regardless of body type or ability level.

Exam-Style Question

Attempt a past-paper style question on engagement patterns in physical activity and sport from the exam board past paper finder (see Resources), then mark it against the scheme.

Exam Tips: Always explain both the barrier AND a strategy to overcome it - examiners want both | Use specific examples for each factor (e.g. Ramadan for religion, not just "religious reasons") | Remember that barriers can be practical (cost, transport) or psychological (stereotyping, self-consciousness) | When discussing age, address both young people AND older adults | Classification in disability sport = fair competition by grouping similar impairments | Link socialisation to family influence - this is a key connection examiners look for
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often list barriers without suggesting solutions. Wrong: "Poor people can't afford sport." Correct: "Low-income families may not be able to afford club membership fees and kit. This can be addressed through subsidised access and equipment recycling schemes." Students often confuse race and religion as factors. Wrong: "Race means you can't eat certain foods." Correct: "Religion may affect diet and fasting (e.g. Ramadan), while race relates to ethnic background which can influence cultural expectations and exposure to racism in sport."
