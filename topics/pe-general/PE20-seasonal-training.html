Lesson 2: Core Concepts: seasonal training

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on seasonal training. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Definition: A training year (macrocycle) is divided into distinct phases, each with specific training goals. The training programme is structured so the athlete peaks (reaches their best performance) at the right time - usually for the most important competitions.
Goal: Build a base of fitness and develop the specific skills needed for the upcoming competitive season. The aim is to prepare the body and mind for the demands of competition.
Goal: Maintain the fitness built in pre-season while performing at the highest possible level in competitions. Training shifts from building fitness to maintaining it and peaking for key events.
Tapering: A planned reduction in training volume (by 40-60%) in the 7-14 days before a major competition. Intensity may remain high but the total training load is reduced. This allows the body to fully recover, replenish energy stores and repair any remaining muscle damage, so the athlete is at peak physical and mental condition for competition.
Goal: Allow the body and mind to recover from the demands of the competitive season. Prevent burnout, repair injuries and recharge for the next pre-season. This is NOT a period of complete inactivity.

Lesson 3: Application: seasonal training

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Goal, Goal, Tapering. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Describe the training focus during the pre-season phase.

Answer: Pre-season focuses on building a base of fitness: extensive aerobic training (continuous and Fartlek) for cardiovascular endurance, weight training for strength and power, skill development and refinement, and flexibility work. Training volume is high and intensity gradually increases towards the start of the competitive season.

Q2: Explain what tapering is and why it is used before major competitions.

Answer: Tapering is a planned reduction in training volume (by 40-60%) in the 7-14 days before a major competition. It is used to allow the body to fully recover from training, replenish energy stores (glycogen and PC), repair muscle damage and ensure the athlete is at peak physical and mental condition for the competition. Intensity may remain high but total training load is reduced.

Q3: Why is active recovery preferred over complete rest during the post-season phase?

Answer: Active recovery (light exercise such as swimming, cycling or walking) is preferred over complete rest because it: maintains some fitness and prevents reversibility; keeps blood flowing to aid recovery and repair; helps remove waste products from the body; prevents stiffness and maintains joint mobility; and provides a psychological break from competitive sport without losing all training benefits.

Q4: Compare the training volume and intensity across the three phases of a training year.

Answer: Pre-season: high training volume, moderate-to-high intensity (gradually increasing). Competition season: moderate training volume (reduced), high intensity in shorter sessions. Post-season: low training volume and low intensity. The pattern follows a curve - building up in pre-season, maintaining in competition, and dropping back in post-season.

Q5: Explain why a footballer's training programme would look different in July (pre-season) compared to March (competition season).

Answer: In July (pre-season), a footballer would do high-volume training: long-distance running for aerobic base, weight training for strength, and gradually increasing intensity. There are no competitive matches, so all energy goes into training. In March (mid-competition season), training volume is reduced to maintain fitness without causing fatigue for matches. Sessions are shorter and more intense, with focus on tactics and sport-specific skills. The priority is performing well in matches, not building new fitness.

Q6: What is periodisation and why is it important for athletes?

Answer: Periodisation is the systematic planning of training across a year (macrocycle) into distinct phases (mesocycles and microcycles) with specific goals for each phase. It is important because it ensures the athlete peaks at the right time (for major competitions), prevents overtraining by including recovery periods, allows progressive development of fitness, reduces injury risk, and balances training, competition and rest.
