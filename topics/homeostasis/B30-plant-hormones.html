B30: Plant Hormones

Foundation Higher All Boards

Learn about plant hormones including auxins, gibberellins, and ethene, and how they control growth, tropisms, and fruit ripening.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the roles of auxins, gibberellins, and ethene in plant growth
  • Explain phototropism and gravitropism (geotropism) in terms of auxin distribution
  • Investigate the effect of light on plant growth direction
  • Describe practical applications of plant hormones in agriculture and gardening

Prerequisites

  • Knowledge of plant structure and growth (B9)
  • Understanding of hormones in the endocrine system (B27)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Growing seedlings (cress or mustard) for investigation
  • Cardboard box with one side open (for light direction experiment)
  • Ruler for measuring growth

Lesson 1: Plant Hormones — Auxins

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Observation

Show a plant growing towards a window. Ask: "Why does the plant grow towards the light? How does it 'know' where the light is?" Discuss that plants respond to their environment using hormones.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Plant hormones: chemicals produced in one part of a plant that affect growth and development in another part. Unlike animals, plants don't have glands or a circulatory system — hormones move through plant tissues by diffusion and active transport. Key plant hormones:
  • Auxins: produced in the tips of shoots and roots. Control cell elongation (making cells longer). The main auxin is indole-3-acetic acid (IAA).
  • Gibberellins: produced in young leaves, seeds, and roots. Control stem elongation, seed germination, and flowering.
  • Ethene (ethylene): a gas that controls fruit ripening and leaf fall.
  • Cytokinins: promote cell division.
Tropisms: growth responses to stimuli:
  • Phototropism: growth response to light. Shoots grow TOWARDS light (positive phototropism). Roots grow AWAY from light (negative phototropism).
  • Gravitropism (geotropism): growth response to gravity. Roots grow TOWARDS gravity (positive gravitropism). Shoots grow AWAY from gravity (negative gravitropism).
Auxin and phototropism:
  • Auxin is produced at the tip of the shoot
  • When light comes from one direction, auxin moves to the SHADY side of the shoot
  • On the shady side, auxin causes cells to elongate (grow longer)
  • The cells on the shady side grow longer than those on the sunny side
  • This causes the shoot to bend towards the light
Auxin and gravitropism:
  • In roots, gravity causes auxin to accumulate on the LOWER side
  • In roots, high auxin concentration INHIBITS cell elongation (opposite to shoots)
  • The cells on the upper side grow faster than those on the lower side
  • This causes the root to bend downwards (towards gravity)
Worked Example

"Explain why a shoot grows towards light, using your knowledge of auxin." Answer: (1) Auxin is produced at the tip of the shoot. (2) When light comes from one direction, auxin is transported to the shaded side. (3) Higher auxin concentration on the shady side causes cells there to elongate more. (4) The cells on the shady side grow longer than those on the sunny side. (5) This differential growth causes the shoot to bend towards the light.

Common Misconception

"Auxin always promotes growth"

Auxin promotes cell elongation in shoots (higher concentration = more growth). But in roots, high auxin concentration inhibits growth. The effect of auxin depends on the tissue type and concentration. This is why roots grow away from light and shoots grow towards it.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on shoots: "Light causes auxin to move to the shady side → shady side grows more → shoot bends towards light." Provide a diagram with arrows showing auxin distribution and growth direction.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Investigate phototropism experimentally: grow cress seedlings in a dark box with one side open. Measure the bending angle over several days. Discuss why roots show negative phototropism.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Draw It

Students draw a shoot bending towards light, showing where auxin is distributed (shady side) and which cells are longer. Label the key features.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain phototropism and gravitropism in terms of auxin distribution and cell elongation
  • Getting there: Knows that auxin causes bending but cannot explain the mechanism
  • Not yet: Cannot explain tropisms or the role of auxin

Lesson 2: Gibberellins and Ethene

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Ripening Fruit

Show a green banana and a yellow banana. Ask: "What causes fruit to ripen? What chemical is responsible?" Discuss that a plant hormone called ethene controls ripening.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Gibberellins:
  • Produced in young leaves, seeds, and roots
  • Stem elongation — gibberellins cause stems to grow longer by stimulating cell division and elongation
  • Seed germination — break seed dormancy and stimulate germination. Gibberellins signal the seed to produce enzymes that break down stored food reserves.
  • Flowering — can be used to induce flowering in plants that would not normally flower
  • Fruit development — stimulate fruit growth
  • Practical uses: increasing grape size for wine production, encouraging malting in barley for brewing, breaking dormancy in seeds
Ethene (ethylene):
  • A gaseous hormone — it is a gas, so it can diffuse through the air
  • Fruit ripening — triggers the ripening process: starch → sugar, chlorophyll breaks down (green → red/yellow), pectinase softens the fruit
  • Leaf fall (abscission) — causes cells at the base of leaf stalks to break down, allowing leaves to fall in autumn
  • Practical uses: commercially used to ripen fruit after picking (e.g. bananas are picked green and ripened with ethene during transport)
Worked Example

"Explain how ethene is used commercially in the banana industry." Answer: Bananas are picked while still green and hard (unripe) so they can be transported without damage. During shipping, ethene gas is pumped into the containers. The ethene triggers the ripening process: starch is converted to sugar (making them sweet), chlorophyll breaks down (green → yellow), and pectinase softens the fruit. By the time they reach the shop, they are yellow and ready to eat.

Common Misconception

"Fruit ripens because it matures on the plant"

While fruit does ripen on the plant, the process is controlled by the hormone ethene. Fruit can also be ripened after picking by exposure to ethene gas. The ethene triggers chemical changes (starch to sugar, colour changes, softening) that constitute ripening.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on two hormones: gibberellins (help plants grow and seeds germinate) and ethene (ripens fruit). Provide a simple matching activity: hormone → function.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research how plant hormones are used in commercial agriculture: auxins in rooting powders, gibberellins in seedless grape production, ethene in fruit ripening. Discuss the ethical implications of using hormones in food production.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Two Hormones

Students write the name and three functions of gibberellins, and the name and two functions of ethene. Compare with a partner.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the roles of gibberellins and ethene with practical applications
  • Getting there: Can name the hormones but cannot describe their functions
  • Not yet: Cannot name the plant hormones or their functions

Lesson 3: Practical Applications and Investigation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Gardening Question

Ask: "How do gardeners use plant hormones without realising it? (Hint: rooting powder, fruit bowls, banana skins in compost.)" Discuss everyday uses.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Practical uses of plant hormones:
  • Auxins in rooting powder: contains synthetic auxin (IBA — indolebutyric acid). When applied to the cut end of a stem, it stimulates root growth. Used for taking cuttings in gardening.
  • Gibberellins in agriculture: used to increase grape size, stimulate seed germination, break dormancy, and induce flowering in commercial plant production.
  • Ethene in fruit ripening: used commercially to ripen fruit during transport and storage. Also used to trigger uniform ripening so all fruit ripens at the same time.
  • Weedkillers (herbicides): some herbicides are synthetic auxins. They cause weeds to grow uncontrollably and die. Selective herbicides target broad-leaved weeds without affecting grass crops.
Investigating phototropism:
  1. Grow cress seedlings in the dark for 2-3 days (etiolated — pale, long stems)
  2. Place seedlings in a cardboard box with a hole in one side
  3. Leave for 3-4 days, ensuring light only enters from one direction
  4. Observe: seedlings bend towards the light
  5. Measure the angle of bending and compare with a control (seedlings in uniform light)
This demonstrates phototropism and the role of auxin.
Worked Example

"A gardener wants to grow new rose plants from cuttings. Explain how they could use plant hormones to increase the success rate." Answer: The gardener could dip the cut end of the stem in rooting powder, which contains synthetic auxin (IBA). The auxin stimulates cell division and root formation at the cut end. This increases the chance of the cutting developing roots and becoming a new plant.

Common Misconception

"Plant hormones only affect growth"

While growth is a major function, plant hormones also control: seed germination, fruit ripening, leaf fall, flowering, and responses to stress (e.g. wound hormones). They play a role in virtually every aspect of plant life.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on three practical uses: rooting powder (auxin), seed germination (gibberellin), fruit ripening (ethene). Provide a simple matching activity.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Design a complete investigation to test the effect of light direction on seedling growth. Include hypotheses, variables, method, and how results will be analysed. Research the use of genetically modified crops that produce their own pest-killing hormones.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Application Match

Match each hormone to its use: auxin (rooting powder), gibberellin (seed germination), ethene (fruit ripening), synthetic auxin (weedkiller).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe practical uses of plant hormones and explain how they work
  • Getting there: Can name some uses but cannot explain the biological mechanism
  • Not yet: Cannot describe practical applications of plant hormones

Homework Suggestions

  • Investigate phototropism at home: place a plant in a dark room with one light source. Observe and sketch the direction of growth after one week
  • Research how auxins are used in commercial agriculture and write a paragraph explaining one use
  • Create a comparison table of auxins, gibberellins, and ethene with functions and practical applications
  • Investigate how ethene affects fruit ripening: place a banana in a paper bag with an apple. Observe what happens over several days
