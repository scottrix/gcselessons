B27: The Endocrine System

Foundation Higher All Boards

Learn how the endocrine system uses hormones to coordinate slower, longer-lasting responses, and compare hormonal and nervous control.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the role of the endocrine system in coordinating body functions
  • Name the main endocrine glands and the hormones they produce
  • Compare the nervous and endocrine systems
  • Explain the role of the pituitary gland as the "master gland"

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of homeostasis (B23)
  • Knowledge of the nervous system (B24)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Diagram of the endocrine system showing glands
  • Comparison table of nervous vs endocrine systems

Lesson 1: The Endocrine System and Hormones

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Two Speeds

Ask: "When you touch something hot, your hand moves away instantly. But when you start puberty, changes happen gradually over years. How does your body control both fast and slow responses?" Discuss that the body has two communication systems: nervous (fast) and endocrine (slow).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The endocrine system: a network of glands that produce and secrete hormones directly into the blood. Hormones: chemical messengers produced by endocrine glands. They travel in the blood to target organs where they cause a response. Key endocrine glands and their hormones:
  • Brain (hypothalamus): produces releasing hormones that control the pituitary gland. Links the nervous and endocrine systems.
  • Pituitary gland (brain): the "master gland." Produces many hormones that control other endocrine glands. Key hormones:
    • FSH (follicle stimulating hormone) — controls egg/sperm development
    • LH (luteinising hormone) — triggers ovulation and testosterone production
    • ADH (antidiuretic hormone) — controls water reabsorption in kidneys
    • Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) — controls the thyroid
    • Growth hormone — stimulates growth of bones and tissues
  • Thyroid (neck): produces thyroxine, which controls metabolic rate and regulates growth and development.
  • Pancreas: produces insulin (lowers blood glucose) and glucagon (raises blood glucose).
  • Adrenal glands (above kidneys): produce adrenaline (fight or flight response — increases heart rate, blood flow to muscles, blood glucose).
  • Ovaries (females): produce oestrogen and progesterone (控制 puberty, menstrual cycle, pregnancy).
  • Testes (males): produce testosterone (控制 puberty, sperm production, secondary sexual characteristics).
The hypothalamus monitors blood conditions and controls the pituitary, which in turn controls other glands. This creates a chain of hormonal control.
Worked Example

"Explain the role of the pituitary gland in the endocrine system." Answer: The pituitary gland is often called the "master gland" because it produces hormones that control many other endocrine glands. It receives signals from the hypothalamus about conditions in the body. In response, it secretes hormones that stimulate the thyroid, adrenal glands, ovaries/testes, and other glands to produce their hormones. This creates a hierarchical control system.

Common Misconception

"Hormones only affect reproductive functions"

Hormones control many body functions beyond reproduction: metabolism (thyroxine), blood glucose (insulin), water balance (ADH), growth (growth hormone), stress response (adrenaline), and more. The endocrine system is involved in virtually all homeostatic processes.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on four key glands: pituitary (master), thyroid (metabolism), pancreas (blood sugar), adrenal (adrenaline). Provide a matching activity: gland → hormone → function.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research negative feedback in the endocrine system: how does the hypothalamus-pituitary-thyroid axis work? Discuss what happens when glands malfunction (e.g. overactive thyroid).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Gland-Hormone Match

Match each gland to its hormone: pituitary (FSH), thyroid (thyroxine), pancreas (insulin), adrenal (adrenaline). Quick matching on mini-whiteboards.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can name the main endocrine glands and their hormones with functions
  • Getting there: Can name some glands but confuses hormones
  • Not yet: Cannot name endocrine glands or their hormones

Lesson 2: Nervous vs Endocrine Control

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Speed Comparison

Ask: "Which is faster — pulling your hand from a hot pan or starting puberty? Why the difference?" Discuss the different speeds of nervous and hormonal responses.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Comparison of nervous and endocrine systems:
FeatureNervous SystemEndocrine System
CommunicationElectrical impulsesChemical hormones in blood
SpeedVery fast (milliseconds)Slower (seconds to days)
DurationShort-livedLonger-lasting
TargetSpecific (one muscle/gland)Anywhere (hormones travel in blood)
ResponsePrecise and localisedWidespread and general
The two systems work together:
  • The hypothalamus links the two systems — it receives nerve impulses and responds by releasing hormones
  • Example: during exercise, the nervous system increases breathing rate immediately, while adrenaline from the adrenal glands increases heart rate and blood glucose over a slightly longer timescale
  • The adrenal medulla is controlled by the nervous system (nerve impulses trigger adrenaline release), while the adrenal cortex is controlled by hormones from the pituitary
Example of integrated control — blood glucose regulation:
  • Nervous system: detects low blood glucose → sends signals to the pancreas
  • Endocrine system: pancreas releases glucagon → liver converts glycogen to glucose → blood glucose rises
Worked Example

"Explain why the nervous system is better suited for the withdrawal reflex, while the endocrine system is better suited for puberty." Answer: The withdrawal reflex needs to be immediate (milliseconds) to prevent injury — the nervous system transmits electrical impulses very quickly to specific muscles. Puberty involves gradual changes over years (growth, development of sexual characteristics) — hormones are released slowly into the blood and cause long-lasting effects throughout the body.

Common Misconception

"The nervous and endocrine systems work independently"

The two systems are closely integrated. The hypothalamus links them — it receives nerve signals and triggers hormone release. Many processes involve both systems working together, such as the stress response (nervous system triggers adrenaline release from adrenal glands).

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Simplify: "Nervous = fast, electrical, short-lived. Endocrine = slow, chemical (hormones), long-lasting." Provide a comparison table template to complete.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research how the "fight or flight" response involves BOTH systems: nervous system activates the adrenal medulla to release adrenaline, which then causes hormonal effects (increased heart rate, blood glucose, etc.). Create a timeline showing how both systems contribute.

Plenary (5 minutes)

System Choice

Give 6 scenarios. Students decide: "Nervous system," "Endocrine system," or "Both." Discuss any tricky ones.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can compare the two systems in detail and explain how they work together
  • Getting there: Can describe the main differences but cannot explain integration
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between the nervous and endocrine systems

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a labelled diagram of the endocrine system showing the main glands and their hormones
  • Write a comparison of the nervous and endocrine systems in a table format
  • Research one hormone (e.g. insulin, adrenaline) and write a detailed profile: gland, target, function, and what happens if there's a problem
  • Explain how the body responds to seeing a spider (real or fake) using both nervous and endocrine systems
