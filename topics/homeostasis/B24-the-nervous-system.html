B24: The Nervous System

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand how the nervous system coordinates rapid responses through electrical impulses, reflex arcs, and synaptic transmission.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the structure and function of neurones (sensory, relay, motor)
  • Explain how electrical impulses travel along neurones
  • Describe the structure and function of synapses
  • Explain the reflex arc and why reflexes are important

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of receptors and coordination centres (B23)
  • Basic knowledge of cell structure (B1)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Diagram of a reflex arc
  • Diagram of a neurone
  • Synapse diagram template

Lesson 1: Neurones and Electrical Impulses

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Reaction Time

Play the ruler drop test: one student holds a ruler, another ready to catch it. Drop the ruler and measure the distance fallen. Ask: "What happened between seeing the ruler drop and catching it?" Discuss nerve impulses.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The nervous system allows rapid, short-lived responses to stimuli. Three types of neurones:
  • Sensory neurones: carry impulses from receptors (sense organs) to the CNS (brain/spinal cord). Cell body is located to the side of the axon. Dendrites are long.
  • Relay (intermediate) neurones: found in the CNS. Connect sensory and motor neurones. Process and integrate information.
  • Motor neurones: carry impulses from the CNS to effectors (muscles/glands). Cell body is at one end. Long axon with short dendrites.
Electrical impulses:
  • Impulses travel along neurones as electrical signals
  • The membrane of a neurone is polarised at rest: positive outside, negative inside (resting potential, about -70mV)
  • When a stimulus is detected, sodium ion channels open and Na⁺ ions rush into the cell → membrane becomes positive inside, negative outside (depolarisation)
  • This creates an action potential that triggers the next section of membrane to depolarise → the impulse travels along the neurone
  • After the impulse passes, potassium ion channels open and K⁺ ions leave → membrane repolarises back to resting potential
  • The impulse is always in one direction because the previous section is still repolarising (refractory period)
Myelin sheath:
  • Many neurones are wrapped in a fatty myelin sheath
  • Myelin insulates the axon and speeds up impulse transmission
  • Impulses jump between gaps in the myelin (Nodes of Ranvier) — this is called saltatory conduction
Worked Example

"Describe the three types of neurone and their functions." Answer: (1) Sensory neurones carry impulses from receptors to the CNS. (2) Relay neurones connect sensory and motor neurones within the CNS. (3) Motor neurones carry impulses from the CNS to effectors (muscles or glands).

Common Misconception

"Electrical impulses are the same as electricity in wires"

Nerve impulses involve the movement of ions (Na⁺, K⁺) across cell membranes, not electrons flowing through a conductor. The impulse is a wave of depolarisation that travels along the neurone, not an electrical current like in a wire.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on three types of neurones: sensory (receptors → CNS), relay (CNS connection), motor (CNS → muscles). Provide a diagram for labelling. Simplify impulse to "signal travels along nerve."
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the speed of impulse transmission in myelinated vs non-myelinated neurones. Why do some neurones have thicker axons? (Squid giant axon as an example.)

Plenary (5 minutes)

Three Types

Students draw a simple diagram showing the three types of neurone in order: sensory → relay → motor. Label each with its function.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can name and describe all three neurone types and explain impulse transmission
  • Getting there: Can name the neurone types but cannot explain impulse transmission
  • Not yet: Cannot name or distinguish between neurone types

Lesson 2: Synapses

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Gap Question

Ask: "Neurones don't actually touch each other. How does an impulse get from one neurone to the next?" Discuss that there is a small gap between neurones called a synapse.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Synapse: the junction between two neurones where the impulse is transmitted chemically. Structure and function:
  • The axon terminal of the sending (presynaptic) neurone contains small sacs called synaptic vesicles
  • These vesicles contain chemical messengers called neurotransmitters (e.g. acetylcholine)
  • When the electrical impulse reaches the axon terminal, calcium ion channels open → Ca²⁺ enters → vesicles fuse with the membrane → neurotransmitter is released into the synaptic cleft (the gap)
  • Neurotransmitter diffuses across the synaptic cleft (about 20-40nm wide)
  • It binds to specific receptor proteins on the membrane of the receiving (postsynaptic) neurone
  • This binding opens ion channels → ions flow in → new electrical impulse is generated in the postsynaptic neurone
  • The neurotransmitter is then broken down by enzymes to prevent continuous stimulation
Importance of synapses:
  • Allow impulses to be transmitted from one neurone to another
  • Allow integration of signals (many synapses converge on one neurone)
  • Allow the signal to be amplified or inhibited
  • Some drugs and poisons work by blocking or stimulating synapses (e.g. curare blocks acetylcholine receptors → paralysis)
Worked Example

"Describe how an impulse is transmitted across a synapse." Answer: (1) The electrical impulse arrives at the axon terminal. (2) Calcium ions enter the terminal. (3) Synaptic vesicles fuse with the membrane and release neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft. (4) Neurotransmitter diffuses across the gap. (5) It binds to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane. (6) Ion channels open, allowing ions to enter and generate a new impulse in the next neurone.

Common Misconception

"Impulses jump across the synapse electrically"

Impulses are transmitted ACROSS synapses chemically (using neurotransmitters), not electrically. The impulse is electrical along the neurone but chemical between neurones. This is why synapses are slower than impulse conduction along axons.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a numbered flow diagram of synaptic transmission. Simplify: "Signal reaches the end → chemical released → crosses gap → triggers next signal." Focus on the key concept: chemical, not electrical.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research how different drugs affect synapses: caffeine (blocks adenosine receptors), nicotine (mimics acetylcholine), alcohol (enhances GABA). Discuss why synapses are targets for pharmaceuticals.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Seven Steps

Students write the seven steps of synaptic transmission from memory. Compare with a partner and fill in gaps.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe synaptic transmission step by step with clear explanations
  • Getting there: Knows that chemicals are involved but cannot describe the full process
  • Not yet: Cannot describe what happens at a synapse

Lesson 3: The Reflex Arc

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Touch Hot

Ask: "If you touch a hot pan, you pull your hand away immediately — before you even feel pain. Why?" Discuss that this is a reflex — an automatic, rapid response that protects the body.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
A reflex arc is the pathway that a reflex impulse follows:
  1. Receptor: detects the stimulus (e.g. pain/heat receptors in the skin)
  2. Sensory neurone: carries the impulse from the receptor to the CNS (spinal cord)
  3. Synapse (in the CNS): the sensory neurone synapses with a relay neurone in the grey matter of the spinal cord
  4. Relay neurone: processes the signal within the CNS
  5. Synapse: the relay neurone synapses with a motor neurone
  6. Motor neurone: carries the impulse from the CNS to the effector
  7. Effector: a muscle that contracts to pull the hand away from the stimulus
Key points:
  • Reflexes are involuntary — they happen automatically without conscious thought
  • The impulse travels to the brain AFTER the reflex action — this is why you feel pain after pulling away
  • Reflexes are fast because they use a short pathway with few synapses
  • The brain can override some reflexes (e.g. holding a hot cup briefly) but not others (e.g. blinking)
Examples of reflexes:
  • Withdrawal reflex (touching something hot)
  • Knee-jerk reflex (patellar reflex)
  • Blinking reflex (something approaching the eye)
  • Coughing/sneezing reflex (irritants in the airway)
  • Pupil reflex (changing light intensity)
Worked Example

"Trace the pathway of a withdrawal reflex when you touch a hot pan." Answer: (1) Heat/pain receptors in fingertip detect the hot pan. (2) Sensory neurone carries impulse along the arm to the spinal cord. (3) Synapse with relay neurone in the spinal cord. (4) Relay neurone connects to motor neurone via another synapse. (5) Motor neurone carries impulse back to the arm muscles. (6) Arm muscles (effectors) contract, pulling the hand away. (7) The brain receives the signal and you feel pain (after the reflex).

Common Misconception

"The brain controls all reflexes"

Most reflexes are controlled by the spinal cord, not the brain. The spinal cord can process the reflex without input from the brain, making the response faster. The brain receives the information after the reflex has occurred, which is why you feel pain after pulling your hand away.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Provide a diagram of the reflex arc with blanks to fill in. Number the pathway and focus on the sequence: receptor → sensory → CNS → motor → effector. Simplify: "short cut through the spinal cord."
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Investigate the knee-jerk reflex using a reflex hammer. Research why some reflexes can be inhibited by the brain (e.g. holding a hot object) and others cannot (e.g. blinking). Discuss why this selective inhibition is important.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Pathway Quiz

Students draw the reflex arc pathway from memory: label receptor, sensory neurone, relay neurone, motor neurone, effector. Include synapses.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the full reflex arc pathway and explain why reflexes are fast and involuntary
  • Getting there: Can trace the pathway but cannot explain why reflexes are important
  • Not yet: Cannot describe the reflex arc pathway

Homework Suggestions

  • Draw and label a detailed diagram of the three types of neurone
  • Create a step-by-step flowchart of synaptic transmission
  • Test reaction times at home using the ruler drop test — repeat 10 times and calculate a mean
  • Research one reflex action (e.g. pupil reflex) and describe the pathway in detail
