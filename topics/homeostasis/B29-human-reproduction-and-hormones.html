B29: Human Reproduction and Hormones

Foundation Higher All Boards

Study the hormonal control of the menstrual cycle, the roles of FSH, LH, oestrogen, and progesterone, and how contraception uses hormones to prevent pregnancy.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the structure and function of the male and female reproductive systems
  • Explain the hormonal control of the menstrual cycle (FSH, LH, oestrogen, progesterone)
  • Describe how hormonal and non-hormonal contraception methods work
  • Understand how fertility treatments (e.g. IVF) work

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of hormones and the endocrine system (B27)
  • Knowledge of negative feedback (B23)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Menstrual cycle diagram template
  • Reproductive system diagrams

Lesson 1: Male and Female Reproductive Systems

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Key Question

Ask: "For reproduction to occur, what needs to happen at the most basic level?" (A sperm must fertilise an egg.) Discuss the organs involved.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Male reproductive system:
  • Testes: produce sperm (spermatogenesis) and the hormone testosterone
  • Scrotum: holds the testes outside the body at ~2°C below body temperature (optimal for sperm production)
  • Epididymis: where sperm mature and are stored
  • Vas deferens: tube carrying sperm from the epididymis to the urethra
  • Seminal vesicles and prostate gland: produce seminal fluid that nourishes and protects sperm, forming semen
  • Urethra: carries semen (and urine, but not at the same time) out through the penis
Female reproductive system:
  • Ovaries: produce eggs (oocytes) by oogenesis and the hormones oestrogen and progesterone
  • Fallopian tubes (oviducts): where fertilisation normally occurs. Cilia sweep the egg towards the uterus
  • Uterus (womb): where the embryo implants and the foetus develops. Lined with endometrium (mucus membrane) that is built up each month and shed during menstruation if no pregnancy occurs
  • Cervix: narrow opening of the uterus. Produces mucus that changes during the cycle to either allow or block sperm entry
  • Vagina: receives the penis during intercourse; passageway for menstrual flow and childbirth
Sperm production:
  • Occurs in the testes at a rate of about 200-300 million per day
  • Each sperm has: a head (containing DNA), a midpiece (containing mitochondria for energy), and a tail (flagellum for swimming)
  • Sperm are adapted for their function: streamlined shape, lots of mitochondria, long tail, acrosome with enzymes to penetrate the egg
Worked Example

"Describe three adaptations of a sperm cell for its function." Answer: (1) Streamlined head shape reduces resistance as it swims. (2) Long tail (flagellum) provides propulsion to swim towards the egg. (3) Midpiece contains many mitochondria to provide energy for swimming. (4) Acrosome contains enzymes to penetrate the egg's coating.

Common Misconception

"The testes produce sperm only at certain times"

Sperm are produced continuously from puberty throughout adulthood (spermatogenesis). About 200-300 million sperm are produced per day. The process takes about 64-72 days from start to mature sperm.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on the key organs and their main functions: testes (make sperm), ovaries (make eggs), uterus (baby grows here). Provide a diagram for labelling.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the hormonal control of spermatogenesis (testosterone, FSH, LH). Why is the scrotum outside the body? Investigate how sperm counts have changed over recent decades.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Organ Function

Match reproductive organs to their functions. Quick matching activity on mini-whiteboards.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the male and female reproductive systems with clear functions
  • Getting there: Can name the main organs but cannot describe their functions
  • Not yet: Cannot name the reproductive organs

Lesson 2: The Menstrual Cycle

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Cycle Question

Ask: "The menstrual cycle is about 28 days long. What happens during this cycle? Why does it repeat?" Discuss that it prepares the body for pregnancy each month.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The menstrual cycle (average 28 days, but 21-35 days is normal): Four key hormones and their roles:
HormoneProduced byRole
FSH (follicle stimulating hormone)Pituitary glandStimulates the development of an egg in the ovary. Stimulates oestrogen production.
OestrogenOvaries (developing egg)Causes the uterus lining to thicken. Stimulates LH release. Inhibits FSH release (negative feedback).
LH (luteinising hormone)Pituitary glandTriggers ovulation (release of the egg) around day 14.
ProgesteroneCorpus luteum (remains of egg sac after ovulation)Maintains the thick uterus lining. Inhibits FSH and LH release (negative feedback).
Phases of the cycle:
  1. Days 1-5 (Menstruation): the thickened uterus lining breaks down and is shed (menstrual blood). Progesterone levels are low.
  2. Days 1-13 ( follicular phase): FSH stimulates an egg to develop in the ovary. The developing egg produces oestrogen. Oestrogen causes the uterus lining to rebuild (proliferation). High oestrogen inhibits FSH (negative feedback) and stimulates LH release.
  3. Day 14 (Ovulation): a surge of LH triggers the release of the mature egg from the ovary. The egg travels into the fallopian tube.
  4. Days 15-28 (Luteal phase): the remains of the egg sac become the corpus luteum, which produces progesterone. Progesterone maintains the thick uterus lining, preparing for possible pregnancy. Progesterone inhibits FSH and LH (negative feedback).
  5. If no fertilisation: the corpus luteum breaks down after about 10 days → progesterone falls → uterus lining breaks down (menstruation) → FSH levels rise again → new cycle begins.
  6. If fertilisation occurs: the embryo implants in the uterus lining → it produces hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin) → maintains the corpus luteum → progesterone stays high → uterus lining maintained → pregnancy continues.
Worked Example

"Explain why progesterone levels drop if fertilisation does not occur." Answer: If no fertilisation occurs, the egg is not implanted in the uterus. The corpus luteum (remains of the egg sac) breaks down after about 10 days. Since the corpus luteum produces progesterone, its breakdown means progesterone levels fall. The drop in progesterone causes the uterus lining to break down (menstruation) and allows FSH levels to rise, starting a new cycle.

Common Misconception

"Ovulation always occurs on day 14"

Ovulation typically occurs around day 14 in a 28-day cycle, but the cycle length varies between individuals and from month to month. In shorter cycles, ovulation may occur earlier; in longer cycles, later. This variability is why the "rhythm method" of contraception is unreliable.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on the four hormones and their key roles. Simplify: "FSH makes eggs develop, oestrogen builds the lining, LH makes the egg release, progesterone maintains the lining." Provide a graph with hormones to trace.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Analyse hormone level graphs and predict what happens at different points. Discuss the feedback loops in detail. Research hormonal disorders affecting the menstrual cycle (e.g. PCOS, amenorrhoea).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Four Hormones

Students write the name and role of each hormone from memory: FSH, oestrogen, LH, progesterone. Compare with a partner.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe all four hormones and their roles, and explain the menstrual cycle phases
  • Getting there: Can name the hormones but cannot describe their roles accurately
  • Not yet: Cannot name the key hormones or describe the menstrual cycle

Lesson 3: Contraception and Fertility

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Question

Ask: "If you understand how the menstrual cycle works, how could you prevent pregnancy?" Discuss that contraception works by preventing ovulation, fertilisation, or implantation.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Methods of contraception: Hormonal methods (prevent ovulation or thicken cervical mucus):
  • Oral contraceptive pill: contains oestrogen and progesterone. High levels inhibit FSH and LH release (negative feedback) → no egg develops → no ovulation. Also thickens cervical mucus (blocks sperm).
  • Contraceptive injection: progesterone only. Given every 3 months. Prevents ovulation and thickens cervical mucus.
  • Contraceptive implant: small rod under the skin releasing progesterone. Lasts 3 years. Prevents ovulation.
  • Contraceptive patch: worn on the skin, releases oestrogen and progesterone. Changed weekly.
Non-hormonal methods (prevent sperm reaching the egg):
  • Barrier methods: condom (male/female), diaphragm. Physically block sperm from entering the uterus.
  • Intrauterine device (IUD): copper coil inserted into the uterus. Copper ions are toxic to sperm. Some IUDs also release hormones.
  • Sterilisation: vasectomy (male — cutting the vas deferens) or tubal ligation (female — blocking the fallopian tubes). Permanent.
  • Absence: not having sex during fertile period. Unreliable.
Fertility treatment:
  • IVF (in vitro fertilisation): eggs are collected from the ovaries and fertilised with sperm in the laboratory. The resulting embryo is placed into the uterus. Hormones are given to stimulate egg production.
  • IVF is used when natural conception is not possible (e.g. blocked fallopian tubes, low sperm count, unexplained infertility)
  • Success rate: about 25-30% per cycle (varies with age)
  • Emotional, physical, and financial costs
Worked Example

"Explain how the oral contraceptive pill prevents pregnancy." Answer: The pill contains synthetic oestrogen and progesterone. These hormones are detected by the hypothalamus and pituitary gland as being at high levels. Through negative feedback, the pituitary reduces production of FSH and LH. Without FSH, no egg develops in the ovary. Without the LH surge, ovulation does not occur. Additionally, the progesterone thickens the cervical mucus, making it harder for sperm to enter the uterus.

Common Misconception

"Contraceptive pills protect against STIs"

Hormonal contraceptive methods only prevent pregnancy — they do NOT protect against sexually transmitted infections (STIs). Only barrier methods (condoms) provide protection against both pregnancy and STIs.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Simplify: "Hormonal methods stop eggs being released. Barrier methods stop sperm reaching the egg." Provide a sorting activity: hormonal vs non-hormonal methods.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the effectiveness rates of different contraceptive methods. Discuss the ethical considerations of IVF, including the fate of unused embryos. Investigate new contraceptive technologies being developed.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Method Match

Match each contraceptive method to how it works: pill (stops ovulation), condom (barrier), sterilisation (permanent block), IUD (toxic to sperm).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe hormonal and non-hormonal contraception and explain how at least two methods work
  • Getting there: Can name contraception methods but cannot explain how they work
  • Not yet: Cannot describe contraception methods

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a labelled diagram of the male and female reproductive systems
  • Draw a graph of the four menstrual cycle hormones showing how levels change over 28 days
  • Research one method of contraception in detail and create an information card
  • Write a paragraph explaining how IVF works and discuss one advantage and one disadvantage
