B28: Control of Blood Glucose

Foundation Higher All Boards

Learn how the pancreas regulates blood glucose concentration through insulin and glucagon, and understand the causes and treatment of diabetes.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Explain how insulin and glucagon regulate blood glucose
  • Describe the role of the liver in glucose storage and release
  • Describe the causes and symptoms of Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes
  • Discuss treatments for diabetes including insulin injections and lifestyle changes

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of negative feedback (B23)
  • Knowledge of the pancreas as an endocrine gland (B27)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Negative feedback diagram template
  • Comparison table of Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes

Lesson 1: Blood Glucose Regulation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

After Lunch

Ask: "What happens to your blood sugar after you eat a meal? What happens if you skip lunch?" Discuss that blood glucose rises after eating and falls when you haven't eaten. The body must keep it within a narrow range.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Blood glucose regulation — two negative feedback loops: When blood glucose is TOO HIGH (after eating):
  1. High blood glucose detected by receptors in the pancreas (beta cells)
  2. Pancreas releases the hormone insulin into the blood
  3. Insulin causes body cells (especially liver and muscle cells) to take up more glucose from the blood
  4. In the liver, insulin stimulates the conversion of glucose to glycogen (a storage molecule) — this process is called glycogenesis
  5. Blood glucose level falls back to normal
  6. As blood glucose drops, insulin secretion reduces (negative feedback)
When blood glucose is TOO LOW (between meals or during exercise):
  1. Low blood glucose detected by receptors in the pancreas (alpha cells)
  2. Pancreas releases the hormone glucagon into the blood
  3. Glucagon causes the liver to convert stored glycogen back to glucose — this process is called glycogenolysis
  4. Glucose is released into the blood
  5. Blood glucose level rises back to normal
  6. As blood glucose rises, glucagon secretion reduces (negative feedback)
The liver is the key organ — it stores glucose as glycogen and releases it when needed.
Worked Example

"Describe the negative feedback loop that lowers blood glucose after a meal." Answer: (1) Blood glucose rises after food is absorbed. (2) Pancreas (beta cells) detects the rise. (3) Pancreas releases insulin into the blood. (4) Insulin causes liver and muscle cells to take up glucose. (5) In the liver, glucose is converted to glycogen (glycogenesis). (6) Blood glucose falls. (7) As glucose returns to normal, insulin secretion reduces.

Common Misconception

"Insulin lowers blood glucose by destroying it"

Insulin does not destroy glucose. It causes cells to take up glucose from the blood and the liver to convert it to glycogen for storage. The glucose is still there — it has just moved from the blood into cells where it can be used for respiration or stored.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Simplify: "High blood sugar → pancreas makes insulin → liver stores glucose as glycogen → blood sugar drops. Low blood sugar → pancreas makes glucagon → liver releases glucose → blood sugar rises." Provide a flow diagram template.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research how insulin was first extracted and used to treat diabetes. Discuss the role of adrenaline in raising blood glucose during exercise. Why is this useful?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Two Hormones

Students complete a table: Insulin (when released: high blood glucose; effect: lowers blood glucose; target: liver/muscles). Glucagon (when released: low blood glucose; effect: raises blood glucose; target: liver).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe both negative feedback loops for blood glucose with clear hormonal mechanisms
  • Getting there: Knows insulin lowers blood glucose but cannot describe the full loop
  • Not yet: Cannot describe how blood glucose is regulated

Lesson 2: Diabetes

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Question

Ask: "What would happen if the pancreas couldn't produce insulin? What problems would this cause?" Discuss that blood glucose would stay dangerously high.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Diabetes mellitus: a condition where the body cannot regulate blood glucose properly, leading to dangerously high levels (hyperglycaemia).
FeatureType 1 DiabetesType 2 Diabetes
CauseAutoimmune — immune system destroys beta cells in the pancreasBody cells become less responsive to insulin (insulin resistance). Pancreas may also produce less insulin over time.
Age of onsetUsually childhood/young adulthoodUsually adulthood (increasingly in younger people)
Insulin productionLittle or noneInitially normal or high, may decrease over time
Body typeOften slimOften overweight/obese
TreatmentInsulin injections (lifelong)Diet, exercise, weight loss, medication (metformin), sometimes insulin
PreventionCannot be preventedCan often be prevented or delayed by healthy lifestyle
Symptoms of diabetes (both types):
  • Frequent urination (glucose in urine draws water by osmosis — osmotic diuresis)
  • Excessive thirst (due to water loss)
  • Weight loss (cells cannot take up glucose, so body breaks down fat and protein for energy)
  • Fatigue (cells cannot respire properly without glucose)
  • Blurred vision (lens swells due to osmotic effects of high glucose)
Complications of poorly controlled diabetes:
  • Damage to blood vessels (atherosclerosis) → heart disease, stroke
  • Damage to small blood vessels → kidney failure, eye damage (retinopathy), nerve damage (neuropathy)
  • Increased risk of infections (high glucose feeds bacteria)
Treatment:
  • Type 1: regular insulin injections (or insulin pump), blood glucose monitoring, controlled diet
  • Type 2: lifestyle changes (diet, exercise, weight loss), oral medication (metformin), insulin if needed
  • Both types: regular monitoring of blood glucose, HbA1c tests to assess long-term control
Worked Example

"Explain why a person with Type 1 diabetes needs insulin injections but a person with Type 2 diabetes may not." Answer: In Type 1 diabetes, the immune system has destroyed the beta cells in the pancreas, so no insulin is produced. Without insulin, blood glucose cannot be lowered. Insulin injections replace the missing hormone. In Type 2 diabetes, the body still produces insulin, but cells are less responsive to it. Initially, lifestyle changes (diet, exercise) can help cells respond better to insulin, so injections may not be needed.

Common Misconception

"Type 2 diabetes only affects overweight people"

While being overweight is a major risk factor for Type 2 diabetes, it is not the only cause. Genetics, age, ethnicity, and other factors play a role. Some people of normal weight develop Type 2 diabetes. However, maintaining a healthy weight significantly reduces the risk.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on the key differences: Type 1 = no insulin (need injections), Type 2 = insulin doesn't work well (lifestyle changes). Provide a simple comparison table.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research how insulin pumps and continuous glucose monitors work. Discuss the social and psychological impact of living with diabetes. Investigate new treatments: islet cell transplants, artificial pancreas systems.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Type 1 or Type 2?

Give five case studies. Students classify each as Type 1 or Type 2 and explain their reasoning.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can compare Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes in detail and explain treatments
  • Getting there: Can describe the basic difference but struggles with treatments and complications
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a detailed comparison table of Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes
  • Research the discovery of insulin by Banting and Best — write a short biography
  • Design a healthy meal plan for a person with Type 2 diabetes, explaining why each food choice is appropriate
  • Investigate how blood glucose monitoring devices work and write a user guide
