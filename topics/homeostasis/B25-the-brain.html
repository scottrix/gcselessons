B25: The Brain

Higher All Boards

Learn about the structure of the brain, including the cerebrum, cerebellum, and medulla oblongata, and how brain damage affects function.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Higher only
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the structure and function of the cerebrum, cerebellum, and medulla oblongata
  • Explain how different parts of the brain control different functions
  • Describe the effects of brain damage on different areas
  • Understand how the brain is protected

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of neurones and the nervous system (B24)
  • Basic knowledge of the CNS

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Diagram of the human brain with labelled regions
  • Brain model (if available) or 3D images
  • Case study cards for brain damage effects

Lesson 1: Structure and Function of the Brain

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Brain Quiz

How much does the human brain weigh? (About 1.4 kg.) How many neurones does it contain? (About 86 billion.) How much of the body's energy does it use? (About 20%.) Discuss how remarkable the brain is.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The brain is the main organ of the CNS, protected by the skull (cranium). Three main regions:
  • Cerebrum (cerebral cortex): the largest part of the brain. Divided into two hemispheres. Responsible for:
    • Consciousness and thought
    • Intelligence, learning, and memory
    • Language
    • Sensory processing (interpreting what you see, hear, feel)
    • Voluntary muscle movement (initiating actions)
    • Emotions and personality
    The cerebrum has specialised regions: motor cortex (controls movement), sensory cortex (processes sensory information), Broca's area (speech production), Wernicke's area (language comprehension).
  • Cerebellum: located at the back of the brain, below the cerebrum. Responsible for:
    • Coordination of voluntary movements
    • Balance and posture
    • Muscle memory (learning physical skills like riding a bike)
  • Medulla oblongata (brainstem): the lowest part of the brain, connecting to the spinal cord. Responsible for:
    • Involuntary (autonomic) functions: breathing rate, heart rate, blood pressure
    • Reflexes: coughing, sneezing, swallowing, vomiting
    This is why damage to the medulla is often fatal — it controls life-sustaining functions.
Protection of the brain:
  • Skull (cranium) — bone
  • Membranes: dura mater (outer), arachnoid mater (middle), pia mater (inner)
  • Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) — cushions the brain, circulates through ventricles
  • Blood-brain barrier — selective barrier that prevents toxins from entering brain tissue
Worked Example

"Explain why damage to the medulla oblongata is more dangerous than damage to the cerebellum." Answer: The medulla controls involuntary, life-sustaining functions such as breathing and heart rate. Damage to the medulla can stop these functions, leading to death. The cerebellum controls balance and coordination — damage causes problems with movement and balance but is not immediately life-threatening.

Common Misconception

"The left brain is logical and the right brain is creative"

While the two hemispheres have some specialisation, the idea that people are either "left-brained" (logical) or "right-brained" (creative) is a myth. Both hemispheres work together for most tasks, and most functions involve both sides of the brain.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on three regions with simple functions: cerebrum (thinking), cerebellum (balance), medulla (breathing/heartbeat). Provide a diagram with three labels to learn.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the case of Phineas Gage — what happened to his personality after brain damage? Discuss neuroplasticity — can the brain recover from damage?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Three Regions

Students write the name and function of each brain region from memory: cerebrum, cerebellum, medulla. Compare with a partner.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe all three brain regions with clear functions and explain consequences of damage
  • Getting there: Can name the regions but cannot describe functions accurately
  • Not yet: Cannot name or describe the brain regions

Lesson 2: Brain Scanning and Disorders

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Scanning Technology

Show a brain scan (MRI or CT). Ask: "How do scientists know which part of the brain does what?" Discuss brain scanning technologies.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Brain scanning methods:
  • CT (computerised tomography) scan: uses X-rays to create cross-sectional images. Good for detecting bleeds, tumours, and fractures. Quick but uses ionising radiation.
  • MRI (magnetic resonance imaging): uses strong magnetic fields and radio waves. Produces detailed images of soft tissue. No ionising radiation. Better for studying brain structure.
  • PET (positron emission tomography) scan: uses a radioactive tracer to show brain activity. Areas with more activity take up more tracer and appear brighter. Shows which parts of the brain are active during different tasks.
  • EEG (electroencephalogram): records electrical activity from the scalp. Used to diagnose epilepsy and study sleep patterns. Lower resolution than other scans.
Brain disorders:
  • Alzheimer's disease: progressive loss of brain function. Brain cells die and connections are lost. Memory loss, confusion, difficulty with everyday tasks. Cause: not fully understood, but involves build-up of amyloid plaques and tau tangles.
  • Parkinson's disease: loss of dopamine-producing neurones in the substantia nigra. Causes tremors, stiffness, slow movement, and difficulty with balance. Treatment: L-DOPA (a dopamine precursor) can help replace lost dopamine.
  • Epilepsy: abnormal electrical activity in the brain causing seizures. Can be treated with anti-epileptic drugs that reduce the tendency of neurones to fire excessively.
  • Brain tumours: abnormal cell growth in the brain. Can damage surrounding tissue by compression. Treatment: surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy.
Worked Example

"Suggest why a PET scan would be more useful than a CT scan for studying which part of the brain controls language." Answer: A PET scan shows brain activity by tracking glucose use or radioactive tracers — it can show which areas are active during a language task. A CT scan only shows structure (physical anatomy) and would not reveal which areas are being used during the task.

Common Misconception

"Brain scans show exactly what each brain cell is doing"

Brain scans show activity or structure at a relatively coarse level. PET scans show which large regions are active; MRI shows detailed structure. They cannot show individual neurone activity — that requires much more specialised techniques used in research.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on two scanning methods: CT (structure, uses X-rays) and PET (activity, shows which parts are working). Simple comparison.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research how deep brain stimulation is used to treat Parkinson's disease. Discuss the ethical issues of brain scanning and privacy.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Scan Match

Match each scan type to its use: CT (detecting bleeds), MRI (detailed structure), PET (brain activity), EEG (detecting epilepsy).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe brain scanning methods and explain which is best for different purposes
  • Getting there: Can name scanning methods but cannot explain their uses
  • Not yet: Cannot describe brain scanning methods

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a labelled diagram of the brain showing the cerebrum, cerebellum, and medulla with their functions
  • Research one brain disorder and write a fact sheet: causes, symptoms, treatments, and prognosis
  • Compare CT and MRI scans: what are the advantages and disadvantages of each?
  • Investigate how the brain changes during adolescence — why do teenagers take more risks?
