B26: The Eye

Foundation Higher All Boards

Study the structure of the human eye, how images are formed on the retina, and how the eye accommodates to focus on objects at different distances.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the structure of the eye and the function of each part
  • Explain how the eye focuses on near and distant objects (accommodation)
  • Describe common defects of vision and their correction
  • Understand how the iris controls the amount of light entering the eye

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of light and reflection
  • Knowledge of the nervous system (B24)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Diagram of the human eye
  • Convex and concave lenses (if available)
  • Diagram of myopia and hyperopia correction

Lesson 1: Structure and Function of the Eye

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Cover One Eye

Ask students to cover one eye and try to thread a piece of string through a needle (or just judge distance). Then uncover the eye. Ask: "Why was it harder with one eye?" Discuss depth perception and introduce the eye as a complex organ.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Structure of the eye — key parts and their functions:
  • Cornea: transparent front surface. Refracts (bends) light rays as they enter the eye. Responsible for most of the focusing.
  • Iris: coloured part of the eye. Contains muscles that control the size of the pupil. In bright light, the iris constricts the pupil (less light enters). In dim light, it dilates the pupil (more light enters).
  • Pupil: the hole in the centre of the iris through which light enters.
  • Lens: transparent, flexible structure behind the iris. Fine-tunes the focus by changing shape (accommodation). Focuses light onto the retina.
  • Ciliary muscles: muscles attached to the lens by suspensory ligaments. Control the shape of the lens.
  • Suspensory ligaments: connect the ciliary muscles to the lens. Hold the lens in place and transfer tension.
  • Retina: light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye. Contains photoreceptor cells:
    • Rods: detect light intensity (black and white vision). More sensitive than cones. Found mainly in the peripheral retina. Active in dim light.
    • Cones: detect colour. Three types (red, green, blue). Concentrated in the fovea. Need bright light to work. Responsible for detailed, colour vision.
  • Fovea: central point of the retina with the highest concentration of cones. Provides the sharpest vision.
  • Optic nerve: carries electrical impulses from the retina to the brain (visual cortex).
  • Sclera: the white, tough outer layer. Protects the eye.
  • Choroid: dark layer between sclera and retina. Absorbs light to prevent reflection inside the eye.
Worked Example

"Explain why you can see better in colour in bright light than in dim light." Answer: In bright light, the cones in the retina are activated, allowing colour vision. In dim light, only the rods are sensitive enough to detect light, and rods only detect light intensity (black and white), not colour. Cones require more light to function.

Common Misconception

"The lens does most of the focusing in the eye"

The cornea does most of the focusing (about 2/3 of the refractive power). The lens fine-tunes the focus by changing shape (accommodation). This is why laser eye surgery reshapes the cornea, not the lens.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on five key parts: cornea (bends light), iris (controls pupil), lens (fine focus), retina (detects light), optic nerve (sends to brain). Provide a diagram with these five labels.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research how the eye adapts to darkness (dark adaptation — rods become more sensitive over 20-30 minutes). Discuss why fovea has no rods and what this means for peripheral vision.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Structure Label

Show an unlabeled eye diagram. Students label as many parts as they can from memory in two minutes.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe all key parts of the eye with their functions
  • Getting there: Can name some parts but confuses functions
  • Not yet: Cannot identify the main parts of the eye

Lesson 2: Accommodation and Defects of Vision

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Focus Switch

Ask students to look at something far away, then quickly at something close (e.g. their finger). Ask: "How did your eye change focus?" Discuss accommodation.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Accommodation: the process by which the lens changes shape to focus on objects at different distances.
  • Focusing on distant objects: ciliary muscles relax → suspensory ligaments are taut (tight) → lens is pulled flat (thin/less curved) → less refraction → image focuses on the retina.
  • Focusing on near objects: ciliary muscles contract → suspensory ligaments become slack → elastic lens becomes rounder (more curved) → more refraction → image focuses on the retina.
Defects of vision:
  • Myopia (short-sightedness): can see near objects clearly but distant objects are blurry. The eyeball is too long or the lens is too curved, so the image focuses in FRONT of the retina. Corrected with a concave (diverging) lens.
  • Hyperopia (long-sightedness): can see distant objects clearly but near objects are blurry. The eyeball is too short or the lens is too flat, so the image focuses BEHIND the retina. Corrected with a convex (converging) lens.
  • Presbyopia (age-related): the lens loses elasticity with age and cannot change shape to focus on near objects. Corrected with convex lenses (reading glasses).
The iris reflex:
  • Bright light → photoreceptors in retina → optic nerve → brain → parasympathetic nerve → iris contracts (pupil constricts) → less light enters → protects the retina
  • Dim light → photoreceptors → optic nerve → brain → sympathetic nerve → iris dilates (pupil widens) → more light enters → improves vision in the dark
Worked Example

"A student can read the board clearly but struggles to read the textbook on their desk. Explain the defect and how it can be corrected." Answer: This is hyperopia (long-sightedness). The lens is too flat or the eyeball is too short, so light from near objects focuses behind the retina. The student needs convex (converging) lenses to bend the light more, bringing the focus forward onto the retina.

Common Misconception

"Myopia means you can't see up close"

Myopia (short-sightedness) means you CAN see near objects clearly but distant objects are blurry. The name is confusing: "short-sighted" means you can only see SHORT distances clearly. Hyperopia (long-sightedness) is the opposite — you can see far but not near.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Simplify: "Myopia = can't see far → concave lens. Hyperopia = can't see near → convex lens." Provide a simple table of the two defects with images showing where the image focuses.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research laser eye surgery (LASIK) — how does it correct myopia? What are the risks? Discuss why some people choose contact lenses over glasses.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Defect Diagnosis

Give four scenarios (reading board blurry, reading book blurry, both blurry, etc.). Students diagnose the defect and prescribe the correction. Quick-fire round.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain accommodation and correct both types of vision defects with clear reasoning
  • Getting there: Can describe accommodation but confuses myopia and hyperopia
  • Not yet: Cannot explain accommodation or distinguish between vision defects

Homework Suggestions

  • Draw and label a diagram of the eye showing all key structures
  • Write a comparison of myopia and hyperopia: causes, symptoms, and corrections
  • Research the difference between rods and cones and create a comparison table
  • Investigate why people need reading glasses as they age — explain the biology behind presbyopia
