Lesson 2: Core Concepts: school processes & the hidden curriculum

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on school processes & the hidden curriculum. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Labelling: teachers attach labels to students based on assumptions about ability, behaviour and background. Becker: teachers have an 'ideal pupil' image (middle-class, well-behaved, motivated) and judge students against it. Raynor: teachers labelled students as 'bright' or 'difficult' based on appearance and behaviour, not ability. Labels become self-fulfilling prophecies (Rosenthal & Jacobson: Pygmalion in the Classroom).
Key Fact: Streaming and setting: ability grouping within schools. Streaming: broad ability groups across all subjects. Setting: ability groups within individual subjects. Ball found that working-class and ethnic minority students are over-represented in lower streams. Once placed in a lower stream, it is very difficult to move up - streams become self-perpetuating.
Key Fact: Pupil subcultures: pro-school subculture (accept school values, work hard, aim for qualifications - typically in higher streams) and anti-school subculture (reject school values, disrupt lessons, seek status through deviance - typically in lower streams). Hargreaves: streaming creates anti-school subcultures because lower-stream students suffer a loss of self-esteem and seek status through alternative means.
Key Fact: Lacey's concepts: differentiation (teachers label and categorise students as 'bright' or 'thick') and polarisation (students respond by moving towards pro-school or anti-school subcultures). Woods identified more responses: ingratiation (trying to please teachers), ritualism (going through the motions), retreatism (withdrawing), rebellion (active opposition), and colonisation (minimum effort to avoid trouble).
Key Fact: The hidden curriculum reinforces inequality: it teaches compliance and obedience (particularly to working-class students who will fill low-status jobs), competition (which benefits those who are already advantaged), and gender/ethnic stereotypes through curriculum content and teacher expectations.
Sociology Exam Tips: When discussing school processes, always: show the chain from labelling to achievement outcomes, name specific studies (Becker, Hargreaves, Lacey, Ball), and evaluate whether school processes create inequality or merely reflect external differences.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Explain how labelling and streaming can affect students' educational achievement.

Answer: Labelling: teachers label students based on class/gender/ethnicity (Becker: ideal pupil is middle-class). Labels become self-fulfilling prophecies (Rosenthal & Jacobson). Streaming: working-class and ethnic minority students are placed in lower streams (Ball). Lower streams = less qualified teachers, less challenging work, lower expectations. The cycle: label -> lower stream -> low self-esteem -> anti-school subculture -> underachievement.

Lesson 3: Application: school processes & the hidden curriculum

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Sociology Exam Tips. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain how labelling and streaming can affect students' educational achievement.

Answer: Labelling: teachers label students based on class/gender/ethnicity (Becker: ideal pupil is middle-class). Labels become self-fulfilling prophecies (Rosenthal & Jacobson). Streaming: working-class and ethnic minority students are placed in lower streams (Ball). Lower streams = less qualified teachers, less challenging work, lower expectations. The cycle: label -> lower stream -> low self-esteem -> anti-school subculture -> underachievement.

Q2: Describe how pupil subcultures develop in response to streaming, using Hargreaves' and Lacey's research.

Answer: Hargreaves: students in lower streams suffer status frustration (loss of self-esteem from being labelled failures) and form anti-school subcultures to gain status through non-academic means (troublemaking, rejecting school values). Lacey: differentiation (labelling by ability) leads to polarisation (students move to pro- or anti-school extremes). This explains why streaming WIDENS the achievement gap rather than narrowing it.

Q3: Evaluate whether school processes are more important than external factors in explaining educational inequality.

Answer: School processes (labelling, streaming, subcultures) are important because they actively create inequality during schooling. External factors (poverty, cultural capital) set the starting point but internal factors determine the trajectory. However: external factors create the conditions for labelling (middle-class children are labelled positively because they fit the ideal pupil); school processes interact with external factors; and some students resist labels. Conclusion: both are important, but internal factors are more significant because they represent missed opportunities - schools COULD reduce inequality but instead often reproduce it.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: school processes & the hidden curriculum

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Evaluate the view that school processes are more important than home background in explaining working-class underachievement. <div class="

A grade 9 response will: describe home background factors (material deprivation, cultural capital, Bernstein's codes); describe school processes (labelling, streaming, self-fulfilling prophecy, subcultures); argue school processes are more important (they actively CREATE inequality during school - even working-class children who start well are pulled down by labelling); argue home background matters (sets the starting point, affects teacher perceptions); conclude: both interact - home background creates conditions for labelling, but school processes determine whether the gap narrows or widens. Schools have the power to reduce inequality through anti-labelling policies and mixed-ability teaching, making internal factors more significant as a potential lever for change.

Exam Tips: Labelling -> self-fulfilling prophecy -> streaming -> status frustration -> anti-school subculture -> underachievement. This is the PROCESS of inequality. | Pro-school subculture = accept school values (higher streams). Anti-school subculture = reject school values (lower streams). Hargreaves and Lacey. | Differentiation (label by ability) + Polarisation (move to extremes) = streaming widens the gap.
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often make mistakes here. Wrong: Streaming helps all students because they learn at their own pace with students of similar ability. Correct: While streaming may seem logical, research shows it WIDENS inequality: working-class and ethnic minority students are disproportionately placed in lower streams (Ball); lower-stream students receive less challenging work and lower expectations; streaming creates anti-school subcultures (Hargreaves); students rarely move between streams, making placement self-perpetuating. Mixed-ability teaching with differentiated support is a fairer alternative because it avoids the self-fulfilling prophecy of labelling and streaming.
