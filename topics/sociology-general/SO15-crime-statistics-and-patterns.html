Lesson 2: Core Concepts: crime statistics & patterns

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on crime statistics & patterns. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Official statistics: recorded by the police and published by the Home Office. They show that working-class people, men, young people and some ethnic minorities are over-represented. However, official statistics have major limitations: the 'dark figure of crime' (unreported and unrecorded crime) means they significantly underestimate true crime rates.
Key Fact: The dark figure of crime: only about 40% of crime is reported to the police. Reasons: too trivial, police can't do anything, fear of retaliation, embarrassment. Of reported crime, only about 30% is recorded. The real crime rate is much higher than official statistics suggest.
Key Fact: Self-report studies: anonymous surveys asking people what crimes they have committed. Show that middle-class people commit more crime than official statistics suggest, especially white-collar crime. These studies challenge the class bias in official statistics.
Key Fact: Gender and crime: men commit about 80% of recorded crime. Heidensohn: women are controlled at home, in public, and at work. Chesney-Lind: women's crime is often a response to abuse. The gender gap is narrowing (female crime rates are rising).
Key Fact: Ethnicity and crime: Black people are over-represented in prison (3% of population, 12% of prison). However: this reflects selective policing (stop and search), socio-economic factors, and labelling rather than higher criminality.
Sociology Exam Tips: When discussing crime statistics, always: question the accuracy of official data, compare with self-report and victim surveys, and explain how bias in the CJS distorts patterns.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Explain why official statistics may not provide an accurate picture of crime.

Answer: Official statistics underestimate crime because: most crime is not reported (dark figure); police do not record all reported crime. The CSEW suggests real crime is about twice the official figure. Self-report studies show middle-class crime is underrepresented.

Lesson 3: Application: crime statistics & patterns

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Sociology Exam Tips, UK Context, UK Context. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain why official statistics may not provide an accurate picture of crime.

Answer: Official statistics underestimate crime because: most crime is not reported (dark figure); police do not record all reported crime. The CSEW suggests real crime is about twice the official figure. Self-report studies show middle-class crime is underrepresented.

Q2: Evaluate explanations for why men commit more crime than women.

Answer: Men commit 80% of recorded crime. Heidensohn: patriarchal control restricts women's opportunities. Chesney-Lind: women's crime often responds to abuse. However: the gap is narrowing; female crime is rising; biological explanations (testosterone) are reductionist.

Q3: Assess whether ethnic differences in crime statistics reflect real differences in criminality.

Answer: Black people are over-represented in official statistics but this reflects: selective policing (stop and search 9x higher for Black people), labelling, socio-economic factors. Self-report studies show similar offending across ethnic groups. Conclusion: ethnic differences reflect bias in the CJS, not higher criminality.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: crime statistics & patterns

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Evaluate the usefulness of official statistics for understanding patterns of crime. <div class="

A grade 9 response will: describe official statistics and their strengths (comprehensive, regularly collected); explain limitations (dark figure, selective reporting, recording practices); compare with alternatives (self-report, victim surveys); evaluate bias; conclude: official statistics are useful for broad patterns but significantly distort the true picture. Triangulation using multiple sources provides a more accurate understanding.

Exam Tips: Official statistics: 40% reported, 30% recorded. DARK FIGURE means real crime is much higher. | Gender: men = 80% of crime. Heidensohn: patriarchal CONTROL. Gap is NARROWING. | Ethnicity: over-representation in stats reflects POLICING BIAS not higher criminality.
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often make mistakes here. Wrong: Official crime statistics accurately reflect who commits the most crime. Correct: Official statistics significantly distort the true picture: the dark figure means most crime is unreported; white-collar crime is massively under-represented; police selectively target working-class and ethnic minority communities. Statistics reflect policing priorities and biases, not actual criminality.
