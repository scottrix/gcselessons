Lesson 2: Core Concepts: key concepts in sociology

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on key concepts in sociology. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Socialisation is the process through which individuals learn the norms, values and culture of their society. Primary socialisation (0-5 years): family teaches basic norms, language, and values. Secondary socialisation (5+ years): school, peers, media and religion reinforce and extend social learning.
Key Fact: Norms are social rules that guide behaviour (e.g. queuing, shaking hands). Values are broad beliefs about what is good and desirable (e.g. democracy, equality, achievement). Norms are specific; values are general. Norms enforce values through sanctions (positive: praise; negative: disapproval).
Key Fact: Status: a social position within a group. Ascribed status: given at birth (gender, ethnicity, royal family). Achieved status: earned through effort (qualifications, career). Role: the behaviour expected of someone with a particular status (teachers are expected to teach, mark work, and maintain discipline).
Key Fact: Culture: the way of life of a particular society, including norms, values, language, beliefs and material objects. Subculture: a smaller group within society with distinct norms and values (e.g. youth subcultures, religious groups). Cultural diversity: different cultures coexisting within one society.
Key Fact: Identity: how we see ourselves and how others see us. Social identity: based on group membership (gender, ethnicity, class). Personal identity: based on individual characteristics. Identity is shaped by socialisation, social structures and personal agency.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Explain the difference between primary and secondary socialisation, with examples.

Answer: Primary socialisation: family teaches basic norms (saying please/thank you), language, right/wrong, and core values (honesty, respect). Secondary socialisation: school teaches punctuality, obedience and competition (the hidden curriculum); peers teach group norms and identity; media teaches values and consumer behaviour; religion teaches moral codes.

Lesson 3: Application: key concepts in sociology

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: key terms from key concepts in sociology. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Explain the difference between primary and secondary socialisation, with examples.

Answer: Primary socialisation: family teaches basic norms (saying please/thank you), language, right/wrong, and core values (honesty, respect). Secondary socialisation: school teaches punctuality, obedience and competition (the hidden curriculum); peers teach group norms and identity; media teaches values and consumer behaviour; religion teaches moral codes.

Q2: Describe the relationship between norms, values and sanctions.

Answer: Values are broad beliefs (e.g. 'education is important'). Norms are specific rules that put values into practice (e.g. 'attend school every day', 'complete homework'). Sanctions enforce norms: positive sanctions reward conformity (praise for good work), negative sanctions punish deviance (detention for being late). Formal sanctions are official (laws, school rules); informal sanctions are unofficial (gossip, dirty looks).

Q3: Evaluate whether socialisation determines our identity or whether we have free choice.

Answer: Socialisation shapes identity: gender socialisation teaches 'masculine' and 'feminine' behaviour; class socialisation teaches aspirations and tastes; ethnic socialisation teaches cultural traditions. However, individuals also have agency: they can accept, modify or reject socialised norms (e.g. choosing non-traditional gender roles). Evaluation: socialisation is powerful but not deterministic - people resist and reinterpret social messages.

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Evaluate the importance of socialisation in shaping human behaviour. <div class="

A grade 9 response will: define socialisation and distinguish primary from secondary; describe key agents (family, school, peers, media) with examples of norms/values learned; argue socialisation is crucial (feral children show what happens without it); argue agency matters (people resist and reinterpret socialisation); conclude: socialisation provides the framework within which identity develops, but individuals actively construct their identity within this framework. Neither socialisation alone nor individual choice alone explains human behaviour.

Exam Tips: Primary = family (0-5). Secondary = school, peers, media (5+). Both shape identity. | Values = broad beliefs. Norms = specific rules. Sanctions = rewards/punishments that enforce norms. | Ascribed status = given (birth). Achieved status = earned (effort). Modern societies value achieved status.
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often make mistakes here. Wrong: Socialisation completely determines our behaviour, leaving no room for free will. Correct: While socialisation strongly influences behaviour, individuals are not passive recipients. People can: resist socialisation (rebellious teenagers reject parental values), reinterpret norms (feminists challenge traditional gender roles), and choose between competing social messages (different agents teach different values). The structure-agency debate shows that socialisation sets patterns but individuals have the capacity to make choices within those constraints.
