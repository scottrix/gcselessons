Lesson 2: Core Concepts: family roles & relationships

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on family roles & relationships. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Conjugal roles: the roles of husbands and wives within a family. Segregated conjugal roles (Bott): clear division between male (breadwinner) and female (homemaker) roles with separate leisure activities. Joint conjugal roles: couples share tasks and leisure.
Key Fact: Young and Willmott's symmetrical family (1973): the family is becoming more symmetrical - couples share domestic work, decision-making and leisure. They identified three stages: pre-industrial (unit of production), early industrial (segregated roles), symmetrical (joint roles). This change is driven by women working, geographical mobility, and new technology.
Key Fact: Feminist challenge: Oakley (1974) argued the symmetrical family is a myth. Her research found: 72% of husbands had 'no regular domestic duties'; housework and childcare remain overwhelmingly done by women; men may do more visible tasks (DIY, driving) but not routine daily work. The British Social Attitudes survey (2018) shows women still do twice as much housework as men.
Key Fact: The impact of women working: dual-earner families are now the norm (72% of women work). This has increased women's financial independence and bargaining power in the home. However, working women still do most housework - Ferri and Smith called this the 'dual burden' (paid work plus unpaid housework).
Key Fact: Decision-making and money: Pahl found that in dual-earner couples, money management is more equal. However, Vogler found women still manage day-to-day spending while men control major financial decisions. This suggests progress is incomplete - public equality has not translated into full domestic equality.
Sociology Exam Tips: When evaluating family roles, always use specific evidence: statistics on housework time, named studies (Oakley, Young & Willmott, Pahl), and consider whether self-reported equality matches actual behaviour.

Practice (10 minutes)

Q: Describe Young and Willmott's symmetrical family and evaluate whether it accurately represents modern family life.

Answer: Young and Willmott: the family is becoming more equal - shared tasks, leisure and decision-making. Three stages led to symmetry: industrialisation, women working, technology. Evaluation: Oakley criticised their methodology (one question about housework was inadequate); women still do most routine housework; the BSA (2018) shows women do twice as much; 'symmetry' may reflect ideology rather than reality.

Lesson 3: Application: family roles & relationships

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Recall the key terms: Sociology Exam Tips, UK Context, UK Context. Define each in one sentence.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Parent/Teacher Guide: Let your student attempt each question alone first, then compare with the model answer. Award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

Q1: Describe Young and Willmott's symmetrical family and evaluate whether it accurately represents modern family life.

Answer: Young and Willmott: the family is becoming more equal - shared tasks, leisure and decision-making. Three stages led to symmetry: industrialisation, women working, technology. Evaluation: Oakley criticised their methodology (one question about housework was inadequate); women still do most routine housework; the BSA (2018) shows women do twice as much; 'symmetry' may reflect ideology rather than reality.

Q2: Explain how feminism has challenged traditional family roles, using evidence.

Answer: Feminism challenged: the housewife role is socially constructed, not natural (Oakley); the family exploits women through unpaid labour (Delphy and Leonard); domestic violence is hidden behind 'private' family walls. Evidence: women do 60%+ of housework even when working full-time; the gender pay gap means women often earn less, reinforcing economic dependence.

Q3: Evaluate whether conjugal roles have become more equal in modern families.

Answer: Evidence for greater equality: more dual-earner couples, men do more childcare than in the 1950s, some couples share tasks equally. Evidence against: women still do majority of housework, the dual burden, men control major decisions, commercialisation of housework (ready meals) masks ongoing inequality. Conclusion: roles have become more equal but full equality has not been achieved - progress is incomplete.

Lesson 4: Exam Practice: family roles & relationships

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Command Words

Review what these command words require: state (one point), describe (say what happens), explain (say why), compare (both sides), evaluate (judgement).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Evaluate the view that conjugal roles have become more equal in modern society. <div class="

A grade 9 response will: describe Young and Willmott's symmetrical family; present Oakley's critique with evidence; discuss the impact of women's employment (dual burden); evaluate using recent evidence (BSA statistics); consider decision-making and money management; conclude: conjugal roles have become more equal than in the 1950s but full equality has not been achieved. Progress is uneven - some couples are more equal than others, and structural factors (gender pay gap, workplace culture) continue to disadvantage women in both public and private spheres.

Exam Tips: Young and Willmott: roles becoming SYMMETRICAL (equal). Oakley: this is a MYTH - women still do most housework. | Dual burden = paid work + housework. Triple shift = paid work + housework + emotional work. | Progress is incomplete: women work more but still do most domestic labour. Public equality > domestic equality.
Common Errors: Watch Out! Students often make mistakes here. Wrong: Men and women now have completely equal roles in the family because most women work. Correct: While women's employment has increased (72% work), this has created the dual burden rather than equality. The BSA (2018) shows women do twice as much housework as men even when working full-time. Men's contribution has increased mainly in visible, intermittent tasks (DIY, driving children) rather than routine daily work (cooking, cleaning, laundry). Women's employment has changed family roles significantly, but full equality has not been achieved.
