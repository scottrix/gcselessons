C26: Polymers

Foundation All Boards

Describe how alkenes polymerise, identify common polymers, and explain the problems with disposing of plastics.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe addition polymerisation and draw repeat units for common polymers
  • Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of plastics and describe methods of disposal

Prerequisites

  • Cracking and Alkenes (C24) — alkenes, C=C double bonds

Lesson 1: Addition Polymerisation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Plastic Everywhere

List every plastic item you can see in the room. Where does plastic come from? What is it made of?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Polymers are large molecules made from many small repeating units (monomers). The process of joining monomers together is called polymerisation.

Addition polymerisation:
• Many alkene monomers join together by breaking their C=C double bonds
• The double bonds open up and link to form long chains
• No other product is formed (unlike condensation polymerisation)
• The repeat unit shows the smallest repeating section of the polymer

Common polymers and their monomers:
• Poly(ethene) (PE): monomer = ethene C₂H₄ → used for bags, bottles, cling film
• Poly(propene) (PP): monomer = propene C₃H₆ → used for containers, rope, carpets
• Poly(chloroethene) / PVC: monomer = chloroethene C₂H₃Cl → used for pipes, window frames, flooring
• Poly(phenylethene) / polystyrene (PS): monomer = phenylethene C₈H₈ → used for packaging, cups, insulation
• Poly(tetrafluoroethene) / PTFE / Teflon: monomer = tetrafluoroethene C₂F₄ → used for non-stick coatings

How to draw the repeat unit:
1. Draw the monomer with the C=C double bond
2. Change the double bond to a single bond
3. Show bonds going out to the next unit
4. Put brackets around and add the subscript 'n' (for many units)

Properties of polymers:
• Strong, tough, flexible
• Electrical insulators
• Unreactive (inert) — do not decompose easily
• Low density (lightweight)
• Can be moulded into any shape when heated (thermoplastics)
Poly(ethene) from ethene

n(C₂H₄) → (C₂H₄)ₙ
Many ethene molecules join: the C=C double bond opens up and forms single bonds to adjacent monomers. The repeat unit is –CH₂–CH₂–. The 'n' means many hundreds or thousands of units joined together.

Drawing repeat units

Ethene: CH₂=CH₂ → repeat unit: –[CH₂–CH₂]ₙ–
Propene: CH₂=CHCH₃ → repeat unit: –[CH₂–CH(CH₃)]ₙ–
Chloroethene: CH₂=CHCl → repeat unit: –[CH₂–CHCl]ₙ–
The double bond breaks and forms single bonds to the next unit.

Addition polymerisation:
n(monomer) → polymer

Ethene → poly(ethene): n(CH₂=CH₂) → (–CH₂–CH₂–)ₙ
Propene → poly(propene): n(CH₂=CHCH₃) → (–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–)ₙ
Chloroethene → PVC: n(CH₂=CHCl) → (–CH₂–CHCl–)ₙ

Common Misconception

Polymers are made from alkanes.

Addition polymers are made from alkenes (unsaturated monomers). The C=C double bond breaks and opens up to form the polymer chain. Alkanes cannot undergo addition polymerisation because they have no double bonds.

Differentiation

Support:
Use paper models: draw many ethene molecules, then cut them and stick them together in a chain to show polymerisation.
Stretch:
Why can't ethane undergo addition polymerisation? Draw the repeat unit for poly(chloroethene) and explain why PVC is used for water pipes.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Draw the repeat unit for: poly(ethene), poly(propene), poly(chloroethene). What is the monomer for each?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Draws repeat units correctly, names common polymers and their monomers, and explains addition polymerisation.
  • Getting there: Can name monomers and polymers but struggles to draw repeat units.
  • Not yet: Cannot link monomers to polymers. Reteach with paper chain model.

Lesson 2: Disposal of Plastics

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Plastic Problem

Over 300 million tonnes of plastic are produced worldwide each year. Much ends up in landfill or the ocean. How long do you think a plastic bottle takes to decompose? (Answer: up to 450 years!)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Methods of disposing of plastics:

1. Landfill:
• Plastic buried in holes in the ground
• Takes up space, does not decompose for hundreds of years
• Can release harmful chemicals into soil and groundwater
• Not a sustainable long-term solution

2. Incineration (burning):
• Burns plastic to release energy (waste-to-energy)
• Reduces volume of waste
• Produces CO₂ (contributes to climate change)
• May release toxic gases (e.g. dioxins from PVC) — needs filters
• Some energy is recovered as electricity

3. Recycling:
• Melts down plastic and remoulds into new products
• Saves energy and raw materials
• Not all plastics can be recycled (depends on type)
• Limited number of times plastic can be recycled (quality degrades each time — 'downcycling')
• Requires sorting by type (different plastics have different properties)

4. Biodegradable and photodegradable plastics:
• Biodegradable: broken down by bacteria/fungi into CO₂, water and biomass
• Photodegradable: broken down by UV light, then biodegraded
• Take much shorter time to decompose than conventional plastics
• Some made from plant starch (e.g. polylactic acid — PLA)
• May still release CO₂ as they decompose

Advantages of plastics:
• Cheap to produce, durable, lightweight, versatile, good insulators, unreactive

Disadvantages of plastics:
• Non-biodegradable (take hundreds of years to decompose), pollution, microplastics, toxic fumes when burned, reliance on crude oil
Recycling symbols

Plastics are labelled with numbers 1-7. PET (1) and HDPE (2) are widely recycled. PVC (3) and polystyrene (6) are rarely recycled. Different types cannot be mixed — they have different melting points and properties.

Microplastics

When plastics break down into tiny pieces (<1mm), they become microplastics. These enter the food chain — found in oceans, soil, air, and even human blood. They can carry toxic chemicals and harm wildlife.

Disposal methods:
• Landfill: buried, takes centuries, pollutes soil
• Incineration: burns waste, releases energy + CO₂ + toxic gases
• Recycling: remelts plastic, saves resources, limited cycles
• Biodegradable: broken down by bacteria, much faster

Plastics: advantages (cheap, durable, versatile) vs disadvantages (non-biodegradable, pollution, microplastics)

Common Misconception

All plastics can be recycled.

Not all plastics can be recycled — it depends on the type. Different plastics have different properties and melting points. Some (like PVC) are rarely recycled because they release toxic gases when heated. Plastics must be sorted by type before recycling.

Differentiation

Support:
Collect plastic packaging from home. Look at the recycling symbols. Which ones can be recycled? Which cannot? Discuss why.
Stretch:
Research: what are the Great Pacific Garbage Patch? How big is it? What is being done about it?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Evaluate: should we ban all single-use plastics? Give three arguments for and three against.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Describes all four disposal methods, evaluates advantages and disadvantages of plastics, and discusses environmental impact.
  • Getting there: Knows disposal methods but cannot evaluate them critically.
  • Not yet: Cannot describe how plastics are disposed of. Reteach with examples from home.

Homework Suggestions

  • Draw repeat units for 5 different polymers and name their monomers
  • Survey plastic items in your home — which have recycling symbols? Which types?
  • Research: what is bioplastic? How is it different from conventional plastic?
