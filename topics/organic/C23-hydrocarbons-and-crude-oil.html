C23: Hydrocarbons and Crude Oil

Foundation All Boards

Describe what crude oil is, how it is separated by fractional distillation, and the properties of alkanes.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe what crude oil is and how it is a mixture of hydrocarbons
  • Explain fractional distillation and the uses of each fraction
  • Describe the trends in properties of alkanes as chain length increases

Prerequisites

  • Atoms and Elements (C1) — atomic structure
  • Bonding (C5-C6) — covalent bonding

Lesson 1: Crude Oil and Fractional Distillation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Oil Everywhere

Oil is used in cars, planes, ships, plastics, clothes, medicine, cosmetics, roads, and electricity generation. How many things in your house are made from or powered by oil?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons found underground. It formed from the remains of ancient sea organisms buried under layers of rock for millions of years. It is a finite resource (fossil fuel).

Hydrocarbons are compounds containing only hydrogen and carbon. Most hydrocarbons in crude oil are alkanes (general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂).

Fractional distillation separates crude oil into fractions (groups of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points):

1. Crude oil is heated to about 350°C → most hydrocarbons vaporise
2. Vapour enters the bottom of a fractionating column
3. The column is hottest at the bottom (~350°C) and coolest at the top (~25°C)
4. Each fraction condenses at a different height in the column (where temperature = boiling point of that fraction)
5. Fractions are collected as liquids at different levels

Key fractions and uses:
• Refinery gases (C₁-C₄): bottled gas (LPG), heating, cooking
• Petrol/gasoline (C₅-C₇): car fuel
• Naphtha (C₇-C₉): chemical feedstock, plastics
• Kerosene (C₁₀-C₁₅): jet fuel, heating
• Diesel oil (C₁₅-C₂₀): diesel engines, heating
• Heavy fuel oil (C₂₀-C₅₀): ships, power stations
• Bitumen (C₅₀+): roads, roofs

Trends in fractions:
• As chain length increases: boiling point increases, viscosity increases, flammability decreases, density increases
Why distillation works

Short-chain hydrocarbons (e.g. methane C₁) have weak intermolecular forces → low boiling point → stay as gas at top of column. Long-chain hydrocarbons (e.g. bitumen) have strong intermolecular forces → high boiling point → condense near the bottom.

Fractional distillation column

Hot crude oil enters at the bottom. The column has a temperature gradient: ~350°C at bottom, ~25°C at top. Each fraction condenses where the temperature matches its boiling point. Gases exit at the top, liquids are tapped off at different levels, thick bitumen is removed from the bottom.

Crude oil → fractional distillation → fractions

Fraction: group of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points

As chain length increases:
• Boiling point ↑
• Viscosity ↑ (thicker)
• Flammability ↓
• Density ↑

Common Misconception

Fractional distillation separates crude oil into pure compounds.

Each fraction is still a mixture of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points, not a pure substance. For example, petrol is a mixture of several different hydrocarbons (C₅-C₇).

Differentiation

Support:
Use a physical model: fill a column with cotton wool. Pour coloured water at the bottom. Different colours rise to different heights based on density. This models how fractions separate.
Stretch:
Research: why can't crude oil be used directly as fuel? What problems would occur?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Match each fraction to its use: bitumen, kerosene, petrol, refinery gases. Why do short-chain hydrocarbons have lower boiling points?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains fractional distillation clearly, describes the column, and lists fractions with their uses.
  • Getting there: Knows that fractions separate by boiling point but cannot describe the column process.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain how crude oil is separated. Reteach with the physical model.

Lesson 2: Alkanes

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Simplest Hydrocarbon

Methane (CH₄) is the simplest hydrocarbon. What is its structure? How many bonds does each carbon form? Why can't hydrocarbons have double bonds and still be alkanes?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons (contain only single C-C and C-H bonds).
General formula: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂

First six alkanes:
• Methane CH₄ (natural gas, fuel)
• Ethane C₂H₆ (fuel, chemical feedstock)
• Propane C₃H₈ (bottled gas, heating)
• Butane C₄H₁₀ (camping gas, lighter fuel)
• Pentane C₅H₁₂ (petrol component, solvent)
• Hexane C₆H₁₄ (solvent, gasoline component)

Properties of alkanes:
• All are unreactive at room temperature (strong C-C and C-H bonds)
• Flammable — burn in excess oxygen to produce CO₂ and H₂O (used as fuels)
• Insoluble in water (non-polar molecules)
• As chain length increases: boiling point increases, viscosity increases, flammability decreases

Burning alkanes (combustion):
• Complete combustion: Alkane + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O
  CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
  C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O
• Incomplete combustion (limited oxygen): produces CO (carbon monoxide — toxic) and/or C (soot)

Cracking: breaking long-chain alkanes into shorter, more useful molecules (alkanes + alkenes). Needed because crude oil produces more long-chain fractions than we need, but we need more short-chain ones (e.g. petrol).
Methane combustion

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. This is the complete combustion of methane (natural gas). It releases energy — this is why we use natural gas for heating and cooking. If there isn't enough oxygen, incomplete combustion occurs and carbon monoxide (CO) is produced — this is dangerous because CO is toxic and odourless.

Boiling point trend

Methane (C₁): −161°C (gas at room temperature). Hexane (C₆): 69°C (liquid). Decane (C₁₀): 174°C (liquid, thicker). As chain length increases, more electrons → stronger intermolecular forces → more energy needed to separate molecules → higher boiling point.

Alkane general formula: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂

Complete combustion:
Alkane + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Trends as chain length increases:
• Boiling point ↑
• Viscosity ↑
• Flammability ↓

Common Misconception

Alkanes contain double bonds.

Alkanes contain only single bonds — they are saturated hydrocarbons. Unsaturated hydrocarbons contain at least one double bond (alkenes). You can test for unsaturation using bromine water: alkenes decolourise it, alkanes do not.

Differentiation

Support:
Use molecular model kits to build methane, ethane, propane. Show how each adds one more C-C and two more C-H bonds.
Stretch:
Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of hexane (C₆H₁₄). How many moles of O₂ are needed?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Name the first four alkanes and give their molecular formulae. Write the combustion equation for ethane.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Names and draws alkanes, uses the general formula, and writes combustion equations.
  • Getting there: Knows the general formula but cannot always draw or name specific alkanes.
  • Not yet: Cannot use the alkane general formula. Reteach with molecular model kits.

Homework Suggestions

  • Draw dot-and-cross diagrams for methane, ethane, and propane
  • Write balanced equations for the complete combustion of butane and pentane
  • Research: what is hydraulic fracturing (fracking)? What are the environmental concerns?
