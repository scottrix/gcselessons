P1: Recording Outcomes

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand how to record and describe the outcomes of probability experiments using sample spaces, frequency tables, and relative frequency.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define and identify outcomes, events, and sample spaces
  • Record outcomes using systematic lists, tables, and frequency trees
  • Calculate and interpret relative frequency from experimental data
  • Understand that relative frequency approaches theoretical probability as trials increase
  • Use frequency trees to solve multi-stage probability problems

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of fractions, decimals, and percentages
  • Experience with simple probability language (certain, likely, unlikely, impossible)
  • Ability to calculate simple probabilities (Number / Algebra topics)

Materials & Equipment

  • Dice, coins, spinners, coloured counters for practical experiments
  • Mini whiteboards for quick-fire questions
  • Frequency tree templates (printed or drawn)
  • Spreadsheet software (optional) for simulating large numbers of trials

Lesson 1: Outcomes, Sample Spaces, and Frequency Tables

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

What Could Happen?

Roll a standard 6-sided die. Ask students to list all possible outcomes. Then ask: "If I roll the die 60 times, how many times would you expect each number to appear? Why?" Introduce the idea of theoretical vs experimental results.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Key Definitions:
  • Experiment: A repeatable process with observable results (e.g. rolling a die, flipping a coin)
  • Outcome: A single possible result of an experiment (e.g. rolling a 4)
  • Sample Space: The set of ALL possible outcomes. Denoted by S or Ω. For a 6-sided die: S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}
  • Event: A subset of the sample space (e.g. "rolling an even number" = {2, 4, 6})
Recording Outcomes:
  • Systematic Listing: List all outcomes in an organised way (e.g. for two coins: HH, HT, TH, TT)
  • Frequency Table: Record how often each outcome occurs in an experiment
  • Tally Charts: Useful for collecting data during an experiment
Relative Frequency:
  • Relative frequency = (Number of times event occurs) ÷ (Total number of trials)
  • As the number of trials increases, relative frequency tends to get closer to the theoretical probability (Law of Large Numbers)
  • Relative frequency can be expressed as a fraction, decimal, or percentage
Worked Example

A biased spinner has 4 sections: Red, Blue, Green, Yellow. It is spun 100 times with these results: Red: 35, Blue: 20, Green: 25, Yellow: 20.
(a) Complete the frequency table.
(b) Calculate the relative frequency of each colour.
(c) Is the spinner fair? Explain.

Answers:
(a) Table with colours and frequencies
(b) Red: 35/100 = 0.35, Blue: 20/100 = 0.20, Green: 25/100 = 0.25, Yellow: 20/100 = 0.20
(c) No, the spinner is not fair. A fair 4-section spinner would have relative frequency ≈ 0.25 for each colour. Red occurs much more often (0.35).

Common Misconception: "If I flip a coin 10 times and get 7 heads, the coin must be biased." Correction: Small samples can deviate significantly from theoretical probability. A fair coin can easily give 7 heads in 10 flips. You need many more trials (hundreds) to reliably detect bias.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide pre-drawn frequency tables and sample space templates. Use physical manipulatives (dice, coins) for concrete experience before abstract recording. Focus on simple experiments with few outcomes.
Stretch:
Ask students to design an experiment to test whether a die is biased. How many rolls would they need? How would they analyse the data? Introduce the concept of expected frequency: Expected frequency = Theoretical probability × Number of trials.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exit Ticket

Students answer on mini-whiteboards: "A bag contains 3 red, 5 blue, and 2 green counters. One counter is drawn at random, its colour recorded, and it is replaced. This is repeated 50 times. If the relative frequency of red is 0.28, is this what you would expect? Explain."

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can define sample space, event, outcome; construct frequency tables; calculate relative frequency; explain relationship between relative frequency and theoretical probability
  • Getting there: Can construct frequency tables and calculate relative frequency but struggles to explain the link to theoretical probability
  • Not yet: Cannot list all outcomes systematically or calculate relative frequency

Lesson 2: Frequency Trees and Multi-Stage Experiments

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Two-Stage Outcomes

Show a bag with 2 red and 3 blue counters. Draw one counter, note colour, replace it, draw again. Ask: "How many different outcomes are possible? List them." (RR, RB, BR, BB). Introduce frequency trees as a way to record this systematically.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Frequency Trees:
  • A diagram showing all possible outcomes of a multi-stage experiment with frequencies
  • Branches represent stages; end branches show outcomes
  • Frequencies are written at the end of each branch
  • Sum of frequencies on branches from the same node = total frequency at that node
Constructing a Frequency Tree:
  1. Identify the stages of the experiment
  2. Draw branches for each possible outcome at each stage
  3. Use given data to fill in known frequencies
  4. Work backwards or forwards to find missing frequencies
  5. Check: frequencies at each node sum correctly
Relative Frequency from Frequency Trees:
  • Relative frequency of a final outcome = Frequency of that outcome ÷ Total frequency at root
  • Can be used to estimate probabilities for future trials
Worked Example

100 students each flip a coin twice. The first flip gave 55 Heads and 45 Tails. Of the 55 who got Heads first, 30 got Heads on the second flip. Of the 45 who got Tails first, 20 got Heads on the second flip.
(a) Complete the frequency tree.
(b) Find the relative frequency of getting two Heads.

Answer:
(a) Root: 100 → H (55) → H (30), T (25); T (45) → H (20), T (25)
(b) Relative frequency of HH = 30/100 = 0.3

Common Misconception: Students put probabilities on frequency tree branches instead of frequencies. Correction: Frequency trees show ACTUAL frequencies from an experiment (or expected frequencies). Probability trees (covered later) show theoretical probabilities. Keep them separate.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide partially completed frequency trees. Use colour-coding for different stages. Start with experiments where first-stage frequencies are given and second-stage are to be found.
Stretch:
Give a frequency tree with some missing values that require working backwards using subtraction. Ask: "If we repeated this experiment 500 times, approximately how many times would we expect each outcome?" (Scaling up using relative frequency).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Frequency Tree Reconstruction

Show a completed frequency tree with all frequencies. Ask students to write a description of the experiment it represents: number of stages, outcomes at each stage, total trials.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can construct and complete frequency trees from given data; calculate relative frequencies from the tree
  • Getting there: Can complete a frequency tree when structure is given but struggles to construct one from a description
  • Not yet: Cannot interpret the structure of a frequency tree or sum frequencies correctly

Homework Suggestions

  • Roll a die 60 times. Record results in a frequency table. Calculate relative frequencies. Compare to theoretical probability (1/6 ≈ 0.167).
  • Complete a frequency tree worksheet with 3 multi-stage experiments (e.g. coin + die, two spinners, drawing counters with replacement).
  • Research: "Why do insurance companies use relative frequency from historical data rather than theoretical probability?"
  • Create a poster explaining the difference between an outcome, an event, and a sample space with examples.
