P6: Systematic Listing Strategies

Foundation Higher All Boards

Master systematic listing strategies including tables, grids, and the product rule for counting outcomes in multi-stage experiments.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • List outcomes systematically for single and multi-stage experiments
  • Use two-way tables and sample space diagrams to list combined outcomes
  • Apply the product rule for counting: Total outcomes = n₁ × n₂ × n₃ × ...
  • Distinguish between ordered and unordered outcomes (permutations vs combinations)
  • Solve problems involving systematic listing and counting

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of sample spaces and outcomes (P1)
  • Basic probability calculations (P3)
  • Multiplication and division facts
  • Set notation basics

Materials & Equipment

  • Blank two-way tables and sample space grids
  • Coloured counters, dice, spinners for physical modelling
  • Mini whiteboards for quick listing practice
  • Product rule flowchart/reference cards

Lesson 1: Systematic Listing and Sample Space Diagrams

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

List All Outcomes

"A meal deal offers: Main (Burger, Pasta, Salad), Drink (Water, Juice, Cola). List all possible meal combinations." Students list. Discuss strategies: list all mains with first drink, then all mains with second drink, etc.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Why Systematic Listing Matters: Missing outcomes leads to wrong probabilities. A systematic method ensures completeness.
Strategies for Systematic Listing:
  • Tree-like listing: Fix first choice, list all second choices; repeat. (Burger+Water, Burger+Juice, Burger+Cola, Pasta+Water...)
  • Two-way table / Sample space diagram: Rows = first event outcomes, Columns = second event outcomes. Each cell = combined outcome.
  • Ordered pairs: Write as (Outcome1, Outcome2). e.g. (Burger, Water)
Sample Space Diagrams for Two Dice / Spinners:
  • 6×6 grid for two 6-sided dice (36 outcomes)
  • 4×4 grid for two 4-sided spinners (16 outcomes)
  • Can show sums, products, differences in cells
Product Rule for Counting: If there are n₁ outcomes for stage 1, n₂ for stage 2, n₃ for stage 3, etc., then Total Outcomes = n₁ × n₂ × n₃ × ...
This works when choices are INDEPENDENT (choice at one stage doesn't restrict options at later stages).
Examples:
  • Coin (2) × Die (6) = 12 outcomes
  • 3 shirts × 4 trousers × 2 shoes = 24 outfits
  • 4-digit PIN (10×10×10×10 = 10,000) if digits can repeat
When NOT to Use Product Rule:
  • Without replacement: choosing 2 cards from a deck → 52 × 51 (not 52²)
  • Restrictions: "First digit cannot be 0" → 9 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 9000
  • Order doesn't matter (combinations): Choosing 3 students from 10 → not 10×9×8
Worked Example

A restaurant offers 3 starters, 5 mains, and 4 desserts. A three-course meal consists of one of each.
(a) How many different three-course meals are possible?
(b) If a customer chooses at random, what is the probability they choose Soup (starter), Fish (main), and Ice Cream (dessert)?
(c) List all meals that include Soup as starter.

Answers:
(a) 3 × 5 × 4 = 60 (Product rule)
(b) 1/60 (only 1 favourable out of 60 equally likely)
(c) Fix Soup as starter: 1 × 5 × 4 = 20 meals. List: Soup + (each main) + (each dessert).

Common Misconception: Using product rule when order doesn't matter. "Choose 2 toppings from 5 for a pizza: 5 × 4 = 20." Correction: Toppings {Pepperoni, Mushroom} is the same as {Mushroom, Pepperoni}. Order doesn't matter. This is a COMBINATION problem: 5C2 = 10. Product rule counts PERMUTATIONS (ordered). Divide by 2! = 2 when choosing 2, 3! = 6 when choosing 3, etc.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide pre-drawn grids for two-stage experiments. Use physical objects (coloured counters) to build combinations. Colour-code rows/columns. Start with small numbers (2×3, 3×3).
Stretch:
Problems with restrictions: "How many 3-digit numbers > 500 can be made from digits 1-6 without repetition?" (First digit: 5,6 → 2 choices; then 5×4 = 40). Explore combinations vs permutations: "A committee of 3 from 8 people vs President/VP/Secretary from 8 people."

Plenary (5 minutes)

Spot the Error

Show flawed systematic lists. Students identify missing or duplicated outcomes.
1. Two coins: HH, HT, TT (missing TH)
2. Die + Coin: (1,H), (2,H), (3,H), (4,H), (5,H), (6,H), (1,T)... (missing 2,T etc)
3. Product rule: 3 starters, 4 mains, 3 desserts → 3+4+3=10 (should be 36)

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Lists outcomes systematically without omission/duplication; uses product rule correctly; identifies when product rule doesn't apply
  • Getting there: Lists systematically for simple cases but misses outcomes in complex cases; applies product rule mechanically without checking independence
  • Not yet: Lists randomly with omissions/duplications; adds instead of multiplies for product rule

Lesson 2: Advanced Listing and Problem Solving

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Product Rule Quick Fire

Mini-whiteboards: Total outcomes for...
1. 4 shirts × 3 shorts × 2 hats
2. 5-digit code (digits 0-9, can repeat)
3. 3-digit number (digits 1-9, no repeat)
4. Choose captain + vice-captain from 11 players
5. Choose 2 representatives from 11 players

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Permutations (Order Matters): Arranging r items from n distinct items. nPr = n! / (n-r)!. Examples: race positions (1st, 2nd, 3rd), passwords, arranging books on shelf.
Combinations (Order Doesn't Matter): Choosing r items from n distinct items. nCr = n! / (r!(n-r)!). Examples: lottery numbers, committee members, pizza toppings.
Key Question: "Does swapping the order give a DIFFERENT outcome?" Yes → Permutation. No → Combination.
Factorial Notation: n! = n × (n-1) × ... × 2 × 1. 0! = 1.
Systematic Listing for Probability:
  1. List ALL equally likely outcomes (sample space)
  2. Count total outcomes (n)
  3. Identify favourable outcomes for the event
  4. Count favourable outcomes (r)
  5. P(Event) = r/n
Using Complements: Sometimes easier to list what you DON'T want. P(A) = 1 - P(not A).
Worked Example

A bag contains 4 red (R) and 3 blue (B) counters. Two counters are drawn at random WITHOUT REPLACEMENT.
(a) List all possible outcomes systematically. (Note: order matters for listing, but R₁R₂ = R₂R₁ for probability of "two reds")
(b) Find P(both same colour).
(c) Find P(one of each colour).

Answers:
(a) Systematic list (ordered): R₁R₂, R₁R₃, R₁R₄, R₁B₁, R₁B₂, R₁B₃, R₂R₁, R₂R₃, R₂R₄, R₂B₁, R₂B₂, R₂B₃, R₃R₁, R₃R₂, R₃R₄, R₃B₁, R₃B₂, R₃B₃, R₄R₁, R₄R₂, R₄R₃, R₄B₁, R₄B₂, R₄B₃, B₁R₁, B₁R₂, B₁R₃, B₁R₄, B₁B₂, B₁B₃, B₂R₁, B₂R₂, B₂R₃, B₂R₄, B₂B₁, B₂B₃, B₃R₁, B₃R₂, B₃R₃, B₃R₄, B₃B₁, B₃B₂. Total = 42 ordered outcomes.
But for probability, we often use unordered: P(RR) = (4/7)×(3/6) = 12/42, P(BB) = (3/7)×(2/6) = 6/42, P(RB or BR) = 24/42.
(b) P(same) = P(RR) + P(BB) = 12/42 + 6/42 = 18/42 = 3/7
(c) P(one each) = 1 - 3/7 = 4/7 (or 24/42 = 4/7)

Common Misconception: "Without replacement means you can't use the product rule." Correction: You CAN use a modified product rule: choices decrease at each stage. For 2 cards from 52: 52 × 51 = 2652 ordered outcomes. For unordered: 52C2 = 1326. The product rule still applies, but the number of options changes.

Differentiation

Support:
Focus on WITH REPLACEMENT problems first (product rule applies directly). Use tree diagrams alongside listing. Provide factorial button calculator practice.
Stretch:
Derive nCr formula from nPr: nCr = nPr / r!. Solve: "nC3 = 56, find n." Investigate Pascal's Triangle connections. "How many ways to deal 5 cards from 52?" (2,598,960).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Permutation or Combination?

Quick classification: Students hold up P or C card for each scenario.
1. 4-digit PIN code (P)
2. Choosing 3 pizza toppings from 10 (C)
3. Arranging 5 books on a shelf (P)
4. Dealing a poker hand of 5 cards (C)
5. Gold/Silver/Bronze in 100m final (P)
6. Choosing 2 students for a trip (C)

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Distinguishes permutations/combinations; uses nPr/nCr notation; solves complex listing problems with restrictions
  • Getting there: Can identify permutation vs combination but struggles with formula; lists outcomes for without-replacement problems
  • Not yet: Confuses permutations and combinations; uses n×n for without replacement

Homework Suggestions

  • Systematic listing worksheet: 10 problems covering 2-stage and 3-stage experiments, with and without replacement.
  • Product rule problems: Calculate total outcomes for 8 scenarios (some with restrictions).
  • Permutation/Combination classification: 15 scenarios to classify and calculate.
  • Challenge: "A password must be 8 characters: 2 uppercase letters, 3 lowercase letters, 2 digits, 1 symbol. How many possible passwords if characters can repeat? If they cannot repeat?"
