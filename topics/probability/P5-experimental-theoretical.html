P5: Experimental and Theoretical Probability

Foundation Higher All Boards

Distinguish between theoretical probability (calculated from equally likely outcomes) and experimental probability (estimated from relative frequency), understand the Law of Large Numbers, and use experimental data to test for bias.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define theoretical probability and experimental probability (relative frequency)
  • Calculate theoretical probability for equally likely outcomes
  • Estimate probability from experimental data using relative frequency
  • Understand the Law of Large Numbers: more trials → better estimate
  • Compare experimental and theoretical probabilities to detect bias
  • Calculate expected frequency: Expected = Theoretical Probability × Number of Trials

Prerequisites

  • Probability scale and notation (P3)
  • Sample spaces and equally likely outcomes (P1, P3)
  • Relative frequency from frequency tables (P1)
  • Fractions, decimals, percentages conversions

Materials & Equipment

  • Dice, coins, spinners, counters for experiments
  • Spreadsheet software (Excel/Google Sheets) for simulating many trials
  • Graph paper for plotting relative frequency vs trials
  • Pre-printed results tables for experiments

Lesson 1: Theoretical vs Experimental Probability

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Predict and Test

"A fair coin is flipped 10 times. How many heads do you expect?" (5). "Now flip it 10 times. Did you get exactly 5?" Repeat with 100 flips (simulate or use spreadsheet). Discuss why results vary.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Theoretical Probability: Calculated from the structure of the experiment, assuming equally likely outcomes. P(Event) = Favourable Outcomes / Total Possible Outcomes. This is a PREDICTION of what SHOULD happen in the long run. Example: P(6 on fair die) = 1/6 ≈ 0.1667.
Experimental Probability (Relative Frequency): Estimated from ACTUAL results of an experiment. Experimental P(Event) = Frequency of Event / Total Trials. This is what DID happen. Example: Die rolled 60 times, 6 appears 12 times → Experimental P(6) = 12/60 = 0.2.
Key Differences:
  • Theoretical is exact (for fair, ideal conditions); Experimental is an estimate
  • Theoretical uses reasoning; Experimental uses data
  • Experimental probability varies from experiment to experiment; Theoretical is fixed
  • As trials increase, Experimental → Theoretical (Law of Large Numbers)
Law of Large Numbers: As the number of trials increases, the relative frequency of an event tends to get closer to the theoretical probability. It does NOT guarantee exact equality for any finite number of trials.
Expected Frequency: The number of times we expect an event to occur in n trials. Expected Frequency = Theoretical Probability × n. Example: Fair die rolled 300 times → Expected sixes = (1/6) × 300 = 50.
Worked Example

A spinner has 5 equal sections numbered 1-5. It is spun 200 times. Results: 1: 38, 2: 42, 3: 40, 4: 45, 5: 35.
(a) What is the theoretical probability of each number?
(b) Calculate the experimental probability of each number.
(c) Calculate the expected frequency for each number.
(d) Does the spinner appear fair? Explain.

Answers:
(a) Theoretical P(each) = 1/5 = 0.2
(b) Experimental: P(1)=38/200=0.19, P(2)=42/200=0.21, P(3)=40/200=0.20, P(4)=45/200=0.225, P(5)=35/200=0.175
(c) Expected frequency = 0.2 × 200 = 40 for each number
(d) Results are close to expected (40). Variation is within what you'd expect by chance. No strong evidence of bias.

Common Misconception: "If theoretical probability is 1/6, then in 6 rolls you MUST get one 6." Correction: Theoretical probability describes LONG-RUN behaviour. In 6 rolls, you might get 0 sixes, 1 six, 2 sixes, etc. The probability of exactly one 6 in 6 rolls is only about 40%. The Law of Large Numbers applies over many hundreds/thousands of trials, not small numbers.

Differentiation

Support:
Conduct a simple experiment together (e.g. coin flips). Record cumulative relative frequency after 10, 20, 50, 100 flips. Plot on graph to visualise convergence. Provide a table template.
Stretch:
Use a spreadsheet to simulate 10,000 coin flips. Plot relative frequency of heads after every 10 flips. Discuss the "random walk" appearance. Calculate standard deviation of relative frequency: σ = √[p(1-p)/n]. Investigate: how many trials needed to be 95% confident the experimental probability is within 0.01 of theoretical?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Convergence Discussion

Show a graph of relative frequency vs number of trials for a fair coin (pre-made). Ask: "Why does the line wiggle at first but then settle near 0.5?" "Will it ever stay exactly at 0.5?" "What would a biased coin's graph look like?"

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Clearly distinguishes theoretical vs experimental; calculates both; explains Law of Large Numbers; computes expected frequency
  • Getting there: Can calculate both but confuses which is which; knows more trials = better but can't explain why
  • Not yet: Thinks theoretical and experimental should match exactly in small samples; cannot calculate expected frequency

Lesson 2: Using Experimental Data to Test Fairness

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Biased or Just Unlucky?

A die is rolled 60 times. Results: 1: 8, 2: 12, 3: 10, 4: 9, 5: 11, 6: 10. Is the die biased? What about if 6 appeared 25 times? Discuss how to decide.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Comparing Experimental to Theoretical:
  • Calculate expected frequency for each outcome: E = n × P(theoretical)
  • Compare observed (O) vs expected (E)
  • Small differences are due to random variation; large differences suggest bias
  • Rule of thumb: If |O - E| > 2√E for any outcome, it's suspicious (rough guide)
Chi-Squared Test (Higher/Extension): A formal statistical test for goodness of fit. χ² = Σ(O - E)²/E. Compare to critical value. (Mention only — not required for GCSE).
Making Judgements:
  • Consider sample size: 20 rolls vs 600 rolls — same deviation means different things
  • Look at ALL outcomes, not just one
  • Context matters: A casino die vs a board game die
  • State conclusions carefully: "There is evidence to suggest bias" NOT "The die is definitely biased"
Using Experimental Probability for Prediction:
  • If theoretical probability is unknown (e.g. a drawing pin landing point-up), experimental probability is our BEST ESTIMATE
  • Use relative frequency from a large number of trials as the probability for future predictions
  • Example: Pin dropped 1000 times, lands point-up 632 times → P(point-up) ≈ 0.632 for future drops
Worked Example

A drawing pin is dropped 500 times. It lands point-up 312 times, point-down 188 times.
(a) Estimate the probability the pin lands point-up.
(b) If the pin is dropped 2000 times, how many times would you expect it to land point-up?
(c) A student says "The pin is more likely to land point-up because it has a heavier base." Is this a theoretical or experimental argument?

Answers:
(a) Experimental P(point-up) = 312/500 = 0.624
(b) Expected = 0.624 × 2000 = 1248
(c) This is a THEORETICAL argument (based on physical reasoning), not experimental.

Common Misconception: "Experimental probability from 50 trials is just as good as from 5000 trials." Correction: Precision increases with sample size. Margin of error ≈ 1/√n. For n=50, margin ≈ 0.14; for n=5000, margin ≈ 0.014. Small samples give very rough estimates.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a structured comparison table: Outcome | Theoretical P | Expected Freq | Observed Freq | Difference. Use colour coding: Green (close), Yellow (moderate), Red (large difference).
Stretch:
Investigate: "A coin is flipped 100 times, giving 60 heads. Is it biased?" Calculate the probability of getting 60+ heads with a fair coin (binomial distribution). Discuss p-values and significance levels. Explore Bayesian perspective: prior belief about fairness updated with data.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Bias Verdict

Present 3 scenarios with observed vs expected frequencies. Students vote: "Fair", "Possibly biased", "Strong evidence of bias". Discuss reasoning.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Compares observed vs expected; calculates expected frequencies; makes reasoned judgements about bias; uses experimental probability for predictions when theoretical unknown
  • Getting there: Calculates expected frequencies but struggles to interpret differences; confuses experimental/theoretical reasoning
  • Not yet: Thinks any difference from expected means bias; cannot calculate expected frequency

Homework Suggestions

  • Conduct a 100-trial experiment (coin, die, spinner, drawing pin). Record cumulative relative frequency every 10 trials. Plot graph. Write a conclusion about fairness.
  • Complete a worksheet: Given theoretical probabilities and observed frequencies for 5 scenarios, calculate expected frequencies and state whether each suggests bias.
  • Research: "How do casinos test dice for fairness?" Write a short report.
  • Spreadsheet challenge: Simulate 1000 rolls of a fair die. Calculate relative frequency after 10, 50, 100, 500, 1000 rolls. Screenshot the graph.
