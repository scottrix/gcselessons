P2: Fairness and Randomness

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand what makes a probability experiment fair or biased, the concept of randomness, and how to identify and avoid bias in sampling and experiments.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define fair experiment, biased experiment, and randomness
  • Identify sources of bias in experiments, surveys, and sampling methods
  • Understand the difference between random and haphazard selection
  • Design fair experiments and random sampling methods
  • Evaluate whether a given scenario describes a fair or biased process

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of equally likely outcomes (from P1)
  • Basic probability vocabulary (outcome, event, sample space)
  • Ability to calculate simple probabilities

Materials & Equipment

  • Various dice (standard, weighted), coins, spinners (fair and biased)
  • Bags with coloured counters (different ratios)
  • Random number generators (calculators, online tools, random number tables)
  • Scenario cards for bias identification activity

Lesson 1: Fairness, Bias, and Randomness

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Fair or Not?

Show three scenarios:
1. A standard die rolled on a flat table.
2. A die with a small weight inside, rolled on a flat table.
3. A coin spun on a table (not flipped).
Ask: "Which are fair? Which might be biased? Why?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Fair Experiment: All outcomes in the sample space are equally likely. Example: fair 6-sided die → P(1) = P(2) = ... = P(6) = 1/6.
Biased Experiment: Outcomes are NOT equally likely. One or more outcomes have a higher (or lower) probability than others. Example: a weighted die where P(6) = 0.3 and others are 0.14 each.
Randomness: Each outcome occurs without predictable pattern; each trial is independent; the process cannot be predicted or controlled. True randomness is difficult to achieve in practice.
Sources of Bias:
  • Physical bias: Weighted dice, bent coins, uneven spinners, worn balls in lottery machines
  • Procedural bias: How the experiment is conducted (e.g. spinning a coin vs flipping, always starting the spinner from the same position)
  • Selection bias: Non-random sampling (e.g. surveying only people leaving a gym about fitness habits)
  • Response bias: Leading questions, social desirability in surveys
  • Measurement bias: Faulty equipment, inconsistent measurement technique
Random vs Haphazard:
  • Random: Uses a genuinely random mechanism (random number generator, well-shuffled cards, lottery machine)
  • Haphazard: "Just picking" without a system — humans are very bad at being random (tend to avoid patterns, pick "random-looking" numbers like 37)
Worked Example

A teacher wants to select 5 students from a class of 30 to represent the school. She considers these methods:
A) Pick the first 5 students on the register.
B) Ask for volunteers and pick the first 5.
C) Number students 1-30, use a random number generator to pick 5 numbers.
D) Pick 5 students sitting near the front.

Which method(s) give a fair, random sample? Explain why the others are biased.

Answer: Only C is fair and random. A is biased (alphabetical order may correlate with nothing, but it's not random). B is biased (volunteers may be more confident/extroverted). D is biased (location in room not random).

Common Misconception: "Random means 'anything can happen' or 'no pattern at all'." Correction: Random means each outcome has a known probability (often equal). Patterns CAN appear in random data (e.g. HHHHH in coin flips) — this doesn't mean it's not random. Randomness is about the PROCESS, not the appearance of the results.

Differentiation

Support:
Focus on concrete examples: fair/unfair dice, biased spinners. Use physical objects to test. Provide a checklist: "Is each outcome equally likely? Is the process repeatable without human choice?"
Stretch:
Discuss: "Can a computer generate truly random numbers?" (Pseudo-randomness, seeds, cryptographic randomness). Investigate Benford's Law as a tool for detecting made-up data (non-random first digits).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Bias Detective

Show 4 short scenarios. Students identify the type of bias (physical, procedural, selection, response, measurement) and suggest a fair alternative.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can define fairness, bias, randomness; identify bias in various contexts; explain why random ≠ haphazard
  • Getting there: Can identify obvious bias (weighted die) but misses subtle bias (selection, procedural)
  • Not yet: Confuses random with haphazard; thinks "fair" just means "not cheating"

Lesson 2: Designing Fair Experiments and Random Sampling

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Fix the Bias

Scenario: "A company wants to know if employees like the new canteen food. They put a suggestion box in the canteen." Identify the bias. How would you fix it?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Designing Fair Experiments:
  • Use properly manufactured equipment (casino-grade dice, precision spinners)
  • Standardise the procedure (e.g. "shake die in cup 3 times, roll onto felt surface")
  • Use randomisation: random starting positions, random order of trials
  • Blind/Double-blind: person recording result doesn't know the hypothesis (reduces unconscious bias)
  • Large number of trials to reduce chance variation
Random Sampling Methods:
  • Simple Random Sampling: Every member of population has equal chance; every sample of size n has equal chance. Use random number generator / random number tables.
  • Systematic Sampling: Choose random start, then every kth member. (Can introduce bias if list has a pattern.)
  • Stratified Sampling: Divide population into strata (groups), sample proportionally from each. Ensures representation.
  • Cluster Sampling: Divide into clusters, randomly select clusters, sample all in chosen clusters.
Random Number Generation:
  • Calculator: RAN# or RanInt function
  • Spreadsheet: =RANDBETWEEN(1, n) or =RAND()
  • Online: random.org, random number generator apps
  • Tables: Random number tables (old school but still used)
Worked Example

A school has 1200 students: 300 in Year 7, 280 in Year 8, 320 in Year 9, 300 in Year 10. They want a stratified sample of 60 students.
(a) How many from each year group?
(b) Describe how to select the Year 7 students randomly.

Answers:
(a) Year 7: (300/1200)×60 = 15; Year 8: (280/1200)×60 = 14; Year 9: (320/1200)×60 = 16; Year 10: (300/1200)×60 = 15. Check: 15+14+16+15 = 60.
(b) Number Year 7 students 1-300. Use random number generator to generate 15 unique numbers between 1 and 300. Select those students.

Common Misconception: "Stratified sampling means you pick the same number from each group." Correction: Stratified sampling means you pick PROPORTIONALLY from each group. If groups are different sizes, you pick different numbers. Equal numbers from each group would be quota sampling, not stratified.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a step-by-step template for stratified sampling calculations. Use smaller numbers (e.g. population 100, sample 20). Focus on simple random sampling first.
Stretch:
Compare efficiency of different sampling methods. Discuss: "When might systematic sampling be biased?" (e.g. sampling every 10th house on a street where houses alternate large/small). Investigate capture-recapture method for estimating population size.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Method Match

Give 5 research scenarios. Students match each to the most appropriate sampling method and justify their choice.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can design a fair experiment; choose and justify appropriate sampling method; calculate stratified sample sizes
  • Getting there: Can calculate stratified samples but struggles to justify sampling method choice
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between sampling methods or calculate proportional samples

Homework Suggestions

  • Design a fair experiment to test whether a coin is biased. Include: equipment, procedure, number of trials, how you'll analyse results.
  • Find a real-world survey reported in the news. Identify the sampling method used and any potential sources of bias.
  • Complete stratified sampling calculations worksheet (5 problems with different population structures).
  • Write a paragraph explaining why humans are bad at generating random sequences. Give examples.
