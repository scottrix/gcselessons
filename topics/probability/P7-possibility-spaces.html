P7: Possibility Spaces

Foundation Higher All Boards

Construct and interpret possibility space diagrams (sample space diagrams) and two-way tables for combined events, and use them to calculate probabilities.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, Eduqas, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Construct possibility space diagrams for two independent events (dice, spinners, coins)
  • Complete and interpret two-way tables for categorical data
  • Calculate probabilities from possibility spaces: P(Event) = Favourable Outcomes / Total Outcomes
  • Calculate probabilities of combined events: P(A and B), P(A or B)
  • Understand when outcomes are equally likely in a possibility space
  • Use possibility spaces to solve probability problems

Prerequisites

  • Systematic listing and product rule (P6)
  • Probability calculations and notation (P3, P4)
  • Fractions, decimals, percentages
  • Basic understanding of independent events

Materials & Equipment

  • Blank possibility space grids (6×6, 4×4, 6×4, etc.)
  • Blank two-way table templates
  • Dice (6-sided, 4-sided, 8-sided), spinners, coins
  • Coloured pencils for highlighting favourable outcomes
  • Mini whiteboards

Lesson 1: Possibility Space Diagrams

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Two Dice Totals

Roll two dice. What totals are possible? Which total is most likely? Students discuss, then reveal a 6×6 grid showing all 36 outcomes with sums.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Possibility Space Diagram (Sample Space Diagram): A grid showing all possible outcomes of a two-stage experiment. Rows = outcomes of first event, Columns = outcomes of second event. Each cell = combined outcome (ordered pair).
When to Use: Two independent events with small numbers of outcomes (dice, spinners, coins, drawing with replacement).
Key Features:
  • All cells equally likely IF the two events are fair and independent
  • Total cells = n₁ × n₂ (Product Rule)
  • Can write sums, products, differences, or just ordered pairs in cells
Constructing a Possibility Space:
  1. Identify the two events and their outcomes
  2. Draw a grid with appropriate dimensions
  3. Label rows and columns
  4. Fill in each cell with the combined outcome
  5. Check: total cells = product of outcome counts
Calculating Probabilities:
  • Count total cells (denominator)
  • Identify cells corresponding to the event
  • Count favourable cells (numerator)
  • P(Event) = Favourable / Total
  • Simplify fraction, convert if needed
Common Events to Find:
  • Sum = 7 (two dice)
  • Product > 10
  • Both even / both odd
  • At least one 6
  • Difference = 2
Worked Example

A fair 4-sided spinner (1-4) is spun twice. A possibility space diagram shows the 16 outcomes.
(a) Complete the diagram showing the SUM of the two spins.
(b) Find P(Sum = 5).
(c) Find P(Sum is even).
(d) Find P(Sum ≥ 6).

Answers:
(a) Grid with sums: row+col. Totals range 2 to 8.
(b) Sum=5: (1,4), (2,3), (3,2), (4,1) → 4/16 = 1/4
(c) Even sums: 2,4,6,8. Count: 1+3+5+3 = 12/16 = 3/4
(d) Sum≥6: 6,7,8. Count: 5+3+1 = 9/16

Common Misconception: "All sums are equally likely because there are 7 possible sums (2-8)." Correction: The INDIVIDUAL OUTCOMES (ordered pairs) are equally likely (each 1/16). But the SUMS are NOT equally likely. P(Sum=2) = 1/16, P(Sum=5) = 4/16. Always count cells, not distinct sum values.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide pre-drawn grids with row/column labels. Use colour-coding: highlight favourable cells in one colour. Start with coin+die (2×6=12) before two dice (6×6=36).
Stretch:
Non-standard dice: "Two dice numbered 1,2,2,3,3,4. Draw possibility space for sum." "A 6-sided die and a 4-sided spinner. Draw possibility space for product." Explore: "When are row/column outcomes NOT equally likely?" (biased dice/spinners).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Probability from Diagram

Show a completed 6×6 possibility space for two dice (showing products). Quick-fire: P(Product=12), P(Product is prime), P(Product > 20), P(Product is square). Students write answers on mini-whiteboards.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Constructs possibility spaces accurately; calculates probabilities for various events; understands equally likely cells vs non-equally likely combined results
  • Getting there: Constructs grids correctly but miscounts favourable cells; confuses "number of different sums" with "probability"
  • Not yet: Cannot set up grid; puts wrong outcomes in cells; adds probabilities incorrectly

Lesson 2: Two-Way Tables and Mixed Problems

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Two-Way Table Completion

Partially completed two-way table for "Gender vs Favourite Subject". Students fill in missing values using row/column totals. 50 students: 30 Male, 20 Female. 15 like Maths, 20 like English, 15 like Science. 10 Males like Maths. Complete the table.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Two-Way Tables: Organise data for two categorical variables. Rows = one variable, Columns = another. Cells = frequencies. Margins = row/column totals. Grand total = bottom right.
From Two-Way Table to Probability:
  • P(Row Category AND Column Category) = Cell Frequency / Grand Total
  • P(Row Category) = Row Total / Grand Total
  • P(Column Category) = Column Total / Grand Total
  • P(A or B) = (Row Total + Column Total - Overlap) / Grand Total
Probability from Possibility Space vs Two-Way Table:
  • Possibility space: theoretical, equally likely outcomes (e.g. two dice)
  • Two-way table: experimental/observed data (e.g. survey results)
  • Both use same probability logic: Favourable / Total
Conditional Probability Preview (Foundation: intuition only):
  • "Given that a student is Male, what's the probability they like Maths?"
  • Use row total as new denominator: P(Maths | Male) = Male&Maths / Total Male
  • Formal conditional probability notation P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B) is Higher (P9)
Mixed Problems: Questions may combine: possibility space for a game, then two-way table for tournament results.
Worked Example

100 students surveyed on Pet (Dog, Cat, None) and Sibling (Yes, No).
Two-way table:
| | Dog | Cat | None | Total |
| Yes | 25 | 15 | 20 | 60 |
| No | 10 | 5 | 25 | 40 |
| Total | 35 | 20 | 45 | 100 |
Find:
(a) P(Dog and Sibling)
(b) P(Cat | No Sibling)
(c) P(No Pet or Sibling)

Answers:
(a) 25/100 = 0.25
(b) Given No Sibling (row total 40): Cat = 5. P = 5/40 = 1/8 = 0.125
(c) No Pet = 45, Sibling = 60, Overlap (None & Yes) = 20. P = (45+60-20)/100 = 85/100 = 0.85

Common Misconception: Using the grand total as denominator for conditional probability. "P(Cat | No Sibling) = 5/100 = 0.05" Correction: "Given No Sibling" means we restrict to the "No" row. New total = 40. P = 5/40 = 0.125. The condition CHANGES the sample space.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide two-way tables with all margins filled in. Colour-code rows/columns. Use simpler numbers (total 50, 2×2 tables). Focus on "AND" and "OR" probabilities first.
Stretch:
Algebraic two-way tables: "Total = 100. P(Dog) = 0.4. P(Sibling) = 0.6. P(Dog and Sibling) = 0.3. Complete the table." Work backwards: "P(A|B) = 0.4, P(B) = 0.5, P(A) = 0.3. Find P(A∩B) and complete table."

Plenary (5 minutes)

Which Diagram?

Students decide: Possibility Space or Two-Way Table?
1. Two fair dice rolled, find P(sum=7) → Possibility Space
2. Survey of 200 people: Transport (Car/Bus/Walk) vs Distance (<5mi/>5mi) → Two-Way Table
3. Spinner (1-4) and Coin → Possibility Space
4. Exam results: Grade (9-1) vs Gender → Two-Way Table

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Constructs and interprets both diagrams; calculates joint, marginal, and conditional probabilities; chooses correct diagram for context
  • Getting there: Handles possibility spaces well but struggles with two-way table conditional probabilities
  • Not yet: Cannot complete two-way tables; uses wrong denominator for conditional probability

Homework Suggestions

  • Possibility space worksheet: 6 grids to complete and find probabilities (two dice, die+spinner, two spinners, coin+die, biased dice, sum/product/difference).
  • Two-way table worksheet: Complete 5 tables from partial data, calculate 10 probabilities including conditional.
  • Mixed exam questions: 5 past paper questions on possibility spaces and two-way tables.
  • Create your own: Design a two-stage experiment, draw its possibility space, write 5 probability questions with answers.
