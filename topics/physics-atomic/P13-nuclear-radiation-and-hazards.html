P13: Nuclear Radiation and Hazards

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understanding alpha, beta, and gamma radiation, their properties, uses, and dangers. Exploring half-life, background radiation, and the safe use of radioactive materials.

Lesson Overview
Estimated Lessons: 4
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the properties of alpha, beta, and gamma radiation
  • Explain what nuclear decay is and write decay equations
  • Define half-life and use it to calculate remaining activity
  • Describe the sources and dangers of background radiation
  • Evaluate the uses and hazards of radioactive materials

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of atomic structure (P12)
  • Knowledge of isotopes and notation
  • Basic understanding of safety and risk

Materials & Equipment

  • Scientific calculator
  • Simulation of radioactive decay (online or software)
  • Graph paper (for half-life graphs)

Lesson 1: Types of Radiation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Radiation Symbols

Show the three radiation symbols (α, β, γ). Ask students what they know about each type. Discuss any prior knowledge from chemistry or news.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Unstable nuclei release radiation to become more stable. This is radioactive decay — a random, spontaneous process.

Three types of nuclear radiation:

1. Alpha (α) radiation:
- What: 2 protons + 2 neutrons (= helium nucleus, 42He)
- Charge: +2
- Mass: 4
- Penetrating power: very low — stopped by paper or skin
- Ionising power: very high (most dangerous if source is inside the body)
- Range in air: a few cm

2. Beta (β) radiation:
- What: a fast-moving electron (or positron) emitted from the nucleus
- Charge: −1
- Mass: ~0 (1/1836 of a proton)
- Penetrating power: moderate — stopped by thin aluminium (a few mm)
- Ionising power: moderate
- Range in air: up to 1 m
- Created when a neutron turns into a proton (β⁻ decay) or a proton turns into a neutron (β⁺ decay)

3. Gamma (γ) radiation:
- What: electromagnetic waves (high-energy photons)
- Charge: 0
- Mass: 0
- Penetrating power: very high — reduced by thick lead or thick concrete
- Ionising power: low
- Range in air: unlimited (but intensity decreases with distance)
- Always emitted with alpha or beta radiation
Worked Example

A radioactive source emits all three types of radiation. It is placed behind three barriers: paper, aluminium, and lead.
- Radiation detected behind paper but not behind aluminium: alpha (stopped by paper)
- Radiation detected behind aluminium but not behind lead: beta (stopped by aluminium)
- Radiation detected behind lead: gamma (penetrates aluminium, reduced by lead)

Key Summary:
Alpha: 42He, stopped by paper, most ionising
Beta: electron, stopped by aluminium, moderate ionising
Gamma: electromagnetic wave, reduced by lead, least ionising
Common Misconception: Students think alpha is "the most dangerous" radiation. Alpha is most dangerous when the source is inside the body (ingested/inhaled), but outside the body it is stopped by skin and is relatively safe.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a table with columns for each property of radiation. Students fill in the table for all three types.
Stretch:
Explain why hospitals use gamma radiation for sterilising equipment rather than alpha or beta. Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of each type for medical use.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Penetration Ranking

Students rank alpha, beta, and gamma by: penetration power, ionising power, and danger outside the body. Compare rankings.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe all properties of alpha, beta, and gamma radiation and compare them accurately
  • Getting there: Can describe some properties but confuses penetration and ionising power
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between the three types of radiation

Lesson 2: Nuclear Decay Equations

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Conservation Check

Students check whether these equations are balanced: 23892U → 23490Th + ? (Answer: 42He, alpha decay)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Nuclear decay equations must be balanced: mass number and atomic number must be the same on both sides.

Types of decay:

Alpha decay:
AZX → A−4Z−2Y + 42He
Mass number decreases by 4, atomic number decreases by 2

Beta (β⁻) decay:
AZX → AZ+1Y + 0−1e
Mass number stays the same, atomic number increases by 1
(Neutron → proton + electron)

Gamma emission:
AZX* → AZX + γ
Nucleus in excited state releases energy. Mass and atomic number unchanged.

Background radiation:
Radiation that is always present in the environment, from:
- Natural sources: radon gas from rocks, cosmic rays from space, radioactive rocks and soil
- Man-made sources: medical X-rays, nuclear weapons testing, nuclear accidents (Chernobyl, Fukushima), nuclear power stations

Measuring radiation:
- Use a Geiger-Müller tube and counter
- Measure count rate (counts per second)
- Subtract background radiation to get the source's count rate
- Activity is measured in becquerels (Bq): 1 Bq = 1 decay per second
Worked Example

Write the decay equation for carbon-14 beta decay:
146C → 147N + 0−1e

Check: Mass: 14 = 14 + 0 ✓
Atomic number: 6 = 7 + (−1) ✓

Decay Equations:
Alpha: AZX → A−4Z−2Y + 42He
Beta: AZX → AZ+1Y + 0−1e
Gamma: AZX* → AZX + γ

Differentiation

Support:
Provide partially completed decay equations. Students fill in the missing particles or numbers.
Stretch:
Complete the decay chain for uranium-238 through to lead-206 (alpha and beta decays). Calculate the total number of alpha and beta decays.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Equations

Students write decay equations for: (a) radon-222 alpha decay (b) iodine-131 beta decay (c) cobalt-60 gamma emission

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can write balanced nuclear equations for all three types of decay and identify background radiation sources
  • Getting there: Can write alpha and beta equations but struggles with gamma or balancing
  • Not yet: Cannot write nuclear decay equations or identify radiation types

Lesson 3: Half-Life

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Prediction

"A radioactive source has a count rate of 800 counts/s. After one half-life, what will the count rate be?" Students predict and explain their reasoning.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Half-life is the time taken for half the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay. Equivalently, it is the time for the activity (count rate) to halve.

Key points:
- Half-life is different for each radioactive isotope
- Radioactive decay is random — we cannot predict when a single nucleus will decay
- But we can predict how many nuclei will decay over time (statistical)
- The activity decreases exponentially (never reaches zero)

Examples of half-lives:
- Carbon-14: 5730 years (used for dating ancient objects)
- Iodine-131: 8 days (used in medical treatment)
- Uranium-238: 4.5 billion years (used for dating rocks)
- Plutonium-239: 24 100 years (nuclear waste problem)

Calculating remaining activity:
After n half-lives: Activity = Original activity × (1/2)ⁿ

Graph of activity against time:
- Starts at the original activity
- Halves at each half-life
- Approaches zero but never reaches it (exponential decay curve)
- The gradient is steepest at the start (most decays per second)
Worked Example

A source has initial activity 6400 Bq and half-life 10 minutes. What is the activity after 40 minutes?
Number of half-lives = 40 ÷ 10 = 4
Activity = 6400 × (1/2)⁴ = 6400 × 1/16 = 400 Bq

After each half-life: 6400 → 3200 → 1600 → 800 → 400 Bq

Half-Life Formula:
Remaining activity = Original activity × (1/2)ⁿ
where n = number of half-lives elapsed
Common Misconception: Students think all nuclei decay at exactly the same rate. Half-life is a statistical concept — it describes the average behaviour of a large number of nuclei, not individual nuclei.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a step-by-step template: (1) calculate number of half-lives, (2) apply the formula. Students practise with five examples.
Stretch:
A sample has activity 1000 Bq and half-life 5 years. How long until the activity falls below 10 Bq? (Answer: about 33 years, since 1000 × (1/2)ⁿ < 10 → n > 6.64 → 33.2 years)

Plenary (5 minutes)

Half-Life Table

Students complete a table showing the activity of a source (starting at 3200 Bq) after each half-life up to 6 half-lives.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can calculate remaining activity after any number of half-lives and draw/interpret decay graphs
  • Getting there: Can calculate activity after whole number of half-lives but struggles with non-whole numbers
  • Not yet: Cannot apply the half-life formula or understand exponential decay

Lesson 4: Uses and Hazards of Radiation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Risk Assessment

Show images of: smoke detector, medical scanner, nuclear power station, irradiated food. Ask: "Which of these uses radioactive materials? How are they used safely?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Uses of radioactive materials:

1. Medical uses:
- Gamma: sterilising medical equipment (kills bacteria without heat damage)
- Gamma: radiotherapy (treating cancer — targeted radiation destroys cancer cells)
- Iodine-131: treating overactive thyroid (absorbed by thyroid gland)
- Technetium-99m: medical imaging (attached to a tracer, detected by gamma camera)

2. Industrial uses:
- Gamma: detecting cracks in metal pipes (gamma rays pass through metal, crack shows on film)
- Gamma: measuring thickness of materials in factories (gauge measures how much radiation passes through)
- Smoke detectors: americium-241 emits alpha particles that ionise air; smoke disrupts the current

3. Scientific uses:
- Carbon-14 dating: measuring age of archaeological finds (up to 50 000 years)
- Uranium-238 dating: measuring age of rocks (billions of years)
- Tracers: radioactive atoms added to a system to track flow (e.g., in pipes, in the body)

Hazards and safety:
The three factors affecting danger:
1. Type of radiation: alpha is most dangerous inside the body; gamma penetrates the body
2. Half-life: long half-life means the source stays dangerous for longer
3. Activity: higher activity means more decays per second

Safety precautions (ALARP principle):
- As low as reasonably practicable
- Keep distance from source (intensity ∝ 1/d²)
- Use shielding (lead aprons, lead-lined containers)
- Minimise time near source
- Store sources in thick lead containers
- Never handle radioactive sources directly
- Monitor exposure with film badges
Worked Example

A factory uses a gamma source to measure the thickness of paper. If the paper is too thin, more gamma rays pass through and the detector gives a higher reading. If the paper is too thick, fewer gamma rays pass through.
(a) Why is gamma used rather than alpha? Gamma penetrates the paper; alpha would be stopped by the paper regardless of thickness.
(b) Why is this a safe use? The source is sealed in a container, workers maintain distance, and the source has a suitable half-life.

ALARP Principle:
As Low As Reasonably Practicable
Three safety measures: Distance, Shielding, Time

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a matching activity: match the use of radiation to the type used and the reason why that type is suitable.
Stretch:
Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of nuclear power as an energy source. Write a balanced argument considering energy output, waste, safety, and environmental impact.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

"Explain why gamma radiation is used to sterilise medical equipment rather than alpha or beta radiation." Students write a 3-mark answer.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe at least four uses of radiation and explain safety precautions using the ALARP principle
  • Getting there: Can list uses but cannot explain why specific radiation types are chosen for specific uses
  • Not yet: Cannot connect radiation types to practical applications or safety measures

Homework Suggestions

  • Calculate the remaining activity of a source (initial: 5000 Bq, half-life: 3 years) after 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 years. Plot a decay graph.
  • Research carbon-14 dating and write a 200-word explanation of how it works
  • Investigate the Chernobyl or Fukushima nuclear disaster. Write a summary of what happened and the long-term effects
  • Create a safety poster for a laboratory that uses radioactive sources
