P14: Nuclear Fission and Fusion

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understanding how energy is released from nuclei by splitting heavy atoms (fission) and joining light atoms (fusion). Exploring nuclear power and energy production in stars.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the process of nuclear fission
  • Explain how a chain reaction works and the role of control rods
  • Describe the process of nuclear fusion
  • Explain why fusion requires extremely high temperatures
  • Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of nuclear power

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of atomic structure and isotopes (P12)
  • Knowledge of nuclear radiation (P13)
  • Basic understanding of energy stores (P1)

Materials & Equipment

  • Scientific calculator
  • Dominoes (for chain reaction simulation)
  • Computer/tablet for simulations

Lesson 1: Nuclear Fission

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Domino Chain

Set up a line of dominoes. Knock over the first one and watch the chain reaction. Ask: "How is this similar to what happens in a nuclear reactor?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Nuclear fission is the splitting of a heavy nucleus into two lighter nuclei, releasing energy.

How fission works:
1. A slow-moving (thermal) neutron is absorbed by a heavy nucleus (e.g., uranium-235)
2. The nucleus becomes unstable and splits into two smaller nuclei (fission fragments)
3. Two or three neutrons are released
4. A large amount of energy is released
5. The released neutrons can trigger further fission events → chain reaction

The chain reaction:
- If at least one neutron from each fission causes another fission → the reaction is self-sustaining
- Control rods (e.g., boron or cadmium) absorb excess neutrons to control the rate
- Moderator (e.g., water or graphite) slows down neutrons so they are more likely to be absorbed
- If too many neutrons cause further fissions → uncontrolled chain reaction → nuclear explosion

Nuclear reactor components:
- Fuel rods: uranium-235 or plutonium-239 (fuel for fission)
- Control rods: boron or cadmium (absorb neutrons, control rate)
- Moderator: water or graphite (slows down neutrons)
- Coolant: water or gas (carries heat away to generate electricity)
- Containment: thick concrete and steel (prevents radiation escape)

Energy released:
In fission, a small amount of mass is converted to a large amount of energy (E = mc²). The total mass of the products is slightly less than the mass of the original nucleus and neutron. This "missing mass" has been converted to energy.
Worked Example

In the fission of uranium-235:
23592U + 10n → 23692U* → 14456Ba + 8936Kr + 310n

Check: Mass: 235 + 1 = 236 → 144 + 89 + 3 = 236 ✓
Atomic number: 92 + 0 = 92 → 56 + 36 + 0 = 92 ✓

Three neutrons are released, each of which can cause further fissions → chain reaction.

Fission Equation Example:
23592U + 10n → 14456Ba + 8936Kr + 310n + energy
Common Misconception: Students think nuclear power stations can explode like nuclear bombs. They cannot — reactors are designed so the chain reaction is controlled. An uncontrolled chain reaction is physically impossible in a reactor due to design limitations.

Differentiation

Support:
Use dominoes to simulate a chain reaction. Students identify where control rods would be placed to slow the reaction.
Stretch:
Calculate the energy released when 1 kg of uranium-235 undergoes complete fission. Compare this to burning 1 kg of coal.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Reactor Components

Students label a diagram of a nuclear reactor, identifying: fuel rods, control rods, moderator, coolant, and containment.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe fission, the chain reaction, and the role of each reactor component
  • Getting there: Can describe fission but cannot explain the chain reaction or reactor components
  • Not yet: Cannot describe the fission process or distinguish fission from fusion

Lesson 2: Nuclear Fusion

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Fusion vs Fission

Students compare fusion and fission: "In fission, a heavy nucleus splits. What do you think happens in fusion?" Discuss predictions before revealing the answer.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Nuclear fusion is the joining of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy.

How fusion works:
1. Two light nuclei (e.g., hydrogen isotopes) are forced together
2. They must overcome the electrostatic repulsion between their positive charges
3. When close enough, the strong nuclear force binds them together
4. The mass of the product nucleus is less than the sum of the original masses
5. This mass difference is converted to energy (E = mc²)

Why fusion requires extreme conditions:
- Nuclei are positively charged and repel each other (electrostatic repulsion)
- They must be moving very fast to overcome this repulsion
- Temperatures of about 10 million °C are needed (similar to the Sun's core)
- At these temperatures, matter exists as plasma (ionised gas)

Fusion in stars:
- The Sun fuses hydrogen into helium in its core
- The proton-proton chain: 4 hydrogen nuclei → 1 helium nucleus + 2 positrons + energy
- The Sun converts 600 million tonnes of hydrogen into helium every second
- A small amount of mass (4 million tonnes per second) is converted to energy

Current fusion research:
- ITER (International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor) in France
- Uses magnetic confinement (tokamak) to contain plasma
- Goal: achieve net energy output from fusion
- Advantages: fuel (hydrogen) is abundant, no long-lived radioactive waste, no risk of meltdown
- Challenge: sustaining the extreme conditions efficiently
Worked Example

The fusion reaction in the Sun:
411H → 42He + 20+1e + energy

Check: Mass number: 4 × 1 = 4 → 4 + 0 = 4 ✓
Atomic number: 4 × 1 = 4 → 2 + 2(1) = 4 ✓

Four hydrogen nuclei (protons) fuse to form one helium nucleus. Two positrons (β⁺) are emitted. The mass of the helium nucleus is less than the total mass of the four protons — this mass difference is released as energy.

Fusion Equation (Sun):
411H → 42He + 20+1e + energy
Common Misconception: Students think fusion is the same as fission. Fission splits heavy nuclei; fusion joins light nuclei. Both release energy, but through different processes.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a comparison table of fission and fusion. Students fill in: what happens, fuel used, conditions needed, products, and applications.
Stretch:
Research ITER and explain how a tokamak uses magnetic fields to confine plasma. Why is this approach more promising than previous fusion experiments?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

"Compare nuclear fission and nuclear fusion. In your answer, explain what happens to the nuclei and why energy is released in both processes." Students write a structured comparison.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe fusion, explain the conditions needed, and compare fission and fusion accurately
  • Getting there: Can describe fusion but cannot explain why extreme temperatures are needed
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between fission and fusion or explain either process

Homework Suggestions

  • Compare fission and fusion in a table: process, fuel, conditions, products, advantages, disadvantages
  • Research the ITER project and write a 200-word summary of its goals and current progress
  • Calculate the energy released when 1 kg of hydrogen undergoes complete fusion (given E = mc² and mass defect data)
  • Write a balanced argument for and against building new nuclear power stations in the UK
