Lesson 2: Core Concepts: radio & podcasts

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on radio & podcasts. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Radio is a broadcast medium that uses sound codes (speech, music, sound effects, silence) to communicate with audiences. It is immediate, intimate, and portable.
Key Fact: BBC Radio operates under public service obligations, providing distinctive programming for diverse audiences. BBC Radio 1 targets young audiences; Radio 4 targets older, educated listeners.
Key Fact: Commercial radio (Global, Bauer) is funded by advertising. It targets specific demographics to maximise ad revenue — e.g. Heart targets 25-44 women, LBC targets news-interested adults.
Key Fact: Radio conventions: scheduling, jingles, station identification, news bulletins, phone-ins, playlists, presenters, and audience interaction (texts, calls, social media).
Key Fact: Podcasting has transformed audio media: audiences can listen on demand, content is more niche, production costs are lower, and anyone can create a podcast.
Key Fact: Key differences between radio and podcasting: radio is scheduled and live; podcasts are on-demand and pre-recorded. Radio is regulated by Ofcom; podcasts are largely unregulated.

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Compare radio and podcasting as media forms. [12 marks] <div class="

Radio and podcasting share the audio medium but differ significantly as media forms in production, distribution, regulation, and audience experience. Radio is a scheduled, live broadcast medium. Programmes air at fixed times, creating shared listening experiences (watercooler moments). BBC radio operates under public service obligations, providing diverse programming. Commercial radio targets demographics for advertisers. Radio is regulated by Ofcom, which ensures content standards and due impartiality. Podcasting is an on-demand, pre-recorded medium. Listeners choose what to hear and when, creating personalised experiences. Production barriers are low — anyone can create a podcast — enabling diverse, niche content. Podcasts are largely unregulated, allowing more freedom but less protection. The audience relationship differs. Radio presenters build parasocial relationships through daily scheduling, becoming companions in listeners' routines. Podcasts build intimacy through deep, long-form content and the sense of private, one-to-one communication that headphones create. Convergence is blurring the boundaries. BBC programmes are released as podcasts, and radio stations stream online

Exam Tips: Distinguish clearly between BBC (public service) and commercial radio | Use sound codes terminology when analysing radio CSPs | Discuss the regulatory context (Ofcom) for broadcast radio
