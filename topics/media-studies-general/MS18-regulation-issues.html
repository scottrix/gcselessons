Lesson 2: Core Concepts: regulation & issues

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on regulation & issues. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Media regulation involves rules and systems that govern what media content can be produced, distributed, and accessed. It aims to protect audiences while preserving freedom of expression.
Key Fact: Ofcom regulates UK broadcasting — setting content standards, protecting audiences from harmful material, ensuring due impartiality in news, and managing the radio/TV spectrum.
Key Fact: The BBFC classifies films and some video content (U, PG, 12A, 12, 15, 18). Classification considers discrimination, drugs, horror, imitable behaviour, language, nudity, sex, threat, and violence.
Key Fact: IPSO (Independent Press Standards Organisation) regulates most UK newspapers and magazines. It handles complaints and enforces the Editors' Code of Practice, but has been criticised as weak.
Key Fact: IMPRESS is an alternative press regulator recognised under the Royal Charter. It has fewer members than IPSO but stronger powers and independence.
Key Fact: The ASA (Advertising Standards Authority) regulates advertising across all media. Adverts must be legal, decent, honest, and truthful. The CAP Code covers non-broadcast ads; BCAP Code covers broadcast.

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Evaluate the effectiveness of media regulation in the UK. [15 marks] <div class="

UK media regulation involves multiple bodies covering different media forms, creating a comprehensive but fragmented system. Ofcom is generally effective in regulating broadcasting — it has statutory powers, can impose significant fines, and ensures content standards and due impartiality. Its decisions are enforceable and respected. However, it faces challenges with streaming services that are not always subject to the same rules as traditional broadcasters. The BBFC classification system is well-understood and widely followed. Age ratings help audiences make informed choices, and cinema enforcement is generally effective. However, online access to classified content is harder to control. Press regulation remains the weakest area. IPSO is self-regulatory and funded by the industry it regulates, which compromises its independence. It cannot initiate investigations without complaints and has limited sanctioning powers. The Leveson Inquiry's recommendations for a stronger, independent regulator were not fully implemented. The ASA effectively regulates advertising through pre-clearance systems and complaint handling, but the rise of influencer marketing and native advertising challenge

Exam Tips: Know the regulatory bodies and what they regulate: Ofcom (broadcasting), BBFC (film), IPSO/IMPRESS (press), ASA (advertising) | Always discuss the tension between protection and freedom | Use specific regulatory examples from CSPs
Common Errors: ✗ All media regulation is censorship. ✓ Regulation and censorship are different. Regulation sets standards (age ratings, impartiality rules) while allowing content to be published. Censorship prevents content from being published at all. The UK primarily uses regulation, not censorship. ✗ IPSO is a government body. ✓ IPSO is a self-regulatory body set up by the newspaper industry, not a government regulator. It is funded by the publications it regulates, which critics argue compromises its independence. ✗ Online content is completely unregulated. ✓ The Online Safety Act 2023 regulates platforms, the ASA regulates online advertising, and existing laws (defamation, hate speech) apply online.
