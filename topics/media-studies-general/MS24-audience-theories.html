Lesson 2: Core Concepts: audience theories & debates

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on audience theories & debates. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Audience theories attempt to explain the relationship between media products and audiences — how media affects people and how people use media.
Key Fact: Hypodermic needle model (1920s-1930s): media injects ideas directly into passive audiences, who are uniformly affected. Now largely discredited but still influences public concern about media effects.
Key Fact: Two-step flow model (Katz and Lazarsfeld, 1955): media messages pass through opinion leaders (influential people) who interpret and pass them on to wider audiences. More nuanced than the hypodermic model.
Key Fact: Uses and Gratifications theory (Blumler and Katz, 1974): audiences actively choose media to fulfil four needs — information/education, entertainment, social interaction, and personal identity.
Key Fact: Reception theory (Stuart Hall, 1980): audiences decode media texts in three ways — preferred reading (accepting the intended meaning), negotiated reading (partially accepting), and oppositional reading (rejecting).
Key Fact: Cultivation theory (Gerbner): long-term exposure to media gradually shapes perceptions of reality — e.g. heavy TV viewers overestimate the amount of violence in society (the 'mean world syndrome').

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Evaluate the usefulness of audience theories in understanding the relationship between media and audiences. [15 marks] <div class="

Audience theories provide valuable frameworks for understanding how media and audiences interact, but each has significant limitations. The hypodermic needle model is historically important but largely discredited. It assumes audiences are passive and uniformly affected, which contradicts evidence of diverse audience responses. However, it persists in public discourse — concerns about media effects (violence in games, social media and mental health) echo the model's assumptions. Uses and gratifications theory offers a more sophisticated view, recognising that audiences actively choose media to fulfil needs. This explains why different audiences select different media products and how the same product can serve different purposes. However, it has been criticised for overstating audience rationality — much media consumption is habitual, not carefully chosen. Hall's reception theory adds nuance by showing how audiences decode texts differently based on cultural position. This explains why the same product generates different responses — preferred, negotiated, and oppositional readings. More nuanced theories (uses and gratifications, reception theory) better explain how audiences actively interpret and use media. ✗ Uses and gratifications means media has no effect on audiences. ✓ Uses and gratifications shows audiences are active in choosing media, but this doesn't mean media has no effect.
