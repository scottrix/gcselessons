Lesson 2: Core Concepts: video games

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on video games. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: Video games are an interactive media form where players actively participate in constructing the narrative and experience, unlike passive media forms.
Key Fact: Game genres: action, RPG, FPS, sports, puzzle, simulation, adventure, strategy, horror, MMO. Genres set player expectations and guide marketing.
Key Fact: Platforms: console (PlayStation, Xbox, Nintendo), PC, mobile, and cloud gaming. Each platform has different conventions, audiences, and business models.
Key Fact: Industry structure: AAA (major studio) games have budgets of $100m+; indie games are made by small teams with limited budgets. The industry generates more revenue than film and music combined.
Key Fact: Representation in games: historically stereotyped (male heroes, sexualised female characters, underrepresented minorities). Recent games have diversified, but problems persist.
Key Fact: PEGI (Pan European Game Information) classifies games by age rating (3, 7, 12, 16, 18) and content descriptors (violence, fear, drugs, bad language).

Extended question: Full-Mark Response Evaluate how video games represent social groups. Refer to your CSPs. [12 marks] <div class="

Video games have historically stereotyped social groups, but contemporary games increasingly offer complex, diverse representations. Gender representation has been particularly problematic. Female characters have been underrepresented as protagonists and overrepresented as sexualised objects or passive supporting characters. The 'male gaze' (Mulvey) is evident in character design that emphasises physical attractiveness over personality or agency. However, recent games feature strong female protagonists (e.g. Aloy in Horizon, Ellie in The Last of Us) who drive the narrative and are not defined by their appearance. Ethnic representation also has a troubled history. Non-white characters have been marginalised, stereotyped, or absent. When included, they are often supporting characters or villains. Recent progress includes more diverse leads, but the industry's workforce remains predominantly white and male, affecting whose stories are told. LGBTQ+ representation is growing but still limited.
