C1: Atoms and Elements

Foundation All Boards

Understand the structure of atoms, the role of subatomic particles, isotopes, electron arrangements, and how elements are defined by their atomic number.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the structure of an atom including protons, neutrons and electrons
  • Explain what isotopes are and describe electron arrangements in atoms
  • Read and interpret the periodic table to find atomic number, mass number and relative atomic mass

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of matter from Key Stage 2 science

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Printed periodic table (A3 size recommended)
  • Coloured pens/highlighters for marking metals vs non-metals

Lesson 1: Atomic Structure

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

What Do You Already Know?

Ask children to draw what they think an atom looks like. Discuss: what particles make up an atom? Where are they found?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
An atom is made of three types of subatomic particle:

Protons — found in the nucleus, mass 1, charge +1
Neutrons — found in the nucleus, mass 1, charge 0
Electrons — orbit the nucleus in shells, mass ~0 (negligible), charge -1

The nucleus is tiny compared to the whole atom. If an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be the size of a marble in the centre. Most of the atom is empty space.

Key definitions:
• Atomic number = number of protons (this defines the element)
• Mass number = number of protons + neutrons
• Number of neutrons = mass number − atomic number
• Atoms have no overall charge (equal protons and electrons)
Carbon atom

Carbon has atomic number 6 and mass number 12. So it has 6 protons, 6 electrons, and 12 − 6 = 6 neutrons.

Chlorine atom

Chlorine has atomic number 17 and mass number 35.5 (average). A chlorine-35 atom has 17 protons, 17 electrons, and 35 − 17 = 18 neutrons.

Mass number = Protons + Neutrons
Number of neutrons = Mass number − Atomic number

Notation: massatomic numberX
e.g. 126C for carbon-12

Common Misconception

Electrons orbit the nucleus in neat circular paths like planets orbit the Sun.

Electrons occupy probability clouds (orbitals) described by quantum mechanics. At GCSE, the simplified shell model is used — electrons fill energy levels starting from the innermost shell.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a simplified periodic table with atomic and mass numbers clearly labelled. Use colour-coded diagrams showing protons (red), neutrons (blue), electrons (yellow).
Stretch:
Research isotopes of carbon (C-12, C-13, C-14) and explain why they are all carbon but have different masses. How is C-14 used in carbon dating?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Give children an element symbol with atomic and mass numbers. They must calculate protons, neutrons, electrons.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Correctly identifies protons, neutrons, electrons and calculates quantities from atomic/mass numbers.
  • Getting there: Can identify particles but occasionally confuses atomic and mass numbers.
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between subatomic particles. Reteach with physical model (e.g. cake with cherries as protons/neutrons and sprinkles as electrons).

Lesson 2: Isotopes and Electrons

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Isotope Puzzle

Show two cards: Carbon-12 and Carbon-14. Ask: what is the same? What is different? Both have 6 protons — so why different mass numbers?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. They have the same atomic number but different mass numbers. Because they have the same number of electrons, isotopes have identical chemical properties.

Electron arrangement:
Electrons occupy energy levels (shells) around the nucleus:
• Shell 1 (closest to nucleus): holds up to 2 electrons
• Shell 2: holds up to 8 electrons
• Shell 3: holds up to 8 electrons (for GCSE elements up to atomic number 20)

The arrangement is written as: 2, 8, 1 for sodium (11 electrons).

Relative masses:
Proton = 1, Neutron = 1, Electron = 1/1836 (negligible)
Chlorine isotopes

Chlorine-35 has 17 protons, 18 neutrons. Chlorine-37 has 17 protons, 20 neutrons. Both are chlorine because they have 17 protons. Chlorine's relative atomic mass of 35.5 is a weighted average of these isotopes.

Electron arrangement of calcium

Calcium has 20 electrons. Arrangement: 2, 8, 8, 2. The outer shell has 2 electrons — this determines its chemical behaviour.

Electron arrangement: Shell 1 max 2, Shell 2 max 8, Shell 3 max 8
Relative atomic mass = weighted average of isotope masses
e.g. Ar(Cl) = (75 × 35 + 25 × 37) / 100 = 35.5

Common Misconception

Chlorine has atomic mass 35.5, so a single chlorine atom weighs 35.5 amu.

No single atom has a fractional mass. 35.5 is the weighted average of chlorine's two naturally occurring isotopes (Cl-35 and Cl-37).

Differentiation

Support:
Use physical tokens to represent electrons filling shells. Start with hydrogen (2,1) and build up to calcium (2,8,8,2).
Stretch:
Research why fluorine has only one stable isotope (F-19) while tin has ten stable isotopes. What determines isotope stability?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Given an element's electron arrangement (e.g. 2,8,7), identify the element and predict its group number.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains isotopes clearly and writes correct electron arrangements for elements 1-20.
  • Getting there: Understands isotopes but makes errors in electron arrangements beyond shell 2.
  • Not yet: Confuses isotopes with ions. Reteach with clear distinction: same element, different neutrons = isotope; same element, different electrons = ion.

Lesson 3: Reading the Periodic Table

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Periodic Table Detective

Give each child a different element tile from a periodic table. What information can they find on it? Can they identify the element's group and period?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The periodic table organises elements by atomic number (increasing from left to right, top to bottom).

Each element tile typically shows:
• Atomic number (top or centre)
• Symbol (one or two letters — first capital, second lowercase)
• Name
• Relative atomic mass (bottom — weighted average of all naturally occurring isotopes)

Groups are vertical columns (1-18). Elements in the same group have the same number of outer electrons and similar chemical properties.

Periods are horizontal rows. Each period represents a new electron shell being filled.

Metals are on the left and centre. Non-metals are on the right. The staircase line separates them.

Naming symbols: Some symbols come from Latin names — Na (sodium, natrium), Fe (iron, ferrum), Cu (copper, cuprum), Ag (silver, argentum), Au (gold, aurum), Pb (lead, plumbum), Hg (mercury, hydrargyrum), K (potassium, kalium), W (tungsten, wolfram).
Reading iron's tile

Fe: Atomic number 26, mass number 56 (rounded). Symbol from Latin 'ferrum'. Iron is in Group 8, Period 4. It is a transition metal.

Finding an element

If an element has atomic number 11, find box number 11 on the periodic table. It is sodium (Na). Mass number ≈ 23. Electron arrangement: 2, 8, 1.

Common Misconception

Elements in the same row (period) have similar chemical properties.

Elements in the same column (group) have similar properties because they have the same number of outer electrons. Periods represent the number of electron shells.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a large wall poster of the periodic table. Colour-code metals, non-metals, and the staircase line. Use highlighters to mark groups.
Stretch:
Research the history of Mendeleev's periodic table. Why did he leave gaps? How did he predict undiscovered elements?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Show a periodic table with some elements removed. Children must identify which element is missing from each gap using the pattern.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Confidently reads any element tile and identifies group, period, and whether it is a metal or non-metal.
  • Getting there: Can read basic information but struggles with group/period identification.
  • Not yet: Cannot navigate the periodic table. Reteach with a simplified version showing only elements 1-20.

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a poster of the first 20 elements with electron arrangements
  • Learn the Latin name symbols (Na, Fe, Cu, Ag, Au, Pb, Hg, K)
  • Practise calculating protons, neutrons and electrons from atomic/mass numbers
