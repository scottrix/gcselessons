C4: Mixtures and Separation Techniques

Foundation All Boards

Distinguish between elements, compounds and mixtures, and learn practical separation techniques including filtration, distillation and chromatography.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish between elements, compounds and mixtures at a particle level
  • Describe and explain the main methods used to separate mixtures
  • Understand chromatography and its applications

Prerequisites

  • Atoms and Elements (C1) — elements, atoms, atomic number

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Filter paper and funnels
  • Beakers and evaporating dishes
  • Bunsen burner or hob for evaporation
  • Sand, salt, and water for demonstration
  • Iron filings and sulfur for mixture demo

Lesson 1: Elements, Compounds and Mixtures

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

What's in the Mixture?

Show a jar containing iron filings and sand mixed together. Can the children separate them? How? This leads into why separation techniques matter.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Elements contain only one type of atom. They cannot be broken down by chemical means. Examples: iron (Fe), oxygen (O₂), gold (Au).

Compounds contain two or more different elements chemically bonded together in a fixed ratio. They can be broken down by chemical reactions. Examples: water (H₂O), sodium chloride (NaCl), carbon dioxide (CO₂).

Mixtures contain two or more substances that are not chemically bonded. They can be separated by physical methods. The components retain their own properties. Examples: air (mixture of gases), seawater (salt + water), alloys like brass (copper + zinc — a mixture, not a compound).

Key difference: Compounds have different properties from their elements. Mixtures have properties that are a combination of the components.

Particle diagrams:
• Element: one type of particle only
• Compound: two or more types chemically bonded (different particles touching)
• Mixture: two or more types not bonded (particles mixed but separate)
Water vs salt water

Pure water (compound) boils at exactly 100°C. Salt water (mixture) boils at a higher temperature and leaves salt behind when evaporated. The salt and water can be separated by evaporation.

Iron and sulfur mixture vs compound

Iron + sulfur mixture: can be separated with a magnet (iron is attracted, sulfur is not). Iron sulfide (compound, FeS): cannot be separated by a magnet — it is a new substance with different properties.

Element → one type of atom
Compound → two+ elements chemically bonded in fixed ratio
Mixture → two+ substances physically mixed, not bonded

Key test: Can you separate it by physical methods? Yes = mixture. No = compound.

Common Misconception

Alloys like brass are compounds because they contain two metals.

Brass is a mixture (an alloy) of copper and zinc. The metals are mixed but not chemically bonded in a fixed ratio. You can vary the proportions of copper and zinc to make different types of brass with different properties.

Differentiation

Support:
Use particle diagrams drawn on the board. Colour-code: red for one element, blue for another. Show element (all red), compound (red-blue pairs), mixture (red and blue separate).
Stretch:
Research: why is bronze harder than pure copper? Explain in terms of layer structure and different-sized atoms disrupting the regular arrangement.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Give a list of substances (air, rust, bronze, CO₂, seawater). Classify each as element, compound or mixture. Justify each answer.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Correctly classifies substances and explains the differences using particle diagrams and bonding.
  • Getting there: Can classify simple cases but struggles with alloys and impure substances.
  • Not yet: Confuses compounds and mixtures. Reteach with physical demonstrations: make iron sulfide (compound) vs mixing iron and sulfur (mixture).

Lesson 2: Filtration, Evaporation and Distillation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Real-World Separation

Ask: how do you think water companies remove salt from seawater? How do oil companies separate crude oil into petrol, diesel etc.? These are separation problems.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Filtration — separates insoluble solids from liquids.
Method: Pour mixture through filter paper in a funnel. Solid stays on paper (residue), liquid passes through (filtrate).
Example: Sand from water, chalk from water.

Evaporation — separates a soluble solid from a liquid.
Method: Heat the solution until the liquid evaporates, leaving solid crystals behind.
Example: Salt from salty water.

Distillation — separates a liquid from a solution (or separates liquids with different boiling points).
Method: Heat solution, liquid evaporates, vapour passes through condenser (cooled by cold water), pure liquid collected.
Example: Pure water from salty water (desalination).

Simple distillation vs fractional distillation:
• Simple: one liquid collected
• Fractional: separates a mixture of liquids with different boiling points using a fractionating column
Example: Separating crude oil into fractions (petrol, diesel, etc.)
Separating sand and salt

1. Dissolve mixture in water (salt dissolves, sand doesn't). 2. Filter (sand stays on paper, salt solution passes through). 3. Evaporate filtrate to get salt crystals.

Obtaining pure water from seawater

Distillation: heat seawater → water evaporates → condenses on cool surface → collected as pure water. Salt remains in the flask. This is desalination.

Filtration: insoluble solid + liquid → solid on filter paper + filtrate
Evaporation: solution → heat → solid + gas (lost)
Distillation: solution → heat → vapour → cool → pure liquid
Fractional distillation: mixture of liquids → heat → different fractions collected at different temperatures

Common Misconception

Distillation and evaporation are the same thing.

Evaporation leaves the solid behind and the liquid is lost (or collected if covered). Distillation collects the liquid (as distillate) and leaves the solid behind. Distillation is used when you want the liquid; evaporation is used when you want the solid.

Differentiation

Support:
Use a flowchart: 'What state is the substance you want?' Solid → filtration or evaporation. Liquid → distillation.
Stretch:
Research: how does a fractionating column work in oil refining? Why do different fractions come off at different heights?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Given a mixture description, choose the correct separation technique and justify why.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Selects and explains appropriate separation techniques for any mixture described.
  • Getting there: Knows the techniques but sometimes selects the wrong one for unfamiliar mixtures.
  • Not yet: Cannot link mixture type to separation method. Reteach with practical demonstrations of each technique.

Lesson 3: Chromatography

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Ink Detective

Show a black felt-tip pen mark on filter paper. What do you think will happen if you add a drop of water below the mark? Will it stay black or split into colours?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Paper chromatography separates mixtures based on how soluble each component is in a solvent.

Method:
1. Draw a pencil line near the bottom of filter paper (pencil doesn't dissolve — pen ink would)
2. Spot the ink/food colouring on the line
3. Dip the paper in a solvent (e.g. water) — solvent level below the pencil line
4. Solvent moves up the paper by capillary action
5. Different components travel at different speeds (more soluble = travels further)
6. When solvent nears the top, remove and mark solvent front with pencil

Calculating Rf values:
Rf = distance travelled by substance / distance travelled by solvent
Rf values are always between 0 and 1
They are characteristic for each substance in a given solvent — used to identify unknown substances

Applications:
• Testing purity of food colourings
• Detecting drugs in sports doping tests
• Identifying substances at crime scenes
• Checking quality of inks in document analysis
Separating black ink

Black ink may separate into blue, red, yellow and purple spots. Each colour is a different dye. The dye that travels furthest is most soluble in the solvent. Rf = 0.8 means the dye travelled 80% of the distance the solvent travelled.

Rf = distance of spot from baseline / distance of solvent front from baseline

Rf is always between 0 and 1
Same substance in same solvent = same Rf value

Thin layer chromatography (TLC) works on the same principle but uses a coated glass/plastic plate instead of paper

Common Misconception

Chromatography separates substances by boiling point.

Chromatography separates based on solubility in the solvent and adsorption to the paper. More soluble substances travel further. Fractional distillation separates by boiling point.

Differentiation

Support:
Do a practical chromatography with food colourings or felt-tip pens if possible. If not, use a step-by-step diagram showing the process.
Stretch:
Research thin layer chromatography (TLC) and how it differs from paper chromatography. How is it used in pharmaceutical quality control?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Calculate Rf values from a chromatogram diagram. Which substance is present in the unknown mixture?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains how chromatography works, can calculate Rf values, and describes applications.
  • Getting there: Understands the principle but makes errors in Rf calculations.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain how chromatography separates mixtures. Reteach with a practical demonstration.

Homework Suggestions

  • Separate a sand/salt mixture at home using filtration and evaporation
  • Create a table comparing all separation techniques: method, what it separates, example
  • Research: how does desalination work in countries with no fresh water?
