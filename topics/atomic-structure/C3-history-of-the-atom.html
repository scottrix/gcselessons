C3: History of the Atom

Foundation All Boards

Trace the development of atomic theory from ancient Greek philosophers to the modern nuclear model, including key experiments and scientists.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the key developments in atomic theory from Democritus to Thomson
  • Describe Rutherford's gold foil experiment and Bohr's nuclear model

Prerequisites

  • Atoms and Elements (C1) — protons, neutrons, electrons

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Printed timeline of atomic theory
  • Ball, cookie, and nut models for different atomic theories
  • Video: Rutherford gold foil experiment animation

Lesson 1: From Democritus to Thomson

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Time Travel Scientists

Show portraits of 6 scientists. Can children guess which century each lived in? What did they each think an atom was?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Timeline of atomic theory:

400 BC — Democritus (Greek philosopher):
Proposed that all matter is made of tiny, indivisible particles called "atomos" (meaning indivisible). This was philosophical reasoning, not experimental evidence. Aristotle disagreed, and his view dominated for 2000 years.

1803 — John Dalton:
• All elements are made of atoms
• Atoms of the same element are identical
• Atoms cannot be created, destroyed or subdivided
• Atoms join together in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds
• Atoms rearrange in chemical reactions

1897 — J.J. Thomson:
Discovered the electron using cathode ray tubes. He showed cathode rays were deflected by electric and magnetic fields, proving they were negatively charged particles much smaller than atoms. This disproved Dalton's idea that atoms were indivisible.
Thomson proposed the "plum pudding" model — electrons embedded in a sphere of positive charge (like plums in a pudding).
Dalton's model

Dalton visualised atoms as solid, indivisible spheres — like billiard balls. Simple but wrong about the internal structure.

Thomson's plum pudding model

Electrons (negative) scattered through a 'soup' of positive charge. This explained electrical neutrality but was later disproved by Rutherford.

Cathode ray tube experiment:
Electric field deflects rays → rays are charged
Magnetic field deflects rays → rays are charged
Direction of deflection → negative charge (electrons)

Thomson calculated e/m ratio of the electron

Common Misconception

Dalton's atomic theory was completely wrong and useless.

Dalton's theory was remarkably accurate for its time. His four postulates were mostly correct — only the idea that atoms are indivisible was wrong (we now know atoms contain subatomic particles).

Differentiation

Support:
Create a visual timeline on the wall. Use models: ball for Dalton, chocolate chip cookie for Thomson.
Stretch:
Research Thomson's actual cathode ray tube experiment. How did he know the particles were negative rather than positive?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Who said it? Match quotes to scientists: 'atoms are indivisible' / 'atoms contain electrons' / 'all matter is atoms'.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can sequence Democritus → Dalton → Thomson and describe each model accurately.
  • Getting there: Remembers some scientists but mixes up their contributions.
  • Not yet: Cannot sequence the discoveries. Reteach using the timeline poster and physical models.

Lesson 2: Rutherford and Bohr Models

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

The Surprise Result

Ask: if you fired bullets at a sheet of paper, what would you expect to happen? What if most bullets went through but a few bounced back? What would that tell you about the paper?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
1911 — Ernest Rutherford (alpha particle scattering experiment):
• Fired alpha particles (positively charged, from a radioactive source) at a thin gold foil
• Most alpha particles passed straight through — most of the atom is empty space
• Some were deflected at small angles — there must be a positive centre
• A very few bounced back — the positive centre must be very small and dense
Conclusion: The atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus containing most of the atom's mass. Electrons orbit the nucleus at a distance.

Problem with Rutherford's model: According to classical physics, orbiting electrons should continuously lose energy, spiral into the nucleus, and the atom should collapse. This doesn't happen.

1913 — Niels Bohr:
Proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed energy levels (shells) without losing energy. An electron can jump between levels by absorbing or emitting specific amounts (quanta) of energy.
This explained why elements produce specific colours of light when heated (emission spectra).
Gold foil experiment results

Out of thousands of alpha particles: ~99.9% passed through undeflected. ~0.1% were deflected. A tiny fraction bounced back. This proved the nucleus is about 10,000 times smaller than the atom.

Bohr's energy levels

Electron in shell 1 has low energy. To move to shell 2, it must absorb a specific amount of energy. When it falls back, it emits light of a specific wavelength (colour). This is how sodium lamps produce yellow light.

Rutherford's conclusion:
Atom = tiny positive nucleus + mostly empty space + orbiting electrons
Nucleus diameter ≈ 10⁻¹⁵ m (atom ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m)

Bohr model: Electrons in fixed energy levels (shells)
Energy absorbed/emitted = difference between levels
E = hf (energy = Planck's constant × frequency)

Common Misconception

Rutherford discovered the nucleus by looking at atoms through a microscope.

Rutherford used an indirect method — firing alpha particles at gold foil and detecting where they landed on a zinc sulfide screen. No one has ever 'seen' a nucleus directly.

Differentiation

Support:
Use the analogy: shooting bullets at tissue paper (most go through) vs shooting at a coin (some bounce back). The nucleus is like the coin — tiny but dense.
Stretch:
Research the Bohr model's limitations. Why is the 'electron cloud' model more accurate? What are orbitals (s, p, d, f)?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Draw and label: Dalton's model, Thomson's plum pudding, Rutherford's nuclear model, Bohr's model. Which is closest to today's model?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains Rutherford's experiment, its conclusions, and Bohr's improvements clearly.
  • Getting there: Understands Rutherford but struggles to explain Bohr's contribution.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain what Rutherford's experiment showed. Reteach with video animation of the alpha scattering experiment.

Homework Suggestions

  • Write a one-page report: 'The Atom — A Story of Discovery'
  • Research James Chadwick (discovery of neutron, 1932)
  • Draw and annotate the four models of the atom
