C2: The Periodic Table

Foundation All Boards

Explore the structure of the periodic table, group trends, and the properties of alkali metals, halogens and noble gases.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the arrangement of the periodic table into groups and periods, and explain group trends
  • Explain the properties and reactions of Group 1 alkali metals
  • Explain the properties and reactions of Group 7 halogens and Group 0 noble gases

Prerequisites

  • Atoms and Elements (C1) — atomic number, mass number, electron arrangements

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Periodic table poster
  • Video clips of alkali metal reactions
  • Colour-coded periodic table handout

Lesson 1: Groups and Group 1 Alkali Metals

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Group Predictions

Show the first element in Group 1 (lithium) — it reacts with water. Without being told, predict what you think the other Group 1 elements might do.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Groups contain elements with the same number of outer (valence) electrons. This gives them similar chemical properties.

Group 1 — Alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr):
• 1 outer electron
• Soft, shiny metals that tarnish quickly in air
• React vigorously with water (more vigorously going down the group)
• Stored in oil to prevent reaction with air/moisture
• Melting point decreases down the group
• Reactivity increases down the group (larger atomic radius, outer electron further from nucleus, easier to lose)

Why reactivity increases down Group 1:
Going down the group, each element has one more electron shell. The outer electron is further from the positively charged nucleus and is shielded by more inner shells. The electrostatic attraction between nucleus and outer electron is weaker, so the electron is lost more easily. The element is more reactive.
Sodium in water

2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g). Sodium floats, melts into a silver ball, fizzes, and produces hydrogen gas which may ignite with a lilac flame. The solution turns alkaline (indicator paper turns blue).

Comparing lithium, sodium and potassium

Lithium fizzes steadily and moves across the surface. Sodium fizzes more vigorously, melts into a ball, and moves rapidly. Potassium fizzes violently, catches fire with a lilac flame, and may explode. Reactivity increases down the group.

Group 1 reaction with water: 2M(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H₂(g)
where M = Li, Na, K

Trend down Group 1:
• Reactivity: INCREASES (electron further from nucleus, easier to lose)
• Melting point: DECREASES (weaker metallic bonds)
• Atomic radius: INCREASES (more electron shells)

Common Misconception

All metals are hard and have high melting points.

Group 1 metals are soft (can be cut with a knife) and have low melting points. Mercury is a metal that is liquid at room temperature. Transition metals like iron and copper are hard with high melting points.

Differentiation

Support:
Use a simplified periodic table showing only Group 1. Provide video clips of Group 1 metals reacting with water (safely viewed, not conducted). Use colour-coding: red for very reactive, amber for moderate.
Stretch:
Research caesium and francium — why is francium extremely rare? Why can't we easily demonstrate its reactivity?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Match element cards to their group. Explain why each group has similar properties.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains group trends and links them to electron arrangement. Can predict reactivity of unfamiliar Group 1 elements.
  • Getting there: Knows group properties but struggles to explain trends in terms of electrons and atomic radius.
  • Not yet: Cannot link outer electrons to chemical properties. Reteach using physical model of atom with outer electron shell.

Lesson 2: Group 7 Halogens

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

State Symbols

Show samples (or photos) of fluorine (yellow gas), chlorine (green gas), bromine (red-brown liquid), iodine (dark grey solid). All are in Group 7. What pattern do you notice about their states at room temperature?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Group 7 — Halogens (F, Cl, Br, I, At):
• 7 outer electrons
• Non-metals, exist as diatomic molecules (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂)
• Colour and state at room temperature changes down the group:
  - Fluorine: pale yellow gas
  - Chlorine: green gas
  - Bromine: red-brown liquid
  - Iodine: dark grey/purple solid

Reactivity decreases down the group:
Going down the group, atoms have more electron shells → outer electron is further from nucleus → harder to attract and gain one more electron → less reactive.

Displacement reactions:
A more reactive halogen will displace a less reactive halogen from its salt solution.
e.g. Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂ (chlorine displaces bromine — chlorine is more reactive)

Reactions with metals:
More reactive metals form halides with lower oxidation states. E.g. NaCl (sodium chloride), FeCl₂ (iron(II) chloride), FeCl₃ (iron(III) chloride).
Chlorine displacing bromine

Add chlorine water to potassium bromide solution. The solution turns orange/brown because bromine is produced. Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂. This proves chlorine is more reactive than bromine.

Reactivity trend down Group 7: DECREASES
• Atomic radius: increases
• Ability to attract an electron: decreases
• Reactivity: decreases

Displacement: more reactive halogen displaces less reactive one
Cl₂ + 2NaBr → 2NaCl + Br₂
Br₂ + 2NaI → 2NaBr + I₂
Cl₂ + 2NaI → 2NaCl + I₂

Common Misconception

The halogens are metals because they are in the periodic table.

The halogens are non-metals. They are on the right side of the periodic table, beyond the staircase line. Their properties (low melting points, poor conductors, form negative ions) are typical of non-metals.

Differentiation

Support:
Use a table showing halogen colour, state, and formula side by side. Use physical samples if available (safely sealed in ampoules).
Stretch:
Research: why is fluorine the most reactive halogen? What happens when fluorine reacts with water? (It reacts violently, even explosively.)

Plenary (5 minutes)

Complete a table: halogen, colour, state at room temp, reactivity compared to neighbours.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains halogen trends and can predict displacement reactions using the reactivity order.
  • Getting there: Knows the trends but cannot explain them in terms of electron structure.
  • Not yet: Cannot describe halogen properties. Reteach with physical samples and the trend table.

Lesson 3: Group 0 Noble Gases and Transition Metals

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Why Don't They React?

Noble gases are in Group 0. Why do you think they are called 'noble'? (Hint: nobility = aloof, unreactive.) What do you notice about their outer electron shells?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Group 0 — Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn):
• Full outer shell (8 electrons, except helium with 2)
• Unreactive (inert) — already stable, no tendency to gain or lose electrons
• Colourless, odourless gases at room temperature
• Boiling point increases down the group (stronger London dispersion forces between larger atoms)

Uses of noble gases:
• Helium: balloons, deep-sea diving gas mixtures (less toxic than nitrogen under pressure)
• Neon: advertising signs (glows orange-red when electricity passes through)
• Argon: light bulbs (inert atmosphere prevents filament from oxidising), welding
• Krypton: energy-efficient windows (insulating gas between glass panes)

Transition metals (brief introduction):
• Found in the middle of the periodic table (Groups 3-12)
• Higher melting points than Group 1 metals
• Good conductors of heat and electricity
• Form coloured compounds (e.g. Cu²⁺ = blue, Fe³⁺ = orange/brown)
• Can form more than one ion (e.g. Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺)
• Used as catalysts (e.g. iron in the Haber process, manganese dioxide in hydrogen peroxide decomposition)
Why light bulbs contain argon

If a light bulb contained air, the hot tungsten filament would react with oxygen and burn out quickly. Argon is inert — it doesn't react with the filament, so the bulb lasts much longer.

Noble gas boiling points:
He: −269°C, Ne: −246°C, Ar: −186°C, Kr: −153°C, Xe: −108°C

Trend: boiling point INCREASES down the group
Reason: larger atoms have more electrons → stronger London dispersion forces → more energy to separate

Common Misconception

Noble gases are completely unreactive and can never form compounds.

Noble gases are extremely unreactive, but the heaviest ones (xenon, krypton) can form compounds with very reactive elements like fluorine under extreme conditions. XeF₂ and XeF₄ are known compounds. However, at GCSE level, noble gases are treated as unreactive.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a summary table of all three groups studied (1, 7, 0) with key properties. Use this as a comparison reference.
Stretch:
Research: how are transition metals used as catalysts? Why does iron(III) oxide have a different colour from iron(II) oxide?

Plenary (5 minutes)

True or false: Noble gases have full outer shells / are metals / have high boiling points / are used in light bulbs. Justify each answer.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains noble gas properties in terms of full outer shells and can give examples of uses.
  • Getting there: Knows noble gases are unreactive but cannot always explain why or give uses.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain why noble gases are unreactive. Reteach linking full outer shells to stability.

Homework Suggestions

  • Learn the first 5 elements in Groups 1, 7 and 0
  • Create a table comparing properties of Li, Na and K
  • Research: why are noble gases used in lighting?
