C31: Greenhouse Gases and Climate Change

Foundation All Boards

Describe the greenhouse effect, the main greenhouse gases, how human activities increase them, and the consequences.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the natural greenhouse effect and why it is important
  • Explain how human activities increase greenhouse gas concentrations
  • Evaluate methods to reduce CO₂ emissions and their effectiveness

Prerequisites

  • Earth's Early Atmosphere (C30) — atmospheric composition
  • Exothermic and Endothermic (C19) — energy changes

Lesson 1: The Greenhouse Effect

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Why is a greenhouse warm?

Light enters a greenhouse and heats the inside. The heat cannot easily escape through the glass. How is this similar to what happens in Earth's atmosphere?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The natural greenhouse effect:
1. Sunlight (short-wave radiation) passes through the atmosphere
2. Earth's surface absorbs the energy and warms up
3. Earth re-emits the energy as infrared radiation (long-wave radiation)
4. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere absorb some of this infrared radiation
5. The gases re-emit the radiation in all directions — including back to Earth
6. This keeps Earth's surface warmer than it would otherwise be (~15°C instead of −18°C)

Main greenhouse gases:
• Carbon dioxide (CO₂): from burning fossil fuels, deforestation, respiration
• Methane (CH₄): from rice paddies, cattle (digestion), landfill sites, natural gas leaks
• Water vapour (H₂O): most abundant greenhouse gas (but not directly increased by humans)
• Nitrous oxide (N₂O): from fertilisers, vehicle exhausts

Human activities increasing greenhouse gases:
• Burning fossil fuels → increased CO₂
• Deforestation → less CO₂ absorbed by trees
• Agriculture → increased CH₄ (cattle, rice) and N₂O (fertilisers)
• Industrial processes → various greenhouse gases

Consequences of enhanced greenhouse effect:
• Global warming (average temperature rising)
• Melting ice caps and glaciers → rising sea levels
• More extreme weather events (droughts, floods, storms)
• Changes to ecosystems and habitats
• Ocean acidification (CO₂ dissolves in seawater)
CO₂ levels rising

Pre-industrial CO₂: ~280 ppm. Current CO₂: ~420 ppm (a 50% increase). This is mainly from burning fossil fuels since the Industrial Revolution. CO₂ is the biggest contributor to the enhanced greenhouse effect (about 60% of the effect).

Methane — more potent but less abundant

Methane is 25-80× more effective at trapping heat than CO₂ (depending on the timescale). But there is much less methane in the atmosphere. CO₂ contributes more to the enhanced greenhouse effect because of its much larger volume.

Greenhouse effect:
Sun → Earth (absorbs) → re-emits infrared → greenhouse gases absorb → re-emit back to Earth

Main greenhouse gases: CO₂, CH₄, H₂O, N₂O

Human sources: fossil fuels (CO₂), deforestation (CO₂), agriculture (CH₄, N₂O)

Common Misconception

The greenhouse effect is bad.

The natural greenhouse effect is essential for life — it keeps Earth warm enough for liquid water and life to exist. The problem is the enhanced (extra) greenhouse effect caused by human activities, which is causing too much warming.

Differentiation

Support:
Use a physical model: a thermometer in a sealed jar vs open air in sunlight. The sealed jar gets warmer — trapped heat. This models the greenhouse effect.
Stretch:
Research: what is ocean acidification? How does increased CO₂ in the atmosphere affect ocean chemistry?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Explain the natural greenhouse effect in 3 sentences. Name the three main greenhouse gases and their human sources.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains the greenhouse effect clearly, names main greenhouse gases, and describes human sources.
  • Getting there: Knows the greenhouse effect but cannot name all greenhouse gases or their sources.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain the greenhouse effect. Reteach with the sealed jar model.

Lesson 2: Reducing CO₂ Emissions

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Carbon Footprint

What is a carbon footprint? What activities in your daily life produce the most CO₂? (Driving, heating, eating meat, flying.)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Methods to reduce CO₂ emissions:

1. Use of renewable energy:
• Wind, solar, hydroelectric, tidal, geothermal
• No CO₂ produced during electricity generation
• Advantages: renewable, no pollution, sustainable
• Disadvantages: expensive to build, weather-dependent, landscape impact

2. Nuclear power:
• No CO₂ produced during electricity generation
• Advantages: reliable, large amounts of energy
• Disadvantages: radioactive waste, meltdown risk, expensive to decommission

3. Energy efficiency:
• Better insulation, LED bulbs, efficient appliances
• Reduces energy demand → less fossil fuel burned
• Advantages: saves money, easy to implement
• Disadvantages: upfront costs

4. Transport:
• Electric cars, hydrogen fuel cells, public transport, cycling
• Reduces CO₂ from vehicle exhausts

5. Carbon capture and storage (CCS):
• Capture CO₂ from power stations/st factories
• Store underground in depleted oil/gas fields or rock formations
• Advantages: allows continued use of fossil fuels while reducing emissions
• Disadvantages: expensive, uncertain long-term storage, doesn't solve the root problem

6. Planting trees (afforestation):
• Trees absorb CO₂ by photosynthesis
• Advantages: natural, also provides habitat
• Disadvantages: slow, limited land available, trees release CO₂ when they die/rot

7. Reducing deforestation:
• Stop cutting down rainforests
• Trees store carbon — cutting them releases it

Alternative fuels:
• Biofuels (from plants) — carbon neutral in theory (CO₂ absorbed during growth = CO₂ released when burned)
• Hydrogen fuel cells — only produce water (if hydrogen is made by electrolysis with renewable energy)
Renewable energy comparison

Solar: works when sunny, no CO₂, but expensive panels and needs storage for night. Wind: works when windy, no CO₂, but visual/noise impact and intermittent. Nuclear: reliable, no CO₂, but radioactive waste and long construction time. Each has trade-offs.

Carbon capture and storage

CCS captures CO₂ from power station flue gases, compresses it, and pipes it underground into porous rock formations (like depleted oil fields). This prevents CO₂ from entering the atmosphere. Currently expensive and limited deployment, but potentially important for reducing emissions from existing fossil fuel plants.

Reducing CO₂:
• Renewables: wind, solar, hydro (no CO₂, but intermittent)
• Nuclear: no CO₂, but radioactive waste
• CCS: capture CO₂, store underground
• Efficiency: reduce energy demand
• Transport: electric, hydrogen, public transport
• Trees: absorb CO₂ by photosynthesis

Common Misconception

Biofuels are always carbon neutral.

Biofuels are only carbon neutral if the CO₂ released when burned equals the CO₂ absorbed during growth. In practice, energy is needed to grow, harvest, transport and process the crops — this often comes from fossil fuels, so biofuels are not perfectly carbon neutral.

Differentiation

Support:
Create a table: Method | How it reduces CO₂ | Advantages | Disadvantages. Complete together for each method.
Stretch:
Evaluate: which method of reducing CO₂ do you think is most important? Justify your answer with scientific reasoning.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Name three ways to reduce CO₂ emissions. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of each.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Evaluates methods for reducing CO₂, explains how each works, and weighs advantages/disadvantages.
  • Getting there: Can name methods but cannot evaluate them critically.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain how CO₂ emissions can be reduced. Reteach with the comparison table.

Homework Suggestions

  • Calculate your family's approximate carbon footprint
  • Research: what is the Paris Agreement? What are its goals?
  • Debate: should the UK ban all fossil fuel cars by 2035?
