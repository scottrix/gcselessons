P17: Newton's Laws and Momentum

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understanding Newton's three laws of motion, calculating force using F = ma, and exploring momentum and its conservation.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 4
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • State and explain Newton's three laws of motion
  • Use F = ma to calculate force, mass, and acceleration
  • Define momentum and use p = mv
  • Apply the principle of conservation of momentum to collisions
  • Explain how safety features use ideas about force and momentum

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of acceleration (P16)
  • Knowledge of forces from KS3
  • Ability to rearrange equations

Materials & Equipment

  • Scientific calculator
  • Ruler
  • Toy cars (for momentum investigations)
  • Spring scales (to measure force)

Lesson 1: Newton's Laws of Motion

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Everyday Physics

Students write down three examples of forces from everyday life (e.g., pushing a door, kicking a ball, weight of a book). Discuss: "What happens when a force acts on an object?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Newton's First Law:
An object remains at rest or continues moving at constant velocity unless acted upon by a resultant force.
- If no resultant force acts, velocity is constant (could be zero = stationary)
- "Balanced forces" means zero resultant force
- Examples: a book on a table (weight balanced by normal force), a car at constant speed on a straight road (driving force balanced by friction and air resistance)

Newton's Second Law:
The resultant force on an object is proportional to its acceleration. F = ma
- F = resultant force (N)
- m = mass (kg)
- a = acceleration (m/s²)
- Greater force → greater acceleration
- Greater mass → smaller acceleration (for same force)

Newton's Third Law:
When object A exerts a force on object B, object B exerts an equal and opposite force on object A.
- The two forces are equal in magnitude but opposite in direction
- They act on different objects (NOT on the same object)
- Examples: rocket exhaust pushes gas down, gas pushes rocket up; you push the ground backward, the ground pushes you forward
- These forces always exist in pairs — you cannot have a single force in isolation
Worked Example

A 500 kg car accelerates from rest to 20 m/s in 10 seconds. Calculate the resultant force.
a = (v − u) ÷ t = (20 − 0) ÷ 10 = 2 m/s²
F = ma = 500 × 2 = 1000 N

Key Equation:
F = ma

F = resultant force (N)
m = mass (kg)
a = acceleration (m/s²)
1 N = 1 kg·m/s²
Common Misconception: Students think Newton's Third Law forces act on the same object. They don't — the forces act on different objects. When you push a wall, you exert a force on the wall, and the wall exerts a force on you. These forces act on different objects.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a table of Newton's Laws with examples. Students add their own examples for each law.
Stretch:
A 2 kg object experiences forces of 10 N to the right and 6 N to the left. Calculate the resultant force and acceleration. If a third force of 4 N is applied at 90° to the first two, what is the new resultant force?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Law Matching

Students match three scenarios to the correct Newton's Law and explain why.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can state all three laws, give examples, and use F = ma to solve problems
  • Getting there: Can state the laws but cannot apply F = ma or give examples
  • Not yet: Cannot state Newton's Laws or use F = ma

Lesson 2: Momentum

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Collision Comparison

Students compare: (a) being hit by a tennis ball at 30 m/s, (b) being hit by a cricket ball at 30 m/s. "Which would hurt more? Why?" Discuss mass and velocity.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Momentum is a measure of how difficult it is to stop a moving object. It is a vector quantity.

p = mv
- p = momentum (kg·m/s)
- m = mass (kg)
- v = velocity (m/s)

Key points:
- Momentum is a vector — direction matters
- A heavy, slow object can have the same momentum as a light, fast object
- The greater the momentum, the harder it is to stop the object

Force and momentum:
Force = rate of change of momentum
F = Δp ÷ t (force equals the change in momentum divided by the time taken)

Impulse:
Impulse = Force × time = change in momentum
A larger time for the same change in momentum means a smaller force
This is why car airbags and crash helmets work — they increase the time of impact, reducing the force
Worked Example

A 0.5 kg ball hits a wall at 10 m/s and bounces back at 8 m/s.
(a) Initial momentum = 0.5 × 10 = 5 kg·m/s (towards the wall)
(b) Final momentum = 0.5 × (−8) = −4 kg·m/s (away from the wall, hence negative)
(c) Change in momentum = −4 − 5 = −9 kg·m/s
(d) If the ball was in contact with the wall for 0.02 s: F = Δp ÷ t = 9 ÷ 0.02 = 450 N

Key Equations:
p = mv
F = Δp ÷ t
Impulse = F × t = Δp
Common Misconception: Students think momentum is always positive. It is a vector — if an object reverses direction, the change in momentum can be larger than the original momentum (as in the worked example above).

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a table for calculating momentum of objects. Students complete the table for different masses and velocities.
Stretch:
A 70 kg person jumps from a height of 3 m. Calculate the velocity on impact. Then calculate the average force if they land on concrete (0.01 s impact) vs on a mat (0.5 s impact). Discuss the difference.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Calculation

A 2 kg trolley moves at 3 m/s. Calculate its momentum. (Answer: 6 kg·m/s)

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can calculate momentum, change in momentum, and force from impulse
  • Getting there: Can calculate momentum but struggles with change in momentum or force
  • Not yet: Cannot use p = mv or understand momentum as a vector

Lesson 3: Conservation of Momentum

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Collision Prediction

Show two toy cars on a track: one moving, one stationary. Predict what happens when they collide. "What stays the same before and after the collision?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Conservation of momentum:
In a closed system (no external forces), the total momentum before a collision equals the total momentum after the collision.

Total momentum before = Total momentum after
m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

Types of collisions:

1. Perfectly elastic collision:
- Kinetic energy is also conserved
- Objects bounce off each other
- Example: billiard balls, subatomic particles

2. Inelastic collision:
- Kinetic energy is NOT conserved (some becomes thermal, sound, etc.)
- Objects may stick together or deform
- Example: car crash, ball hitting mud

3. Perfectly inelastic collision:
- Objects stick together after collision
- Maximum kinetic energy is lost
- Example: two clay balls sticking together

Safety applications:
- Car crumple zones: increase the time of impact → reduce the force (F = Δp ÷ t)
- Airbags: increase the time of impact for the occupant → reduce force on the person
- Seatbelts: spread the force over a larger area and increase the time of deceleration
- Cycle helmets: foam compresses on impact, increasing the time of deceleration
Worked Example

A 1000 kg car travelling at 20 m/s collides with a stationary 1500 kg lorry. They lock together after the collision.
(a) Momentum before: 1000 × 20 + 1500 × 0 = 20 000 kg·m/s
(b) Total mass after: 1000 + 1500 = 2500 kg
(c) Velocity after: 20 000 ÷ 2500 = 8 m/s
(d) The combined vehicles move at 8 m/s in the same direction as the car was travelling

Conservation of Momentum:
m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

Total momentum before = Total momentum after

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a template with "Before" and "After" columns. Students calculate the momentum of each object and show they are equal.
Stretch:
A 2 kg ball moving at 5 m/s collides head-on with a 3 kg ball moving at 3 m/s in the opposite direction. If they stick together, calculate the velocity after the collision.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

"A 0.2 kg ball moving at 4 m/s collides with a stationary 0.3 kg ball. After the collision, the 0.2 kg ball moves at 1 m/s. Calculate the velocity of the 0.3 kg ball."

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can apply conservation of momentum to solve collision problems and explain safety features
  • Getting there: Can apply conservation of momentum but cannot link it to safety features
  • Not yet: Cannot set up or solve conservation of momentum equations

Lesson 4: F = ma Investigations

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Push Challenge

Students push a trolley with different forces (using a spring scale) and observe the effect on acceleration. Discuss: "What happens when you double the force? What happens when you add mass?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Required Practical: Investigate the relationship between force, mass, and acceleration.

Method (investigating F = ma):
1. Attach a toy car to a hanging mass via a pulley on a ramp
2. The hanging mass provides the force (weight = mg)
3. Measure the acceleration using light gates and a data logger
4. Repeat with different hanging masses (different forces)
5. Repeat with different masses on the trolley (adding weights)

Analysing results:
- Plot a graph of F against a (for constant mass) → straight line through origin, gradient = mass
- Plot a graph of a against 1/m (for constant force) → straight line through origin, gradient = force

Investigating terminal velocity:
- Drop a sycamore seed or a small object with a parachute
- Use a data logger to measure velocity over time
- Plot a velocity-time graph
- Identify the terminal velocity from the flat section of the graph
Worked Example

A trolley of mass 2 kg is pulled by a force of 10 N along a frictionless surface. A frictional force of 2 N opposes the motion.
(a) Resultant force = 10 − 2 = 8 N
(b) a = F ÷ m = 8 ÷ 2 = 4 m/s²
(c) If the trolley starts from rest, velocity after 3 s: v = u + at = 0 + 4 × 3 = 12 m/s

Key Equations:
F = ma
Resultant force = driving force − frictional force

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a step-by-step method card for the experiment. Students follow each step and record results in a table.
Stretch:
A car of mass 1200 kg has an engine force of 3000 N and experiences air resistance of 500 N and friction of 300 N. Calculate: (a) resultant force, (b) acceleration, (c) velocity after 10 s from rest.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Graph Interpretation

Show an F-a graph. Students identify the mass from the gradient and explain what the graph would look like for a heavier trolley.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can plan the experiment, collect and analyse data, and use F = ma for complex problems
  • Getting there: Can use F = ma but cannot plan or analyse the experiment
  • Not yet: Cannot use F = ma or design a fair investigation

Homework Suggestions

  • Solve 10 problems using F = ma in different scenarios
  • Calculate the change in momentum when a 1500 kg car going at 20 m/s is brought to rest in 2 seconds. Then calculate the average braking force.
  • Research how car crumple zones work and write a paragraph explaining the physics using F = Δp/t
  • Investigate: why do airbags need to deflate quickly after deployment? Use ideas about impulse and momentum.
