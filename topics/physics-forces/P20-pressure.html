P20: Pressure

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understanding pressure as force per unit area, atmospheric pressure, and how pressure varies with depth in fluids.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define pressure and use p = F/A
  • Explain how pressure depends on force and contact area
  • Describe atmospheric pressure and how it varies with altitude
  • Calculate pressure in fluids using p = ρgh
  • Apply pressure concepts to real-world situations

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of forces (P17)
  • Knowledge of density (P10)
  • Ability to rearrange equations

Materials & Equipment

  • Scientific calculator
  • Blu-tack, pencil, and paper (for pressure demonstration)
  • Plastic bottles with holes at different heights (for fluid pressure)

Lesson 1: Pressure in Solids

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Sharp vs Blunt

Push a blunt pencil into blu-tack, then push a sharp pencil into blu-tack with the same force. Ask: "Why does the sharp pencil go in more easily even though you used the same force?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Pressure is the force acting perpendicularly per unit area.
p = F ÷ A
- p = pressure (Pa, pascals)
- F = force (N)
- A = area (m²)
- 1 Pa = 1 N/m²

Key points:
- Larger force → higher pressure (directly proportional)
- Larger contact area → lower pressure (inversely proportional)
- Pressure in solids is distributed over the contact area

Examples:
- A sharp knife has a small contact area → high pressure → cuts easily
- A snowshoe has a large contact area → low pressure → doesn't sink
- A bed of nails works because each nail has a tiny area, creating high pressure at each point

Atmospheric pressure:
- The atmosphere exerts pressure on everything at Earth's surface
- At sea level: ~101 000 Pa (101 kPa)
- Caused by the weight of the air above
- Decreases with altitude (less air above)
- At the top of Mount Everest (~9000 m): ~31 000 Pa
- In a vacuum, pressure = 0 Pa
Worked Example

A person (mass 70 kg) stands on one foot. The area of contact is 0.025 m².
(a) Weight = mg = 70 × 9.8 = 686 N
(b) Pressure = F ÷ A = 686 ÷ 0.025 = 27 440 Pa ≈ 27.4 kPa
(c) If they stand on both feet (area = 0.05 m²): p = 686 ÷ 0.05 = 13 720 Pa ≈ 13.7 kPa

Key Equation:
p = F ÷ A
p = pressure (Pa)
F = force (N)
A = area (m²)
Common Misconception: Students think pressure depends only on force. It depends equally on the contact area — the same force spread over a larger area creates less pressure.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a table of areas in m² for common objects (shoe, car tyre, person). Students calculate pressure for given forces.
Stretch:
A 500 kg vehicle has four tyres, each with contact area 0.02 m². Calculate the pressure on the road. Then calculate the pressure if two tyres burst and the weight is shared by two tyres.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Calculation

A 0.5 kg book is placed on a table. The book's base area is 200 cm². Calculate the pressure on the table. (Answer: 245 Pa)

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can calculate pressure using p = F/A and explain how area affects pressure
  • Getting there: Can calculate pressure but cannot convert units or explain atmospheric pressure
  • Not yet: Cannot apply p = F/A or understand the relationship between force, area, and pressure

Lesson 2: Pressure in Fluids

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Underwater Experience

Ask students: "Why do your ears hurt when you dive deep in a swimming pool? Why does the water come out of a hole faster when it's deeper?" Discuss.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Pressure in fluids (liquids and gases):
The pressure at a depth h in a fluid of density ρ is:
p = ρgh
- ρ = density of the fluid (kg/m³)
- g = acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s²)
- h = depth below the surface (m)

Key points:
- Pressure increases with depth (more fluid above)
- Pressure is the same in all directions at the same depth
- Pressure depends on the density of the fluid (denser fluid → higher pressure at same depth)
- At the surface, total pressure = atmospheric pressure + ρgh

Pascal's Principle:
Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally throughout the fluid. This is the principle behind hydraulic systems:
- A small force applied to a small piston creates pressure
- This pressure is transmitted to a larger piston
- The larger piston experiences a larger force (F = p × A)
- Used in car brakes, hydraulic lifts, and excavators

Atmospheric pressure variation:
- Decreases with altitude
- At sea level: ~101 kPa
- At 5000 m: ~54 kPa
- At 10 000 m: ~26 kPa
- At the edge of space (~100 km): effectively 0 Pa
Worked Example

Calculate the pressure at 30 m depth in seawater (density = 1030 kg/m³).
(a) Fluid pressure: p = ρgh = 1030 × 9.8 × 30 = 302 820 Pa ≈ 303 kPa
(b) Total pressure at 30 m: 101 000 + 303 000 = 404 000 Pa ≈ 404 kPa
(c) This is about 4 times atmospheric pressure

Key Equation:
p = ρgh (pressure due to fluid column)
Total pressure = Atmospheric pressure + ρgh

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a diagram showing depth and density. Students calculate pressure at different depths in water and mercury.
Stretch:
A hydraulic lift has a small piston of area 0.005 m² and a large piston of area 0.25 m². A force of 200 N is applied to the small piston. Calculate: (a) the pressure, (b) the force on the large piston. (Answer: 40 000 Pa, 10 000 N)

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

"Calculate the total pressure experienced by a diver at 20 m depth in the sea (ρ = 1025 kg/m³). Atmospheric pressure is 101 kPa." Students solve and compare answers.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can calculate fluid pressure using p = ρgh and explain Pascal's principle
  • Getting there: Can use p = ρgh but cannot explain how hydraulic systems work
  • Not yet: Cannot apply p = ρgh or understand pressure in fluids

Homework Suggestions

  • Calculate the pressure at the bottom of a 5 m swimming pool (freshwater, ρ = 1000 kg/m³)
  • Research how a barometer works and explain how it measures atmospheric pressure
  • Calculate the force on a car's brake pad if the hydraulic pressure is 500 000 Pa and the pad area is 0.01 m²
  • Explain why buildings have flat roofs that can be damaged in strong winds, using ideas about atmospheric pressure differences
