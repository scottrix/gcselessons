P19: Forces and Elasticity

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understanding how forces affect the shape of objects, Hooke's Law, and the relationship between force, extension, and the spring constant.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the effects of forces on the shape of objects
  • Define elastic and inelastic deformation
  • State and apply Hooke's Law: F = ke
  • Investigate the relationship between force and extension for a spring
  • Calculate the energy stored in a stretched spring

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of forces and Newton's Laws (P17)
  • Ability to rearrange equations and interpret graphs

Materials & Equipment

  • Scientific calculator
  • Spring, clamp stand, ruler, masses
  • Graph paper

Lesson 1: Hooke's Law

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Stretch Challenge

Give students a rubber band and ask them to stretch it by different amounts. Ask: "What happens to the force needed as you stretch it more? Does it double when you double the stretch?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Forces and deformation:
When a force acts on an object, it can change its shape (deformation). This can be: - Elastic deformation: the object returns to its original shape when the force is removed (e.g., a spring, rubber band within limit) - Plastic deformation: the object does NOT return to its original shape (e.g., bending a paper clip, stretching plasticine)

Hooke's Law:
The extension of a spring is directly proportional to the force applied, provided the elastic limit is not exceeded.
F = ke
- F = force applied (N)
- k = spring constant (N/m) — a measure of the stiffness of the spring
- e = extension (m) — the increase in length, NOT the total length

Key points:
- Extension = stretched length − original length - The spring constant k depends on the material, thickness, and number of coils - A stiffer spring has a larger k value - Hooke's Law only applies within the limit of proportionality (elastic limit)

Graph of force against extension:
- Straight line through the origin up to the limit of proportionality - Gradient = spring constant (k) - Beyond the limit, the graph curves — Hooke's Law no longer applies - At the elastic limit, permanent deformation begins
Worked Example

A spring has a spring constant of 200 N/m. A force of 10 N is applied.
(a) Extension: e = F ÷ k = 10 ÷ 200 = 0.05 m = 5 cm
(b) If the original length was 10 cm, the new length = 10 + 5 = 15 cm
(c) To double the extension to 10 cm, the force needed = 200 × 0.1 = 20 N

Key Equation:
F = ke
k = F ÷ e (spring constant = force ÷ extension)
Common Misconception: Students confuse extension with length. Extension is how much the spring has stretched (new length − original length), not the total length.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a table with columns: Force, Total Length, Extension. Students calculate extension and identify the pattern.
Stretch:
Two springs (k₁ = 100 N/m, k₂ = 300 N/m) are connected in series. Calculate the combined spring constant. (For springs in series: 1/k = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂)

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Calculation

A spring stretches 3 cm under a force of 6 N. What is k? How much force to stretch it 8 cm? (k = 200 N/m, F = 16 N)

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can apply F = ke, interpret F-e graphs, and distinguish elastic from plastic deformation
  • Getting there: Can use F = ke but cannot interpret graphs or explain elastic/plastic deformation
  • Not yet: Cannot apply Hooke's Law or distinguish extension from length

Lesson 2: Energy in Springs

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Spring Energy

Pull a spring back and release it. It launches a small object. Ask: "Where was the energy stored before release? How can we calculate it?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Elastic potential energy:
When a spring is stretched or compressed, energy is stored in the elastic potential energy store. The energy stored equals the work done to stretch the spring.

Energy stored in a spring:
E = ½ × F × e = ½ × k × e²
- This is the area under the force-extension graph (triangle area)
- The graph is a straight line up to the elastic limit
- Area = ½ × base × height = ½ × e × F = ½ke²

Key points:
- Energy is proportional to e² (not e) — doubling extension quadruples the energy stored
- The energy is stored in the bonds between particles in the spring material
- When the spring is released, the elastic potential energy is converted to kinetic energy

Applications:
- Catapults and crossbows store elastic potential energy
- Vehicle suspension systems use springs to absorb energy
- Pogo sticks store and release energy cyclically
Worked Example

A spring with k = 500 N/m is stretched by 0.04 m.
(a) Force: F = ke = 500 × 0.04 = 20 N
(b) Energy stored: E = ½ke² = ½ × 500 × 0.04² = ½ × 500 × 0.0016 = 0.4 J
(c) If the spring is released, this energy becomes kinetic energy of the object launched

Key Equation:
E = ½ke²
E = ½ × Force × extension
(Area under F-e graph)

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a force-extension graph with the area already marked. Students calculate the area using the triangle formula and identify it as the energy stored.
Stretch:
A spring stores 2 J of energy when stretched by 0.1 m. Calculate the spring constant. Then calculate the force and extension when the spring stores 8 J.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Proportionality

If you double the extension of a spring, what happens to: (a) the force? (b) the energy stored? Students explain with reference to F = ke and E = ½ke².

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can calculate energy stored in springs and relate it to the area under F-e graphs
  • Getting there: Can calculate energy but cannot relate it to graph areas
  • Not yet: Cannot use E = ½ke² or understand elastic potential energy

Homework Suggestions

  • Investigate Hooke's Law at home using rubber bands and masses. Plot a graph of force against extension.
  • Calculate the energy stored in a car suspension spring (k = 15 000 N/m) compressed by 0.1 m
  • Research how a crossbow stores and releases energy. Calculate the energy in a crossbow string stretched 0.05 m with k = 50 000 N/m
  • Explain why a bungee cord is different from a spring in terms of its force-extension graph
