C33: Potable Water

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand what potable water is, how it differs from pure water, and the methods used to make water safe to drink including filtration, chlorination, and distillation.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define potable water and distinguish it from pure water
  • Describe the steps in water treatment: filtration, sedimentation, chlorination
  • Explain how distillation produces pure water and why it is energy-intensive
  • Describe how water quality is tested for dissolved solids, pH, and microbes

Prerequisites

  • Mixtures and Separation (C5) — filtration, distillation, chromatography
  • Atoms, Elements, Compounds (C1) — solutions, solutes, solvents

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Water samples (tap, distilled, pond/river if available)
  • Filter paper, funnels, beakers for simple filtration demo
  • Distillation apparatus (or video of distillation setup)
  • pH paper or universal indicator, TDS meter if available

Lesson 1: What is Potable Water and Water Treatment

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Would You Drink This?

Show images of: a muddy river, a clear mountain stream, tap water, distilled water, sea water. Ask: which would you drink? Why? Introduce the term "potable" — safe to drink.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Potable water: Water that is safe to drink. It is NOT pure water (H₂O only). It contains dissolved minerals (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, etc.) which are beneficial for health and taste.

Pure water: Contains only H₂O molecules. No dissolved substances. Obtained by distillation. Tastes flat.

Sources of water:
• Surface water: rivers, lakes, reservoirs — contains microbes, suspended solids, dissolved substances
• Groundwater: aquifers — often cleaner, but may contain dissolved minerals (hard water)
• Sea water: high salt content, not potable without desalination

Water treatment steps (for surface water):
1. Screening: removes large debris (sticks, plastic)
2. Flocculation/Sedimentation: add coagulant (e.g., aluminium sulfate) → tiny particles clump together → settle out
3. Filtration: sand and gravel filters remove remaining suspended solids
4. Chlorination: add chlorine gas or sodium hypochlorite → kills bacteria and viruses
5. pH adjustment: add lime if too acidic

Chlorination:
Cl₂ + H₂O → HCl + HOCl (hypochlorous acid)
HOCl kills microbes by oxidising their cell components.
Residual chlorine remains in pipes to prevent recontamination.
Concern: Chlorine can react with organic matter to form trace disinfection by-products (e.g., trihalomethanes). Levels are strictly regulated.
Flocculation demo

Add a pinch of alum (potassium aluminium sulfate) to muddy water in a jar. Stir gently. Watch particles clump (flocculate) and settle. This models the sedimentation step in water treatment plants.

Potable water ≠ Pure water

Water treatment: Screen → Flocculate → Sediment → Filter → Chlorinate → pH adjust

Chlorination: Cl₂ + H₂O → HCl + HOCl

Common Misconception

Pure water is better for you than tap water.

Pure water (distilled) lacks essential minerals like calcium and magnesium. Potable water contains safe levels of these minerals. Tap water in the UK is among the safest in the world.

Differentiation

Support:
Create a flowchart of water treatment steps with simple icons. Emphasise "potable = safe to drink" not "pure".
Stretch:
Research: how does UV treatment or ozone treatment compare to chlorination? Why do some countries use ozone instead of chlorine?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Order the treatment steps for river water: screening, chlorination, sedimentation, filtration. Explain why each is needed.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Defines potable water, lists treatment steps in order, explains purpose of chlorination.
  • Getting there: Knows water is treated but cannot order steps or explain why potable ≠ pure.
  • Not yet: Confuses potable with pure. Reteach with demo of muddy → clear water.

Lesson 2: Distillation and Water Quality Testing

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Desert Island Water

You are on a desert island with only sea water. How could you make it drinkable? Discuss distillation — boiling water and condensing the steam.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Distillation: Separates water from dissolved solids by evaporation and condensation.
Process: Heat water → water evaporates (leaving salts behind) → steam condenses on cold surface → pure liquid water collected.

Laboratory distillation: Flask → condenser (cold water in/out) → collection flask.
Large-scale desalination: Multi-stage flash distillation or reverse osmosis (membranes).

Why distillation is energy-intensive:
Water has a high specific heat capacity (4.2 J/g°C) and high latent heat of vaporisation (2260 J/g). Boiling large volumes requires huge energy input.

Testing water quality:
• Dissolved solids: Evaporate known volume → mass of residue = total dissolved solids (TDS) in mg/L or ppm
• pH: Universal indicator or pH meter (potable water pH 6.5–8.5)
• Microbial testing: Agar plates, coliform test (E. coli indicates faecal contamination)
• Chemical tests: Flame tests for metal ions, precipitation for anions (e.g., BaCl₂ for sulfate)
• Hardness: Soap test or EDTA titration (Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ concentration)
TDS calculation

50 cm³ of tap water evaporated to dryness leaves 0.085 g residue.
TDS = (0.085 g / 50 cm³) × 1000 = 1.7 g/L = 1700 mg/L = 1700 ppm
(UK tap water typically 100–500 ppm)

Reverse osmosis

High pressure forces water through a semi-permeable membrane that blocks dissolved salts. Used in desalination plants and home filters. More energy-efficient than distillation but membranes need replacement.

Distillation: Heat → Evaporation → Condensation → Pure water

Energy: Q = m × c × ΔT (heating) + m × L (vaporisation)
c (water) = 4.2 J/g°C, L = 2260 J/g

TDS (ppm) = (mass residue in mg) / (volume water in L)

Common Misconception

Distillation removes everything — the water is 100% pure.

Distillation removes non-volatile dissolved solids. Volatile impurities (e.g., some organic solvents with lower boiling points than water) can distil over with the steam. Multiple distillations or fractional distillation improves purity.

Differentiation

Support:
Draw and label a distillation apparatus. Use a simple energy diagram showing heat in → phase change.
Stretch:
Calculate energy needed to desalinate 1 m³ of sea water (approx. 3.5% salt) by distillation vs reverse osmosis. Compare costs.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Why is distillation not used for most UK tap water? When IS it used? (Desalination, labs, pharmaceuticals)

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Describes distillation process, explains energy cost, lists water quality tests.
  • Getting there: Can describe distillation but cannot explain why it is energy-intensive.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain how distillation separates water from salt. Reteach with particle model.

Homework Suggestions

  • Research your local water supplier's annual water quality report — find the TDS, pH, hardness values
  • Draw a labelled diagram of a water treatment plant with all 6 steps
  • Compare distillation vs reverse osmosis for desalination in a table (energy, cost, output, environmental impact)
