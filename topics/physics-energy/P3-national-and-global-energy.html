P3: National and Global Energy

Foundation Higher All Boards

Exploring how electrical energy is generated, transmitted, and distributed. Understanding fossil fuels, renewable energy sources, and the challenges of meeting global energy demand.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 4
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe how electricity is generated in thermal and hydroelectric power stations
  • Explain the advantages and disadvantages of fossil fuels and renewable energy sources
  • Describe how the National Grid transmits electricity
  • Use P = IV and P = VI to calculate power in transmission
  • Evaluate the environmental impact of different energy sources

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of energy stores and transfers (P1-P2)
  • Knowledge of electrical circuits (P6)
  • Basic understanding of current, voltage, and resistance

Materials & Equipment

  • Scientific calculator
  • Computer/tablet for research activities
  • Graph paper (for energy mix charts)

Lesson 1: Fossil Fuels and Thermal Power Stations

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Energy Source Brainstorm

Students list every energy source they can think of in 60 seconds. Group them into "fossil fuels" and "renewables" on the board. Discuss: "Where does our electricity actually come from?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Fossil fuels are formed from the remains of ancient organisms over millions of years. The three main fossil fuels are:

Coal — mined from the ground, burned in power stations
Oil (petroleum) — extracted from offshore or onshore wells, refined into fuels
Natural gas — extracted from underground reserves, burned for heating and electricity

How a thermal power station works:
1. Fuel is burned to heat water → Chemical energy → Thermal energy
2. Water boils to produce steam → Thermal energy store of steam increases
3. Steam drives a turbine → Thermal energy → Kinetic energy of turbine
4. Turbine spins a generator → Kinetic energy → Electrical energy
5. Electricity is transmitted via the National Grid

Energy transfers in a coal power station:
Chemical (coal) → Thermal (water/steam) → Kinetic (turbine) → Electrical (generator)

Problems with fossil fuels:
- Non-renewable: finite supply, will run out
- Release CO₂ when burned → contributes to climate change
- Release other pollutants: sulfur dioxide (acid rain), nitrogen oxides (smog), particulates
- Oil spills cause environmental damage
Worked Example

A coal power station has an efficiency of 38%. If it generates 2000 MW of electrical power:
(a) Total thermal power from burning coal = 2000 ÷ 0.38 = 5263 MW
(b) Wasted thermal power = 5263 − 2000 = 3263 MW
(c) This wasted energy is released into the environment as waste heat (often via cooling towers)

Key Formulae:
Power (W) = Energy (J) ÷ Time (s)
Efficiency = (Useful power output ÷ Total power input) × 100%

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a labelled diagram of a thermal power station with energy transfers shown. Students match energy store labels to the correct part of the diagram.
Stretch:
Research task: Compare the carbon emissions per kWh of electricity from coal, gas, and oil power stations. Present findings as a bar chart.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Four-Step Chain

Students write the energy transfer chain for a gas power station: Chemical → Thermal → Kinetic → Electrical. Then explain what happens to the energy that doesn't become electrical.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the energy transfers in a thermal power station and explain the environmental problems with fossil fuels
  • Getting there: Can describe the energy chain but misses some transfer steps or environmental impacts
  • Not yet: Cannot sequence the energy transfers or identify fossil fuel problems

Lesson 2: Renewable Energy Sources

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Renewable or Not?

Show images of: wind turbine, solar panel, nuclear power station, hydroelectric dam, geothermal plant, biomass plant. Students classify each as renewable or non-renewable and explain their reasoning.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Renewable energy sources are naturally replenished and will not run out:

Wind — wind turns turbine blades → kinetic → electrical. Advantages: no fuel cost, no emissions. Disadvantages: intermittent, visual impact, noise, requires wind.

Solar — photovoltaic cells convert light directly to electricity. Advantages: no fuel cost, silent, scalable. Disadvantages: only works in daylight, expensive, requires large areas.

Hydroelectric — water flows downhill to turn turbines. Advantages: reliable, can store energy (pumped storage). Disadvantages: disrupts ecosystems, requires suitable geography, large dams displace communities.

Geothermal — heat from Earth's interior heats water to drive turbines. Advantages: reliable, small land footprint. Disadvantages: only available in volcanic areas, expensive to set up.

Biomass — burning organic matter (wood, crops, waste). Advantages: uses waste materials, carbon-neutral if managed sustainably. Disadvantages: releases CO₂ (offset by regrowth), requires land.

Tidal — energy from tidal movements. Advantages: predictable, reliable. Disadvantages: expensive, disrupts marine ecosystems, limited suitable locations.

Wave — energy from ocean waves. Advantages: vast resource, no fuel cost. Disadvantages: technology still developing, harsh marine environment damages equipment.
Worked Example

A school installs solar panels with a peak output of 5 kW. On a sunny day, they generate electricity for 6 hours:
Energy generated = Power × Time = 5 × 6 = 30 kWh
If electricity costs 34p/kWh, the school saves: 30 × 0.34 = £10.20 per sunny day
Over a year with 150 sunny days: 150 × £10.20 = £1530 saved

Key Formula:
Energy (kWh) = Power (kW) × Time (h)
Common Misconception: Students often think nuclear energy is renewable. It is NOT renewable — uranium is a finite resource. However, it produces very low CO₂ emissions during operation. Nuclear is classified as non-renewable but low-carbon.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a table with pre-filled advantages and disadvantages. Students complete missing entries for each renewable source.
Stretch:
Debate task: "Should the UK invest more in nuclear power or renewable energy?" Students prepare arguments for both sides using data on cost, reliability, and environmental impact.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Top Trumps

Create Top Trumps cards for renewable energy sources using categories: Cost, Reliability, Environmental Impact, Scalability. Students compare cards and justify their choices.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe at least five renewable energy sources with advantages and disadvantages for each
  • Getting there: Can describe renewable sources but cannot evaluate their advantages and disadvantages systematically
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between renewable and non-renewable sources

Lesson 3: The National Grid

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Tracing the Path

Ask: "What happens between the power station and your light switch?" Students draw a flowchart of the electricity journey from generation to consumption.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The National Grid is the network of cables, transformers, and pylons that transmits electricity from power stations to consumers (homes, factories, schools).

How it works:
1. Generators produce electricity at 25 000 V (25 kV)
2. A step-up transformer increases voltage to 400 000 V (400 kV) for transmission
3. Electricity travels along high-voltage overhead cables or underground cables
4. A step-down transformer reduces voltage to 230 V for home use

Why use high voltage for transmission?
Power lost in cables = I²R (thermal energy due to resistance)
For a given power: P = IV, so increasing V decreases I
Lower current means less energy lost as heat in the cables

Key components:
- Generators: convert kinetic energy to electrical energy
- Step-up transformers: increase voltage, decrease current
- Transmission cables: carry electricity long distances
- Step-down transformers: decrease voltage to safe levels for homes
- Consumers: use the electrical energy (appliances, lighting, heating)
Key Formulae:
P = IV (Power = Current × Voltage)
Energy lost in cables = I² × R × t
(Power loss is proportional to current squared — halving the current quarters the power loss)
Worked Example

A power station generates 500 MW at 25 kV. This is stepped up to 400 kV for transmission.
(a) Current at 25 kV: I = P ÷ V = 500 000 000 ÷ 25 000 = 20 000 A
(b) Current at 400 kV: I = P ÷ V = 500 000 000 ÷ 400 000 = 1250 A
The current is reduced by a factor of 16. Since power loss = I²R, the power loss is reduced by a factor of 16² = 256.

Common Misconception: Students think transformers "create" energy. Transformers change voltage and current but do NOT change the total power (ignoring small losses in the transformer itself). P_in ≈ P_out.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a labelled diagram of the National Grid with boxes for each component. Students fill in the energy transfers at each stage.
Stretch:
Calculate the percentage of power lost in transmission cables for a given scenario. Compare the power loss at 132 kV vs 400 kV for the same transmitted power.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Why Step Up?

Students explain in their own words why electricity is transmitted at high voltage, using the formula P = IV and the concept of power loss in cables.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain the full journey from generation to consumption and why high voltage is used for transmission
  • Getting there: Can describe the components but cannot explain why high voltage reduces power loss
  • Not yet: Cannot sequence the components of the National Grid or understand the role of transformers

Lesson 4: Global Energy Challenges

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Data Analysis

Show a graph of UK energy sources over time (coal declining, renewables increasing). Ask: "What trends do you notice? What might the UK's energy mix look like in 2050?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The UK and world face significant energy challenges:

1. Rising demand — growing global population and increasing use of electronics means more electricity is needed.

2. Depleting fossil fuels — oil, coal, and gas reserves are finite. Some estimates suggest oil reserves may last 50 years at current usage rates.

3. Climate change — burning fossil fuels releases CO₂, a greenhouse gas that traps infrared radiation in the atmosphere, causing global warming.

4. Energy security — relying on other countries for fuel supplies creates political and economic vulnerability.

5. Cost — renewable technologies have high initial costs but low running costs. Fossil fuels have lower initial costs but rising fuel prices.

The UK's energy strategy:
- Phase out coal-fired power stations (closed 2024)
- Increase offshore wind capacity (target: 50 GW by 2030)
- Expand solar installations
- Invest in nuclear power (Hinkley Point C)
- Improve home insulation and energy efficiency
- Develop hydrogen fuel technology
Worked Example

A country currently generates 60% of its electricity from coal (carbon intensity: 900 g CO₂/kWh) and 10% from wind (carbon intensity: 11 g CO₂/kWh).
Coal: 60% × 900 = 540 g CO₂/kWh (from coal)
Wind: 10% × 11 = 1.1 g CO₂/kWh (from wind)
If it switches to 30% coal and 40% wind:
Coal: 30% × 900 = 270 g CO₂/kWh
Wind: 40% × 11 = 4.4 g CO₂/kWh
Total reduction: from 541.1 to 274.4 g CO₂/kWh — a 49% reduction in carbon intensity.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a simplified table of energy sources with their advantages and disadvantages. Students complete a "balanced argument" paragraph for one energy source.
Stretch:
Students design an energy policy for a small island nation, considering geography, climate, budget, and population. They must justify their choices with data.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exit Ticket

Students write three sentences: one thing they learned, one thing they found surprising, and one question they still have about global energy.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of different energy sources and discuss global energy challenges with reference to data
  • Getting there: Can describe energy challenges but cannot support arguments with quantitative data
  • Not yet: Cannot identify the main energy challenges or compare energy sources

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a pie chart showing the UK's current energy mix using data from the Department for Energy Security
  • Research one renewable energy project in the UK and write a 200-word summary of how it works
  • Calculate how many solar panels would be needed to power your home, given average UK sunlight hours
  • Write a persuasive letter to your local MP arguing for or against a new wind farm in your area
