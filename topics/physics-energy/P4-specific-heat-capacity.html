P4: Specific Heat Capacity

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understanding the relationship between thermal energy, mass, temperature change, and the type of material. Applying the specific heat capacity equation to real-world problems.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define specific heat capacity and explain what it means physically
  • Use the equation E = mcΔT to calculate thermal energy changes
  • Interpret graphs of temperature against time for heating experiments
  • Plan and evaluate an experiment to measure specific heat capacity
  • Apply specific heat capacity to real-world heating and cooling problems

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of thermal energy stores (P1)
  • Knowledge that temperature is a measure of average kinetic energy of particles
  • Ability to rearrange equations and use standard form

Materials & Equipment

  • Scientific calculator
  • Aluminium block with hole for thermometer (or copper block)
  • Electrical immersion heater
  • Thermometer (digital or mercury)
  • Stopwatch
  • Balance (to measure mass)
  • Insulation material (wool or polystyrene)

Lesson 1: What is Specific Heat Capacity?

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Predict and Explain

Place two identical beakers on a bench — one with 200 ml of water, one with 200 ml of cooking oil. Heat both with identical heaters for 5 minutes. Ask: "Which will be hotter? Why?" Students predict and explain.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Different materials heat up and cool down at different rates. This is because they have different specific heat capacities.

Specific heat capacity (c) is the amount of energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1°C (or 1 K).

Units: J/(kg·°C) or J/(kg·K)

Key values to know:
- Water: 4200 J/(kg·°C) — very high, takes lots of energy to heat
- Aluminium: 900 J/(kg·°C)
- Copper: 390 J/(kg·°C)
- Glass: 840 J/(kg·°C)
- Concrete: 880 J/(kg·°C)

Why does water have a high specific heat capacity?
Water molecules are held together by strong hydrogen bonds. Energy must be used to break or stretch these bonds before the molecules can move faster (increase temperature). This means water stores a lot of thermal energy for a small temperature rise.

Practical significance:
- Water is used in central heating systems because it stores lots of thermal energy
- Coastal areas have milder climates because the sea absorbs/releases large amounts of energy
- Car engines use water coolant for the same reason
Worked Example

Calculate the energy needed to heat 2 kg of water from 20°C to 100°C.
E = mcΔT
E = 2 × 4200 × (100 − 20)
E = 2 × 4200 × 80
E = 672 000 J = 672 kJ

Key Equation:
E = mcΔT

E = thermal energy (J)
m = mass (kg)
c = specific heat capacity (J/(kg·°C))
ΔT = temperature change (°C)
Common Misconception: Students often confuse temperature and thermal energy. A swimming pool at 30°C has much more thermal energy than a cup of tea at 80°C because it has far more mass, even though its temperature is lower.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide the equation with each variable clearly labelled and a substitution template. Students plug in values and calculate step by step.
Stretch:
Calculate the energy needed to heat a 50 000 litres swimming pool from 15°C to 30°C. Then calculate the cost using an electricity price of 34p/kWh.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Calculation

How much energy is needed to raise the temperature of 5 kg of aluminium by 40°C? (c = 900 J/(kg·°C)). Answer: 180 000 J = 180 kJ

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can define specific heat capacity and use E = mcΔT to solve problems accurately
  • Getting there: Can use the equation with guidance but struggles with rearranging or converting units
  • Not yet: Cannot apply the equation or confuse specific heat capacity with temperature

Lesson 2: Measuring Specific Heat Capacity

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Experimental Design

"How could you measure the specific heat capacity of a metal block?" Students brainstorm possible methods in pairs. Discuss the challenges (measuring energy input, reducing heat loss).

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Required Practical: Determine the specific heat capacity of a metal block.

Method:
1. Measure the mass of the aluminium block using a balance
2. Place the electrical heater in the hole in the block
3. Insert the thermometer into the block
4. Wrap the block in insulation to reduce heat loss
5. Record the initial temperature
6. Switch on the heater and record the temperature every minute for 10 minutes
7. Record the power of the heater (e.g., 50 W)
8. Calculate the total energy supplied: E = P × t
9. Plot a graph of temperature (y-axis) against time (x-axis)
10. Use the gradient of the straight-line portion to find ΔT per second

Calculation:
Energy supplied in time t: E = P × t
From the graph, find ΔT for the same time t
c = E ÷ (m × ΔT)

Reducing errors:
- Insulate the block to reduce heat loss to surroundings
- Use a low-power heater so temperature rises slowly (reduces heat loss)
- Stir the block if using a liquid (not needed for solid blocks)
- Take multiple readings and calculate a mean

Expected results:
The graph should show a straight line initially (constant heating rate) then curve as heat loss increases. The gradient of the straight portion is used for calculations.
Worked Example

An aluminium block has mass 0.5 kg. A 50 W heater is used for 10 minutes (600 s). The temperature rises from 20°C to 58°C (ΔT = 38°C).
Energy input: E = P × t = 50 × 600 = 30 000 J
c = E ÷ (m × ΔT) = 30 000 ÷ (0.5 × 38) = 30 000 ÷ 19 = 1579 J/(kg·°C)
Accepted value for aluminium = 900 J/(kg·°C). The higher result is due to heat loss being underestimated.

Key Equations:
E = P × t (Energy = Power × Time)
c = E ÷ (m × ΔT)

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a results table with column headings. Students fill in readings from a demonstration video or teacher demo. Pre-calculate some values as examples.
Stretch:
Evaluate sources of error in the experiment. Suggest modifications to improve accuracy. Calculate the percentage error between the experimental and accepted values.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Error Analysis

Discuss: "Why is our experimental value for specific heat capacity often higher than the accepted value?" (Heat loss to surroundings means more energy is needed to achieve the same temperature rise.)

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the full experimental method, explain how to reduce errors, and calculate c from experimental data
  • Getting there: Can follow the method and calculate c but cannot explain how to reduce errors
  • Not yet: Cannot describe the experimental method or perform the calculation

Lesson 3: Applications of Specific Heat Capacity

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Real-World Connection

Students work in pairs to brainstorm reasons why: (a) seaside towns have milder weather than inland towns, (b) car engines use water coolant, (c) underfloor heating uses warm water pipes.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Specific heat capacity has many real-world applications:

1. Climate and weather:
- Water has a high c, so oceans absorb large amounts of heat in summer and release it in winter
- This is why coastal areas have milder winters and cooler summers than inland areas
- Sea breezes form because land heats up faster than the sea during the day

2. Central heating:
- Water is used as a coolant/heating fluid because it has a high c
- Hot water carries lots of thermal energy through radiators
- Underfloor heating uses the high c of water to provide even, consistent heat

3. Cooking:
- Water boils at 100°C and stays at that temperature while boiling (due to high c and latent heat)
- This makes it ideal for cooking — food cooks at a consistent temperature

4. Thermal storage:
- Some solar heating systems use water or special phase-change materials to store heat
- Concrete floors can store daytime solar heat and release it at night

5. Industrial applications:
- Quenching hot metal in water or oil (low c = rapid cooling for hardening)
- Cooling systems in nuclear power stations use water
Worked Example

A storage heater contains 80 kg of concrete (c = 880 J/(kg·°C)). It is heated from 20°C to 70°C during off-peak electricity hours.
(a) Energy stored: E = mcΔT = 80 × 880 × (70 − 20) = 80 × 880 × 50 = 3 520 000 J = 3520 kJ
(b) If the heater releases this energy over 8 hours, average power = E ÷ t = 3 520 000 ÷ (8 × 3600) = 122 W
(c) The concrete slowly releases thermal energy, providing gentle heating throughout the day.

Key Equation:
E = mcΔT
Power released = E ÷ t (where t is in seconds)

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a matching activity: match the application to the reason why specific heat capacity is relevant. Students then write a sentence for each.
Stretch:
Calculate the energy stored in a 200 000 litres water tank heated from 10°C to 60°C. Compare this to the energy stored in the same mass of copper heated to the same temperature. Discuss the practical implications.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

"Explain why water is used as a coolant in car engines, referring to specific heat capacity in your answer." Students write a 3-mark response.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can apply specific heat capacity to real-world scenarios and explain why water's high c makes it useful
  • Getting there: Can describe applications but cannot link them to the concept of specific heat capacity
  • Not yet: Cannot connect specific heat capacity to practical applications

Homework Suggestions

  • Calculate the energy needed to heat a bath (150 litres) from 12°C to 40°C. At 34p/kWh, how much does this cost?
  • Research how storage heaters work and explain the role of specific heat capacity
  • Design an experiment to compare the specific heat capacities of different materials (sand, water, cooking oil)
  • Write an explanation of why the sea breeze blows from the sea to the land during the day
