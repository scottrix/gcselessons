P5: Specific Latent Heat

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understanding the energy required to change the state of a substance without changing its temperature. Calculating energy during melting, freezing, boiling, and condensation.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Explain what happens to particles during a change of state
  • Define specific latent heat and distinguish between fusion and vaporisation
  • Use the equation E = mL to calculate energy during changes of state
  • Interpret heating curves showing temperature and state changes
  • Apply latent heat to real-world scenarios including cooling and heating

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of the three states of matter and particle model
  • Knowledge of specific heat capacity (P4)
  • Understanding of kinetic theory and particle motion

Materials & Equipment

  • Scientific calculator
  • Ice cubes and beakers
  • Stopwatch
  • Electric kettle
  • Thermometer
  • Balance (to measure mass)

Lesson 1: Changes of State and Latent Heat

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Temperature Puzzle

Place ice cubes in a beaker and measure the temperature. It reads 0°C. Heat gently. Ask: "What will happen to the temperature while the ice is melting?" Students predict and explain.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
When a substance changes state (e.g., ice melts to water, water boils to steam), the temperature stays constant even though energy is being added. This is because the energy is used to break or weaken the bonds between particles, not to increase their kinetic energy.

Specific latent heat of fusion (Lf) — the energy needed to melt 1 kg of a solid at its melting point (or the energy released when 1 kg freezes).
- For water: Lf = 334 000 J/kg = 334 kJ/kg

Specific latent heat of vaporisation (Lv) — the energy needed to boil 1 kg of a liquid at its boiling point (or the energy released when 1 kg condenses).
- For water: Lv = 2 260 000 J/kg = 2260 kJ/kg

Key points:
- During melting: particles gain energy to break bonds → solid → liquid (temperature constant)
- During boiling: particles gain enough energy to escape completely → liquid → gas (temperature constant)
- During freezing: particles lose energy and form bonds → liquid → solid (temperature constant)
- During condensation: particles lose energy and bond together → gas → liquid (temperature constant)

Why is Lv much larger than Lf?
When a liquid boils, particles must gain enough energy to completely overcome all intermolecular forces and separate completely. When a solid melts, particles only need enough energy to partially overcome forces so they can move past each other. Much more energy is needed for boiling.
Worked Example

Calculate the energy needed to melt 3 kg of ice at 0°C.
E = mLf
E = 3 × 334 000
E = 1 002 000 J = 1002 kJ

Key Equation:
E = mL

E = thermal energy (J)
m = mass (kg)
L = specific latent heat (J/kg)

For melting/freezing: use Lf (latent heat of fusion)
For boiling/condensing: use Lv (latent heat of vaporisation)
Common Misconception: Students think the temperature rises during melting or boiling. It stays constant until all the substance has changed state. Only then does the temperature start rising again.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a heating curve diagram with labels already added. Students identify which parts represent heating, melting, heating again, and boiling.
Stretch:
Calculate the total energy needed to heat 2 kg of ice at −10°C to steam at 110°C, using both E = mcΔT and E = mL.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Graph Interpretation

Show a heating curve of water from −20°C to 120°C. Students label the axes, identify the melting point and boiling point, and explain why the graph has flat sections.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain why temperature stays constant during state changes and use E = mL accurately
  • Getting there: Can use E = mL but struggles to explain the physics behind constant temperature during state changes
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between specific heat capacity and specific latent heat

Lesson 2: Heating Curves and Combined Calculations

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Two Equations

Students write both equations on mini whiteboards: E = mcΔT and E = mL. Then explain in one sentence when to use each one.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Many exam questions combine both specific heat capacity and specific latent heat. Students must identify which equation to use for each part of the problem.

Heating curve of water:
A heating curve shows temperature on the y-axis and time (or energy added) on the x-axis. The graph has four distinct sections:

1. Ice warming (−20°C to 0°C): straight line with gradient, E = mcΔT
2. Ice melting (at 0°C): horizontal line, E = mLf
3. Water warming (0°C to 100°C): straight line with gradient, E = mcΔT
4. Water boiling (at 100°C): horizontal line, E = mLv

The gradient of each sloped section depends on the specific heat capacity of that state. Ice has a lower c than water, so the ice section has a steeper gradient (heats up faster).
Worked Example

Calculate the total energy needed to heat 1 kg of ice from −15°C to steam at 110°C.

Step 1: Heat ice from −15°C to 0°C
E₁ = mcΔT = 1 × 2100 × 15 = 31 500 J

Step 2: Melt ice at 0°C
E₂ = mLf = 1 × 334 000 = 334 000 J

Step 3: Heat water from 0°C to 100°C
E₃ = mcΔT = 1 × 4200 × 100 = 420 000 J

Step 4: Boil water at 100°C
E₄ = mLv = 1 × 2 260 000 = 2 260 000 J

Step 5: Heat steam from 100°C to 110°C
E₅ = mcΔT = 1 × 2010 × 10 = 20 100 J

Total energy = 31 500 + 334 000 + 420 000 + 2 260 000 + 20 100 = 3 065 600 J ≈ 3066 kJ

Key Equations:
E = mcΔT (for temperature changes)
E = mL (for state changes)

c(ice) = 2100 J/(kg·°C)
c(water) = 4200 J/(kg·°C)
c(steam) = 2010 J/(kg·°C)
Lf(water) = 334 000 J/kg
Lv(water) = 2 260 000 J/kg

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a step-by-step template for multi-stage calculations. Students label each step with the equation used and the values substituted.
Stretch:
Calculate the energy released when 500 g of steam at 100°C condenses and then cools to 20°C. Discuss why this can cause severe burns.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Challenge

How much energy is needed to melt 2 kg of ice at 0°C and then heat the water to 50°C? Students calculate and compare answers.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can solve multi-stage heating problems using both equations and draw/interpret heating curves
  • Getting there: Can solve single-step problems but struggles with multi-stage calculations
  • Not yet: Cannot identify which equation to use for each stage

Lesson 3: Real-World Applications of Latent Heat

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Evaporation Discussion

"Why does getting out of a swimming pool make you feel cold, even on a warm day?" Students discuss in pairs. Introduce the idea that evaporation is a cooling process.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Latent heat has important real-world applications:

1. Evaporation as a cooling process:
- When a liquid evaporates, the most energetic particles escape, leaving behind cooler particles
- The average kinetic energy of the remaining liquid decreases → temperature drops
- Applications: sweating cools the body, refrigerators, cooling towers

2. Factors affecting evaporation rate:
- Higher temperature → faster evaporation
- Greater surface area → faster evaporation
- Wind blowing over surface → faster evaporation
- Lower humidity → faster evaporation

3. Refrigeration:
- A refrigerant liquid evaporates inside the fridge (absorbs latent heat → cools the interior)
- The gas is then compressed and condenses outside the fridge (releases latent heat → the back of the fridge gets warm)

4. Steam burns:
- Steam at 100°C causes worse burns than water at 100°C
- When steam hits skin, it first condenses, releasing 2 260 000 J/kg of latent heat
- Then the condensed water cools, releasing more thermal energy (E = mcΔT)

5. Weather phenomena:
- Clouds form when water vapour condenses, releasing latent heat into the atmosphere
- This latent heat release powers thunderstorms and hurricanes
- Frost forms when water vapour freezes directly onto cold surfaces (deposition)
Worked Example

Compare the energy released when 0.01 kg (10 g) of steam at 100°C hits skin versus 0.01 kg of water at 100°C:

Steam: Latent heat released = mLv = 0.01 × 2 260 000 = 22 600 J
Then water cools to body temperature (37°C):
Thermal energy released = mcΔT = 0.01 × 4200 × 63 = 2646 J
Total from steam: 22 600 + 2646 = 25 246 J

Water at 100°C: Only thermal energy released = mcΔT = 0.01 × 4200 × 63 = 2646 J

Steam releases nearly 10 times more energy — this is why steam burns are so severe.

Key Equations:
E = mL (latent heat during state change)
E = mcΔT (thermal energy during temperature change)

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a diagram of a refrigerator with arrows showing where latent heat is absorbed and released. Students label the energy transfers.
Stretch:
Calculate the energy released when a thunderstorm condenses 1 million kg of water vapour. Discuss why this energy release affects weather patterns.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

"A student says that steam at 100°C causes more severe burns than water at 100°C because steam has more thermal energy. Explain whether this statement is correct." Students write a 3-mark answer.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain how latent heat applies to evaporation, refrigeration, and steam burns using correct terminology and calculations
  • Getting there: Can describe applications but cannot link them to the concept of latent heat quantitatively
  • Not yet: Cannot explain why evaporation is a cooling process or why steam burns are worse

Homework Suggestions

  • Calculate the energy needed to freeze 500 g of water at 0°C to ice at 0°C
  • Explain how a desert cooler works, using ideas about evaporation and latent heat
  • Research how cloud seeding works and its relationship to latent heat release
  • Write a comparison of the energy released by 10 g of steam at 100°C versus 10 g of water at 100°C, showing all calculations
