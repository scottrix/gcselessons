P1: Energy Stores and Transfers

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understanding energy as a conserved quantity that is stored in different ways and transferred between stores. Introduces the six main energy stores and the mechanisms of energy transfer.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Identify the six main energy stores and give examples of each
  • Distinguish between energy stores and energy transfer mechanisms
  • Describe how energy is transferred between stores in common scenarios
  • Use the concept of conservation of energy to calculate energy in different stores
  • Draw and interpret Sankey diagrams for energy transfers

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of work and forces (from KS3)
  • Ability to rearrange simple formulae
  • Familiarity with units of measurement

Materials & Equipment

  • Scientific calculator
  • Ruler and protractor (for Sankey diagrams)
  • Stopwatch
  • Rubber band, ruler, and tape (for elastic store practical)
  • Ramp, toy car, and blocks (for gravitational store practical)

Lesson 1: Energy Stores

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Think-Pair-Share

Show students a photo of a roller coaster at the top of a hill. Ask: "Where is the energy in this system right now? What will happen to it as the coaster goes down?" Collect a few ideas on the board without correcting them yet.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Energy cannot be created or destroyed — it is always conserved. However, it can be stored in different ways. We identify six main energy stores:

1. Kinetic energy store — energy of a moving object. The faster an object moves, the more kinetic energy it has. A moving car, a thrown ball, or flowing water all have kinetic energy.

2. Gravitational potential energy store — energy stored by an object's position in a gravitational field. Lifting an object higher increases its gravitational potential energy. A ball held above the ground, water behind a dam, or a person at the top of a hill all store gravitational potential energy.

3. Elastic potential energy store — energy stored when an object is stretched, compressed, or bent. A drawn bow, a compressed spring, or a stretched rubber band all store elastic potential energy.

4. Thermal energy store — energy stored due to the temperature of an object. The hotter an object is, the more thermal energy it has. A hot cup of tea, warm hands, or a heated metal rod all have thermal energy.

5. Chemical energy store — energy stored in the bonds between atoms and molecules. Food, fuel, batteries, and wood all store chemical energy. When chemical reactions occur, this energy is released.

6. Magnetic energy store — energy stored in a magnetic field. Two repelling magnets pushed together store magnetic energy. Electromagnets also store energy in their magnetic fields.
Worked Example

A student lists the following scenarios. For each, identify the main energy store:
(a) A roller coaster at the top of a hill — Gravitational potential
(b) A lit candle — Chemical (in the wax) and thermal (in the flame)
(c) A stretched catapult — Elastic potential
(d) A moving bicycle — Kinetic
(e) A battery-powered torch — Chemical (in the battery)

Common Misconception: Students often think "heat" is a type of energy transfer. In the store model, we use "thermal energy store" for the energy stored due to temperature. Heat (or thermal energy transfer) is the mechanism by which thermal energy is transferred — it is NOT a store.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a matching activity where students match energy store cards to everyday objects. Use a simplified table with pictures of objects and space to write the energy store.
Stretch:
Ask students to consider a system where multiple energy stores are involved simultaneously. For example, a dam holding back water stores gravitational potential energy, and when the water flows through a turbine, it gains kinetic energy.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Fire Round

Show five objects or scenarios on the board. Students write down the main energy store for each on mini whiteboards. Discuss any disagreements.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can correctly identify all six energy stores and give at least two examples of each
  • Getting there: Can identify most energy stores but confuses thermal/kinetic or misses the distinction between stores and transfers
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between energy stores or confuses them with energy transfer mechanisms

Lesson 2: Energy Transfers

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Quick Recall

Students list all six energy stores from memory in 60 seconds. Then add: "What are the four mechanisms by which energy can be transferred?" (heating, radiation, electrical work, mechanical work)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Energy is transferred from one store to another by four main mechanisms:

1. Heating (thermal conduction, convection) — energy transferred from a hotter object to a cooler one. A heater warms a room; hot water heats a cup of tea.

2. Radiation — energy transferred by electromagnetic waves (including infrared, light, microwaves). The Sun warms the Earth by radiation. A hot oven radiates infrared.

3. Electrical work — energy transferred by an electric current flowing through a component. A bulb converts electrical energy to thermal and light energy. A motor converts electrical energy to kinetic energy.

4. Mechanical work — energy transferred by a force acting over a distance. Lifting a box transfers energy from your chemical store to the box's gravitational potential store. Friction transfers energy from kinetic to thermal.

The principle of conservation of energy states that the total energy before a transfer equals the total energy after. Energy is never lost — it is just transferred to different stores, often to thermal energy stores due to friction.
Worked Example

A ball is dropped from 5 m. It hits the ground and bounces to 3 m.
Energy transfers:
1. Ball at 5 m: gravitational potential store is maximum
2. Ball falling: gravitational potential → kinetic
3. Ball hits ground: kinetic → thermal (in ball and ground) + sound
4. Ball bounces up: kinetic → gravitational potential

The ball doesn't reach 5 m again because some energy was transferred to thermal and sound energy stores during the impact.

Sankey Diagrams

A Sankey diagram shows energy transfers visually. The width of each arrow represents the amount of energy. For example, a 100 W bulb receives 100 J of electrical energy per second. About 5 J becomes light energy and 95 J becomes thermal energy. Draw a wide arrow going right (electrical input), with a narrow arrow branching up (light) and a wide arrow branching down (thermal).

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a template Sankey diagram with arrows already drawn. Students label the energy stores and shade the arrows to show relative amounts of energy.
Stretch:
Challenge students to draw a complete Sankey diagram for an electric kettle, including the useful energy (heating water) and wasted energy (heating the kettle body, heating the surrounding air, sound).

Plenary (5 minutes)

Energy Transfer Chain

Students write a step-by-step description of energy transfers when a car brakes to a stop: Chemical → Kinetic → Thermal (due to friction between brake pads and discs).

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe the four energy transfer mechanisms and draw accurate Sankey diagrams with correct proportions
  • Getting there: Can name the transfer mechanisms but struggles with Sankey diagram proportions or misses some transfers
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between energy stores and transfer mechanisms

Lesson 3: Conservation of Energy

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Scenario Discussion

"A pendulum swings back and forth. Why does it eventually stop?" Students discuss in pairs, then share ideas. Use this to introduce the idea that energy is dissipated (spread to thermal stores) due to air resistance and friction at the pivot.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The law of conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed — the total energy in a closed system remains constant.

In everyday situations, we often see objects slow down, stop, or cool down. This does NOT mean energy is destroyed. It means energy has been transferred to thermal energy stores due to friction or air resistance.

Dissipated energy is energy that has been transferred to thermal stores in a way that makes it difficult to use again. For example:
- A car engine: chemical → kinetic, but also chemical → thermal (engine gets hot)
- Friction when sliding: kinetic → thermal (object and surface warm up)
- Sound: kinetic → thermal (sound waves heat the air slightly)

Efficiency is the proportion of energy transferred to useful stores:
Efficiency = (Useful energy output ÷ Total energy input) × 100%

No machine is 100% efficient because some energy is always dissipated.
Worked Example

A kettle has a power rating of 2500 W. When used for 3 minutes (180 s), 400 000 J of energy is transferred to the water as thermal energy.
(a) Total energy input = Power × time = 2500 × 180 = 450 000 J
(b) Efficiency = (400 000 ÷ 450 000) × 100% = 88.9%
(c) Wasted energy = 450 000 − 400 000 = 50 000 J (transferred to heating the kettle body and surrounding air)

Key Formulae:
Energy (J) = Power (W) × Time (s)
Efficiency = (Useful energy output ÷ Total energy input) × 100%

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a scaffolded worksheet with energy flow diagrams partially completed. Students fill in the energy values and calculate efficiency.
Stretch:
Ask students to calculate the cost of running a 2000 W heater for 2 hours at 34p per kWh. They must convert between joules and kilowatt-hours and consider efficiency losses.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

"A toy car rolls down a ramp. Explain, using ideas about energy stores, why the car does not reach the same height on the other side of a second ramp." Students write a model answer using correct terminology.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can apply conservation of energy to calculate energy values and efficiency. Can explain where "lost" energy goes using correct store terminology.
  • Getting there: Can calculate efficiency but struggles to explain energy dissipation in context.
  • Not yet: Thinks energy is "used up" or "destroyed" rather than transferred.

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a poster showing the six energy stores with real-world examples and photos
  • Draw Sankey diagrams for three different household appliances (e.g. toaster, lamp, phone charger)
  • Calculate the efficiency of three appliances in your home and identify where energy is wasted
  • Write a paragraph explaining why a pendulum eventually stops, using energy store terminology
