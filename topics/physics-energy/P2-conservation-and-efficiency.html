P2: Conservation and Efficiency

Foundation Higher All Boards

Deepening understanding of energy conservation, efficiency calculations, and the role of dissipated energy in real-world systems.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Explain the law of conservation of energy
  • Calculate efficiency using energy values and power ratings
  • Draw and interpret Sankey diagrams accurately
  • Identify useful and wasted energy in real-world systems
  • Evaluate ways to improve the efficiency of devices

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of the six energy stores (P1)
  • Knowledge of energy transfer mechanisms (heating, radiation, electrical work, mechanical work)
  • Ability to rearrange formulae and work with percentages

Materials & Equipment

  • Scientific calculator
  • Ruler and protractor
  • Rubber band and marble (for dissipated energy demonstration)
  • Stopwatch

Lesson 1: Efficiency Calculations

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Prediction Task

Show students two light bulbs: a 60 W incandescent bulb and a 10 W LED bulb that produce the same brightness. Ask: "Which is more efficient? How would you measure this?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Efficiency compares useful energy output to total energy input. No device is 100% efficient — some energy is always transferred to thermal stores (dissipated).

Efficiency as a fraction:
Efficiency = Useful energy output ÷ Total energy input

Efficiency as a percentage:
Efficiency = (Useful energy output ÷ Total energy input) × 100%

Typical efficiencies:
- Incandescent light bulb: ~5% (95% wasted as thermal)
- LED light bulb: ~40%
- Car engine: ~25-30%
- Electric kettle: ~85-90%
- Human body: ~25% (movement), ~0% if you consider food → movement as the input

Improving efficiency means reducing wasted energy. This can be done by:
- Reducing friction (lubrication, ball bearings)
- Reducing air resistance (streamlined shapes)
- Better insulation (reducing thermal energy loss)
- Using more efficient components (LED vs incandescent)
Worked Example

An electric motor lifts a 2 kg box to a height of 3 m in 4 seconds. The motor is connected to a 12 V supply and draws a current of 1.5 A.

Useful energy output = weight × height = mg × h = 2 × 10 × 3 = 60 J
Total energy input = V × I × t = 12 × 1.5 × 4 = 72 J
Efficiency = (60 ÷ 72) × 100% = 83.3%

Key Formulae:
Efficiency = (Useful energy output ÷ Total energy input) × 100%
Energy (J) = Power (W) × Time (s)
Electrical energy (J) = Voltage (V) × Current (A) × Time (s)

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a formula triangle for efficiency and a worked example with all steps shown. Students follow the same method with different numbers.
Stretch:
Challenge students to calculate the efficiency of a hydroelectric power station, considering the gravitational potential energy of water in a dam and the electrical energy output.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Calculation

A washing machine uses 2000 W of electrical power. It transfers 1800 J to heating the water each second. What is its efficiency? (Answer: 90%)

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can calculate efficiency from energy or power values and convert between fraction and percentage
  • Getting there: Can calculate efficiency when given clear energy values but struggles with multi-step problems
  • Not yet: Cannot apply the efficiency formula or confuses useful and total energy

Lesson 2: Sankey Diagrams and Energy Flow

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Diagram Analysis

Show a Sankey diagram without labels. Ask students to work out what device it represents and identify which arrows are useful energy and which are wasted.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
A Sankey diagram shows energy transfers visually. The rules for drawing Sankey diagrams:

1. The total width of all arrows must be the same (conservation of energy)
2. Arrows always flow left to right
3. Useful energy continues straight ahead
4. Wasted energy branches downward
5. The width of each arrow is proportional to the energy it represents

Example: 100 W light bulb (1 second)
- Input: 100 J electrical energy
- Useful output: 5 J light energy
- Wasted output: 95 J thermal energy

The input arrow is 100 units wide. The useful output arrow is 5 units wide. The wasted output arrow is 95 units wide. Total output = 100 units = input.
Worked Example

Draw a Sankey diagram for a car engine:
- Fuel provides 100 000 J of chemical energy
- 25 000 J becomes kinetic (useful)
- 75 000 J is wasted as thermal energy

Draw a wide arrow from left (100 units) splitting into: a narrow arrow going right (25 units) and a wide arrow going down (75 units). Use a ruler and scale: e.g., 1 cm = 5000 J.

Common Misconception: Students often draw the output arrows wider than the input arrow. Emphasise that energy is conserved — the total width of outputs MUST equal the width of the input.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide pre-drawn Sankey diagram templates with grid lines. Students label the energy stores and calculate the correct widths using a given scale.
Stretch:
Ask students to draw a Sankey diagram for a power station that is 35% efficient, using a scale of 1 cm = 100 MJ. They must calculate the useful and wasted energy from the total input.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Efficiency Ranking

Students rank five devices from most to least efficient based on their Sankey diagrams: LED bulb, incandescent bulb, car engine, electric kettle, human body during exercise.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can draw accurate Sankey diagrams with correct proportions and identify useful/wasted energy
  • Getting there: Can draw Sankey diagrams but proportions are approximate rather than accurate
  • Not yet: Cannot correctly proportion arrows or confuse useful and wasted outputs

Lesson 3: Reducing Energy Waste

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Household Audit

Students list five appliances in their home and estimate which are most and least efficient. Discuss: "How could you reduce energy waste at home?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Reducing energy waste is important for both cost savings and environmental reasons. Key strategies:

1. Insulation — reduces thermal energy transfer from heated buildings. Loft insulation, cavity wall insulation, and double-glazed windows all reduce heat loss.

2. Reducing friction — lubrication and ball bearings reduce the amount of kinetic energy converted to thermal energy in machines.

3. Streamlining — reduces air resistance, meaning less kinetic energy is transferred to the surrounding air as thermal energy.

4. LED lighting — uses ~80% less electrical energy than incandescent bulbs for the same light output.

5. Efficient engines — modern car engines convert more chemical energy to kinetic energy. Hybrid and electric vehicles improve efficiency further.

6. Combined Heat and Power (CHP) — power stations that capture waste heat from electricity generation and use it to heat homes, increasing overall efficiency from ~35% to ~80%.
Worked Example

A house has 10 × 60 W incandescent bulbs, each on for 5 hours per day.
Energy used per day = 10 × 60 × 5 = 3000 Wh = 3 kWh
Replacing with 10 W LEDs: 10 × 10 × 5 = 500 Wh = 0.5 kWh
Saving per day = 2.5 kWh
At 34p per kWh: 2.5 × 0.34 = £0.85 per day = £310 per year

Key Formula:
Cost = Energy (kWh) × Price per kWh (£/kWh)
1 kWh = 1000 Wh = 3 600 000 J

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a table with pre-filled energy values. Students calculate the savings from switching to efficient alternatives.
Stretch:
Students design a "green house" plan, specifying insulation, efficient appliances, and renewable energy sources. They calculate the annual energy savings compared to a standard house.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

"Explain why no machine can ever be 100% efficient." Students write a paragraph using energy store terminology and the concept of dissipated energy.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain multiple ways to reduce energy waste and calculate cost savings from efficient alternatives
  • Getting there: Can identify ways to reduce waste but cannot perform cost calculations accurately
  • Not yet: Cannot connect efficiency improvements to real-world energy saving

Homework Suggestions

  • Calculate the efficiency of five household appliances using energy meter readings or manufacturer data
  • Draw Sankey diagrams for a television, a laptop charger, and a shower
  • Write a report on how your family could reduce energy waste at home, including cost estimates
  • Research and present on one method of improving energy efficiency in power stations (e.g. CHP, cogeneration)
