Lesson 2: Core Concepts: statistical enquiry cycle in practice

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Review Previous Lesson

Quick recap: write 3 key points from Lesson 1 on statistical enquiry cycle in practice. Check them against the notes below.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Key Fact: The Statistical Enquiry Cycle has four stages: (1) Plan (hypothesis and strategy), (2) Collect (obtain data), (3) Process (calculate and represent), (4) Interpret (analyse and conclude)
Key Fact: Exam questions often ask you to identify which stage a given task belongs to or to describe what happens at each stage
Key Fact: Planning includes: writing a clear hypothesis, identifying the population, choosing a sampling method, deciding what data to collect and how to collect it
Key Fact: Data collection includes: designing data collection sheets, pilot studies, dealing with non-response, and ensuring data is reliable and unbiased
Key Fact: Processing includes: cleaning data, calculating summary statistics, and choosing appropriate diagrams or charts to display the data
Key Fact: Interpretation includes: describing patterns, comparing groups, linking findings to the hypothesis, and acknowledging limitations

Main Content (35 minutes)

Extended Answer

Extended question: Full-Mark Response A researcher investigates the hypothesis: ‘People who walk to work have lower blood pressure than people who drive to work.’ They take a random sample of 50 walkers and 50 drivers from a city and measure their blood pressure. (a) Describe the data collection stage. (b) Write a conclusion if the median blood pressure for walkers is 120/78 and for drivers is 134/86. <div class="

(a) The data collection stage involves measuring the blood pressure of 100 participants (50 walkers, 50 drivers). The sampling method is random, which reduces selection bias. Data is primary (collected firsthand) and quantitative (numerical blood pressure readings). A pilot study could test the measurement protocol. (b) The data supports the hypothesis. Walkers have a lower median systolic blood pressure (120 vs 134, a difference of 14 mmHg) and a lower median diastolic blood pressure (78 vs 86, a difference of 8 mmHg). Both groups show a clear difference that suggests walking to work is associated with lower blood pressure. However, this is an observational study so causation cannot be established — people who walk may also have other healthy habits. A larger sample and controlling for confounding variables (e.g. age, diet) would strengthen the conclusion.

Exam Tips: When a question names a stage of the SEC, give specific detail about what happens — do not just repeat the stage name | Always link conclusions back to the original hypothesis using specific data values from the question | In evaluation questions, give at least two points: one strength and one weakness, each explained with reasoning | When asked to design an investigation, structure your answer using the four SEC stages to ensure completeness | Marks for SEC questions often come from ‘justification’ — always explain why a method is chosen, not just what it is
Common Errors: ✗ Writing vague conclusions without referencing data or statistics from the question ✓ Always quote specific values: ‘The median for Group A (34) was higher than Group B (27)’ ✗ Confusing reliability and validity ✓ Reliability = consistency on repetition; validity = measuring what you intend to measure ✗ Forgetting to evaluate limitations when writing conclusions ✓ A strong conclusion acknowledges what the data cannot tell us, e.g. small sample size or possible bias ✗ Describing only what was done without explaining why at each SEC stage ✓ Explain reasoning: ‘Stratified sampling was used because the year groups differ in size’
