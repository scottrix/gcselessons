P8: Electrical Power and Energy

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understanding electrical power, energy transfer in circuits, and calculating the cost of electricity. Converting between joules and kilowatt-hours.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define power and state its unit (watt)
  • Use P = IV, P = I²R, and P = V²/R to calculate electrical power
  • Convert between joules and kilowatt-hours
  • Calculate the cost of running electrical appliances
  • Explain the relationship between power, energy, and time

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of V = IR (P6)
  • Knowledge of series and parallel circuits (P7)
  • Ability to work with units and convert between them

Materials & Equipment

  • Scientific calculator
  • Appliance labels from household items (for data collection)
  • Electricity bill sample (for real-world context)

Lesson 1: Electrical Power

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Power Comparison

Students compare the power ratings of: phone charger (5 W), LED bulb (10 W), laptop (65 W), TV (120 W), kettle (2500 W). Ask: "What does a higher power rating mean? Why does a kettle need so much more power than a phone charger?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Power is the rate at which energy is transferred. The unit is the watt (W), where 1 W = 1 J/s.

Three equations for electrical power:
1. P = V × I (most fundamental — voltage × current)
2. P = I² × R (useful when current and resistance are known)
3. P = V² ÷ R (useful when voltage and resistance are known)

These can be derived by substituting V = IR into P = VI.

Key points:
- A 100 W bulb transfers 100 J of energy every second
- A 2500 W kettle transfers 2500 J every second — 25 times more
- Higher power means faster energy transfer
- Power rating tells you how much energy an appliance uses per second

Energy transferred by electrical appliances:
E = P × t (Energy = Power × Time)
- E is in joules (J) when P is in watts (W) and t is in seconds (s)
- E is in kilowatt-hours (kWh) when P is in kilowatts (kW) and t is in hours (h)
Worked Example

A 2000 W heater is used for 30 minutes.
(a) Energy in joules: E = P × t = 2000 × (30 × 60) = 2000 × 1800 = 3 600 000 J = 3600 kJ
(b) Energy in kWh: P = 2 kW, t = 0.5 h, E = 2 × 0.5 = 1 kWh

Key Equations:
P = V × I
P = I²R
P = V² ÷ R
E = P × t
Common Misconception: Students think power and energy are the same thing. Power is the RATE of energy transfer (J/s). Energy is the TOTAL amount transferred (J). A 100 W bulb on for 10 hours uses more energy than a 2000 W kettle on for 1 minute.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a table of common appliance power ratings. Students calculate the energy used by each over 1 hour.
Stretch:
A car headlight bulb has resistance 3 Ω and runs at 12 V. Calculate the power, the current, and the energy used in a 5-hour night drive.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Calculations

(1) A 12 V battery supplies 3 A to a motor. What is the power? (36 W)
(2) A 60 W bulb runs for 2 hours. How much energy is transferred? (120 Wh = 0.12 kWh)

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can use all three power equations and calculate energy from power and time
  • Getting there: Can use P = VI but struggles with P = I²R and P = V²/R
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between power and energy or use the equations

Lesson 2: Kilowatt-Hours and Cost

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Bill Analysis

Show a sample electricity bill (or create one). Point out the units used (kWh) and the price per unit. Ask: "How does the electricity company calculate how much you pay?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Electricity is measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh). This is the energy used by a 1 kW appliance in 1 hour.

Converting between units:
1 kWh = 1 kW × 1 h = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3 600 000 J = 3.6 MJ

Calculating cost:
Cost = Number of kWh × Price per kWh

Example electricity prices (UK, 2025):
- Typical unit rate: ~34p per kWh
- Standing charge: ~60p per day (fixed cost regardless of usage)

How to read an electricity meter:
- The meter shows total kWh used
- Take two readings a month apart
- Difference = kWh used in that period

Strategies to reduce electricity bills:
- Use LED bulbs (10 W vs 60 W incandescent)
- Turn off appliances rather than leaving on standby
- Use a tumble dryer less (very high power, ~2500 W)
- Use a timer for heating
Worked Example

A家庭 uses the following appliances per day:
- 5 × 10 W LED bulbs for 6 hours: 5 × 0.01 × 6 = 0.3 kWh
- 150 W TV for 4 hours: 0.15 × 4 = 0.6 kWh
- 2000 W kettle for 15 minutes: 2 × 0.25 = 0.5 kWh
- 2000 W washing machine for 1 hour: 2 × 1 = 2 kWh

Daily total = 0.3 + 0.6 + 0.5 + 2 = 3.4 kWh
Monthly (30 days) = 3.4 × 30 = 102 kWh
Cost = 102 × £0.34 = £34.68 per month

Key Equations:
E (kWh) = P (kW) × t (h)
1 kWh = 3 600 000 J
Cost = E (kWh) × Price per kWh

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a table with appliances, power ratings, and hours of use. Students calculate kWh for each and total cost.
Stretch:
Calculate the annual cost of running a 100 W incandescent bulb for 5 hours/day vs a 10 W LED for the same time. Calculate the savings and the payback period if LEDs cost £8 each.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Real-World Calculation

Students calculate the cost of charging a phone (5 W) for 3 hours every day for a month. Compare this to running a 2500 W kettle for 5 minutes daily for the same period.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can convert between J and kWh, calculate electricity costs, and compare appliance running costs
  • Getting there: Can calculate kWh but struggles with cost calculations or unit conversions
  • Not yet: Cannot convert between joules and kilowatt-hours

Lesson 3: Efficiency in Electrical Circuits

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Where Does It Go?

A 60 W incandescent bulb converts electrical energy to light and thermal energy. Ask: "If the bulb is 5% efficient, how much power becomes light? How much becomes thermal?" (3 W light, 57 W thermal)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
In electrical circuits, power is transferred to components. Some components convert electrical energy usefully (e.g., a motor converts to kinetic), while others dissipate energy (e.g., a resistor converts to thermal).

Power dissipated in a resistor:
P = I²R — this is the rate at which electrical energy is converted to thermal energy in a resistor.

Useful power vs wasted power:
- In a motor: useful power = mechanical output, wasted = thermal (due to resistance of coils)
- In a bulb: useful power = light output, wasted = thermal
- In a resistor: all power is "wasted" as thermal energy (but this may be the intended function, e.g., a heater)

Efficiency of electrical appliances:
Efficiency = (Useful power output ÷ Electrical power input) × 100%

Examples:
- LED bulb: ~40% efficient (60% wasted as heat)
- Incandescent bulb: ~5% efficient (95% wasted as heat)
- Electric motor: ~85-90% efficient
- Electric kettle: ~85-90% efficient
- Power station generator: ~95% efficient (but whole station ~35-40%)
Worked Example

A 12 V motor draws 2.5 A. It lifts a 4 kg mass at 0.5 m/s.
(a) Electrical power input: P_in = V × I = 12 × 2.5 = 30 W
(b) Useful mechanical power output: P_out = force × velocity = mg × v = 4 × 10 × 0.5 = 20 W
(c) Efficiency = (20 ÷ 30) × 100% = 66.7%
(d) Wasted power (thermal): 30 − 20 = 10 W (dissipated in motor coils due to resistance)

Key Equations:
Efficiency = (Useful power output ÷ Electrical power input) × 100%
P_dissipated = I²R (thermal power in resistor)

Differentiation

Support:
Provide efficiency calculations with all steps shown. Students follow the method with different values.
Stretch:
A power station generates 500 MW at 25 kV. The current in transmission cables is 5000 A, and cable resistance is 2 Ω. Calculate the power lost in the cables and the efficiency of transmission.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

"A 230 V kettle has a power rating of 2500 W and is 90% efficient. Calculate the current drawn and the useful power transferred to the water." Students solve and compare answers.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can calculate efficiency of electrical appliances and identify where energy is wasted
  • Getting there: Can calculate efficiency but cannot identify the energy transfers in different appliances
  • Not yet: Cannot link power, efficiency, and energy dissipation in circuits

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a table of 10 household appliances with their power ratings and calculate the daily cost of running each for 1 hour
  • Calculate how much money you could save per year by replacing all incandescent bulbs with LEDs
  • Research the power consumption of a games console and calculate the cost of gaming for 3 hours per day
  • Write an explanation of why power stations need to generate more electrical power than homes actually use
