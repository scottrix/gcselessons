P9: Static Electricity

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understanding how objects become electrically charged through friction, the forces between charged objects, and practical applications and hazards of static electricity.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Explain how objects become charged by friction
  • Describe the forces between charged and uncharged objects
  • Draw electric field patterns around charged objects
  • Describe practical applications of static electricity
  • Explain the hazards of static electricity and how they are managed

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of atoms and electrons (from chemistry)
  • Knowledge that electrons are negatively charged
  • Understanding of forces (from KS3)

Materials & Equipment

  • Scientific calculator
  • Balloon and wool cloth
  • Plastic ruler and hair/fabric
  • Small pieces of paper or polystyrene beads
  • Van de Graaff generator (if available, for demonstration)

Lesson 1: Charging by Friction and Electric Fields

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Balloon Trick

Rub a balloon on a wool jumper and stick it to the wall. Ask: "Why does the balloon stick? Is the wall electrically charged?" Students discuss and predict.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
How objects become charged:
1. Atoms are neutral — they have equal numbers of protons (+) and electrons (−)
2. When two insulating materials are rubbed together, electrons are transferred from one material to the other
3. The material that gains electrons becomes negatively charged
4. The material that loses electrons becomes positively charged
5. Charge is conserved — total charge before = total charge after

Key points:
- Only electrons move — protons are fixed in the nucleus
- Charged objects attract neutral objects (induction)
- Like charges repel, opposite charges attract
- Static charge stays on insulators (conductors allow charge to flow away)

Examples of charging by friction:
- Rubbing a balloon on wool → balloon gains electrons (−), wool becomes positive (+)
- Walking on carpet in socks → socks gain electrons (−)
- Rubbing a plastic ruler on hair → ruler gains electrons (−), hair becomes positive (+)

Electric fields:
A charged object creates an electric field around it. The field exerts a force on other charged objects within it.
- Field lines go from positive to negative
- Closer field lines = stronger field
- Field lines never cross

Field patterns:
- Isolated positive charge: field lines radiate outward
- Isolated negative charge: field lines radiate inward
- Two opposite charges: field lines connect from + to −
- Two like charges: field lines repel each other
Worked Example

A plastic rod is rubbed with a cloth. The rod gains 2 × 10⁻⁷ C of negative charge. Explain what has happened to the cloth and why the rod can now attract small pieces of paper.

The cloth has lost 2 × 10⁻⁷ C of electrons, so it now has a positive charge of 2 × 10⁻⁷ C (charge is conserved).
The rod attracts paper because the negative rod induces a positive charge on the near side of the paper by repelling electrons in the paper to the far side. The attractive force is stronger than the repulsive force because the positive charges are closer.

Key Principle:
Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.
Charge is always conserved — total charge in a closed system remains constant.
Common Misconception: Students think the wall (or paper) must be charged for the balloon to stick. The wall is neutral — the charged balloon induces a temporary opposite charge on the surface by rearranging electrons in the wall.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a table with pairs of materials and ask students to predict which becomes positive and which becomes negative when rubbed together.
Stretch:
Students draw electric field patterns for: (a) an isolated positive charge, (b) two opposite charges, (c) two like charges. Label field direction and indicate field strength.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Charge Transfer

Students explain step by step what happens when a balloon is rubbed on a wool jumper: which particles move, in which direction, and what charges result.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain charging by friction, describe forces between charges, and draw electric field patterns
  • Getting there: Can describe charging by friction but cannot draw field patterns or explain attraction of neutral objects
  • Not yet: Cannot explain how objects become charged or confuse proton and electron movement

Lesson 2: Applications and Hazards of Static

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Static Examples

Students list situations where static electricity is useful (e.g., photocopiers, spray painting) and where it is dangerous (e.g., refuelling aircraft, filling petrol tanks). Discuss why.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Applications of static electricity:

1. Photocopier (xerography):
- A photosensitive drum is charged positively in the pattern of the image
- Toner particles (negatively charged) are attracted to the positively charged areas
- Paper is pressed against the drum, transferring the toner
- Heat fuses the toner to the paper

2. Electrostatic spray painting:
- Paint droplets are given a negative charge
- The object to be painted is grounded (positive)
- Charged paint is attracted to the object, giving an even coat
- Less paint is wasted (overspray reduced by up to 60%)

3. Smoke precipitator:
- Used in power stations and factories to remove smoke particles
- Smoke particles are given a charge and attracted to oppositely charged plates
- Particles collect on the plates and are cleaned off periodically

Hazards of static electricity:

1. Aircraft refuelling:
- Fuel flowing through pipes creates static charge on the aircraft
- A spark could ignite fuel vapour
- Solution: aircraft are earthed/grounded during refuelling with a metal cable

2. Petrol tanker trucks:
- Fuel sloshing in the tank creates static charge
- Solution: a metal chain drags along the ground to earth the truck

3. Flammable environments:
- Static sparks can ignite gas or dust in the air
- Solution: earth all equipment, use anti-static clothing, humidify the air

4. Computer components:
- Static discharge can damage electronic circuits
- Solution: handle components with anti-static wrist straps
Worked Example

A smoke precipitator removes 95% of particles from 1000 m³ of smoke per minute. The initial particle concentration is 50 mg/m³.
(a) Particles entering per minute: 1000 × 50 = 50 000 mg = 50 g
(b) Particles removed per minute: 50 × 0.95 = 47.5 g
(c) Particles remaining per minute: 50 − 47.5 = 2.5 g
(d) Concentration after precipitator: 2.5 g ÷ 1000 m³ = 2.5 mg/m³

Key Principle:
Static charge can be dangerous in the presence of flammable materials.
Earthing (grounding) provides a path for charge to flow away safely.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a matching activity: match the application to a description of how it works. Students then write a step-by-step explanation for one application.
Stretch:
Students design a safety poster for a petrol station, explaining the hazards of static electricity and the precautions that should be taken, with diagrams.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Exam-Style Question

"Explain how a photocopier uses static electricity to produce copies. Use the words 'charge', 'attract', and 'toner' in your answer." Students write a 3-mark response.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain at least two applications and two hazards of static electricity with detailed descriptions
  • Getting there: Can describe applications but cannot explain how they use static electricity in detail
  • Not yet: Cannot link static electricity to practical applications or hazards

Homework Suggestions

  • Research how an electrostatic precipitator works and write a 200-word explanation
  • Investigate why the fuel in aircraft is sometimes deliberately water-contaminated before refuelling (to reduce static)
  • Create a diagram showing how electrostatic spray painting works, with labels for each stage
  • Write a safety guide for handling computer components, explaining why anti-static precautions are needed
