P6: Circuit Basics

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understanding current, voltage, and resistance in simple circuits. Using Ohm's law to calculate values and interpreting circuit diagrams and graphs.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 4
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Draw and interpret circuit diagrams using standard symbols
  • Define current, voltage, and resistance and state their units
  • Use the equation V = IR to calculate voltage, current, and resistance
  • Describe how current varies in series and parallel circuits
  • Investigate the relationship between current, voltage, and resistance

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of electrical safety
  • Knowledge of energy stores and transfers (P1)
  • Ability to rearrange equations

Materials & Equipment

  • Scientific calculator
  • Component circuit kit (wires, bulbs, resistors, switches, cells)
  • Ammeter and voltmeter
  • Rheostat or variable resistor
  • Power supply (low voltage, e.g., 12 V)

Lesson 1: Current, Voltage, and Resistance

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Circuit Challenge

Show students a simple circuit diagram with a cell, bulb, and switch. Ask them to draw it and predict what happens when the switch is closed. Discuss: "What is actually moving in the wire?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Electric current is the flow of electric charge around a circuit. In metal wires, the charge carriers are electrons. Current is measured in amperes (A) using an ammeter connected in series.

Voltage (potential difference) is the energy transferred per unit charge between two points in a circuit. It is measured in volts (V) using a voltmeter connected in parallel across a component. 1 V = 1 J/C (1 joule per coulomb).

Resistance is a measure of how much a component opposes the flow of current. It is measured in ohms (Ω). Resistance depends on the material, length, cross-sectional area, and temperature of a conductor.

How a circuit works:
1. A cell provides a voltage (energy source)
2. Voltage pushes charge carriers (electrons) around the circuit
3. Current flows from negative to positive (conventional current: positive to negative)
4. Components resist the flow of current (resistance)
5. Energy is transferred to components (e.g., light bulb, motor)

Circuit symbols:
- Cell: two parallel lines (one long, one short)
- Battery: multiple cells in series
- Wire: straight line
- Bulb: circle with cross
- Resistor: zigzag line
- Switch: break in line with a dot
- Ammeter: circle with 'A'
- Voltmeter: circle with 'V'
Worked Example

A circuit has a 12 V battery and a bulb with resistance 6 Ω. Calculate the current.
V = IR → I = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 A

Key Equation (Ohm's Law):
V = IR

V = voltage (V)
I = current (A)
R = resistance (Ω)
Common Misconception: Students often think current is "used up" by components. Current is the same at all points in a series circuit. What is used up is energy (voltage drops across components).

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a circuit symbol reference card. Students match symbols to component names before building circuits.
Stretch:
Calculate the current through a 100 Ω resistor connected to a 230 V mains supply. Discuss the dangers of high voltage and low resistance.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Symbol Quiz

Display circuit symbols on the board. Students write the component name and unit of measurement for each.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can draw circuits using correct symbols, define current/voltage/resistance, and use V = IR
  • Getting there: Can use V = IR for simple calculations but struggles with circuit diagrams
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between current and voltage or draw basic circuits

Lesson 2: Series Circuits

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Prediction

Show a circuit with two bulbs in series. Ask: "If we add a third bulb, what happens to the brightness of all bulbs? Why?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
In a series circuit, components are connected one after another along a single path.

Rules for series circuits:
1. Current is the same at all points — I_total = I₁ = I₂ = I₃
2. Voltage is shared between components — V_total = V₁ + V₂ + V₃
3. Resistance adds up — R_total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃

Key points:
- Adding more components increases total resistance, reducing current
- If one component breaks (open circuit), all components stop working
- Voltage is divided between components in proportion to their resistance
- If two identical bulbs are in series, each gets half the supply voltage

Why does voltage add up?
The total energy per coulomb (voltage) supplied by the battery must equal the total energy per coulomb used by all components. Energy is conserved.
Worked Example

A circuit has a 12 V battery and two resistors in series: R₁ = 4 Ω and R₂ = 8 Ω.
(a) Total resistance: R_total = 4 + 8 = 12 Ω
(b) Total current: I = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 12 = 1 A
(c) Voltage across R₁: V₁ = IR₁ = 1 × 4 = 4 V
(d) Voltage across R₂: V₂ = IR₂ = 1 × 8 = 8 V
(e) Check: V₁ + V₂ = 4 + 8 = 12 V ✓

Key Formulae for Series Circuits:
I_total = I₁ = I₂
V_total = V₁ + V₂
R_total = R₁ + R₂

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a step-by-step template for series circuit calculations. Students complete each step with guidance.
Stretch:
Calculate the voltage across each of three resistors in series (2 Ω, 3 Ω, 5 Ω) connected to a 20 V supply. Verify that voltages add up to 20 V.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Quick Check

Two resistors (3 Ω and 6 Ω) are in series with a 9 V battery. What is the current? What is the voltage across each resistor?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can apply all three rules of series circuits to solve problems and verify answers
  • Getting there: Can calculate total resistance and current but struggles with voltage division
  • Not yet: Cannot apply the rules of series circuits or confuse current and voltage

Lesson 3: Parallel Circuits

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Series vs Parallel

Students compare two circuits: two bulbs in series vs two bulbs in parallel. Predict: "Which circuit has brighter bulbs? Which uses more current? What happens if one bulb breaks in each circuit?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
In a parallel circuit, components are connected across the same two points, creating multiple paths for current.

Rules for parallel circuits:
1. Voltage is the same across each branch — V_total = V₁ = V₂ = V₃
2. Current is shared between branches — I_total = I₁ + I₂ + I₃
3. Total resistance decreases when branches are added — 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂

Key points:
- Each branch gets the full supply voltage
- Adding more branches reduces total resistance (more paths for current)
- If one branch breaks, the others continue to work
- Parallel circuits are used in homes so each appliance gets 230 V independently

Why does total resistance decrease?
Adding a parallel branch provides an alternative path for current. Even though each branch has resistance, the overall resistance of the circuit decreases because there are more paths for charge to flow.
Worked Example

Two resistors in parallel: R₁ = 6 Ω and R₂ = 12 Ω, connected to a 12 V supply.
(a) Voltage across each = 12 V
(b) Current through R₁: I₁ = V ÷ R₁ = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 A
(c) Current through R₂: I₂ = V ÷ R₂ = 12 ÷ 12 = 1 A
(d) Total current: I_total = 2 + 1 = 3 A
(e) Total resistance: R_total = V ÷ I_total = 12 ÷ 3 = 4 Ω
(f) Check using formula: 1/R_total = 1/6 + 1/12 = 2/12 + 1/12 = 3/12 → R_total = 4 Ω ✓

Key Formulae for Parallel Circuits:
V_total = V₁ = V₂
I_total = I₁ + I₂
1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂

Differentiation

Support:
Provide a comparison table of series vs parallel circuits. Students complete the table for current, voltage, and resistance rules.
Stretch:
Calculate the total resistance of three resistors (4 Ω, 4 Ω, 4 Ω) in parallel. Then calculate the total resistance if a fourth 4 Ω resistor is added. Discuss why total resistance always decreases.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Circuit Comparison

Students complete a Venn diagram comparing series and parallel circuits, including rules for current, voltage, resistance, and what happens when a component breaks.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can apply all rules of parallel circuits and compare series and parallel circuits accurately
  • Getting there: Can calculate current and voltage in parallel circuits but struggles with total resistance
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish between series and parallel circuit rules

Lesson 4: I-V Characteristics

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Graph Prediction

Show a blank I-V graph (current on y-axis, voltage on x-axis). Ask: "What shape graph would you expect for a resistor? A bulb? A diode?" Students sketch their predictions.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
The I-V characteristic shows how current changes with voltage for different components.

1. Resistor (ohmic conductor):
- Straight line through the origin
- Current is directly proportional to voltage
- Resistance is constant (gradient = 1/R)
- This is Ohm's Law

2. Filament bulb:
- Curved graph that gets less steep at higher voltages
- As voltage increases, filament heats up, increasing resistance
- Current increases more slowly at higher voltages

3. Diode:
- Very little current in one direction (reverse bias)
- Current increases rapidly above the threshold voltage (~0.6 V for silicon)
- Used to allow current in one direction only

4. Thermistor (NTC):
- Resistance decreases as temperature increases
- Used in temperature-dependent circuits

5. LDR (Light Dependent Resistor):
- Resistance decreases as light intensity increases
- Used in light-sensitive circuits (e.g., street lights)
Worked Example

A resistor has an I-V graph showing a straight line through the origin. At 6 V, the current is 0.3 A. Calculate the resistance.
R = V ÷ I = 6 ÷ 0.3 = 20 Ω
Since the graph is a straight line, the resistance is the same at any voltage.

Key Equation:
R = V ÷ I (gradient of I-V graph = 1/R)
Common Misconception: Students think the resistance of a filament bulb is constant. It increases as the bulb gets hotter, which is why the I-V graph is curved, not straight.

Differentiation

Support:
Provide pre-drawn I-V graphs with labels. Students match each graph to the correct component and explain the shape.
Stretch:
Plot an I-V graph from experimental data for a filament bulb. Calculate the resistance at 2 V, 4 V, and 6 V. Explain why resistance changes.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Graph Matching

Show four I-V graphs. Students name the component for each and explain the shape of the graph in one sentence.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can describe and explain I-V characteristics for resistors, bulbs, and diodes. Can calculate resistance from I-V graphs.
  • Getting there: Can identify I-V graph shapes but cannot explain why they differ
  • Not yet: Cannot connect I-V graphs to component behaviour

Homework Suggestions

  • Draw circuit diagrams for five different circuits using at least three different components
  • Calculate the total resistance of five different combinations of resistors in series and parallel
  • Sketch I-V characteristics for a resistor, filament bulb, and diode from memory
  • Research how an NTC thermistor works and describe one application in a temperature-controlled circuit
