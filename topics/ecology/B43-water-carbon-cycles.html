B43: Water and Carbon Cycles

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand how water and carbon are recycled through ecosystems, and the role of decomposition in returning nutrients to the environment.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2 | Tier: Foundation and Higher | Duration: 50 min per lesson | Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the water cycle and explain how water is recycled in ecosystems
  • Describe the carbon cycle and the role of organisms in recycling carbon
  • Explain the process of decomposition and factors affecting its rate
  • Understand the importance of nutrient cycles for sustaining life

Materials

  • Whiteboard and pens, diagram worksheets, optional decomposition experiment setup

Lesson 1: The Water and Carbon Cycles

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Question

Ask: "Where does the water in your glass originally come from? Could it be the same water that dinosaurs drank?" (Yes — water is constantly recycled.)

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Water cycle:
  • Evaporation: water evaporates from oceans, lakes, and rivers (energy from the Sun)
  • Transpiration: plants release water vapour through their leaves
  • Condensation: water vapour cools and forms clouds
  • Precipitation: water falls as rain, snow, or hail
  • Infiltration: water soaks into the ground and becomes groundwater
  • Runoff: water flows over land into rivers and back to the sea
  • The cycle is driven by energy from the Sun
Carbon cycle:
  • Carbon is present in the atmosphere as CO₂
  • Plants absorb CO₂ during photosynthesis and incorporate carbon into glucose and other organic molecules
  • Animals eat plants and obtain carbon in their food
  • Respiration: all living organisms respire, releasing CO₂ back into the atmosphere
  • Decomposition: decomposers break down dead organisms, releasing CO₂ through respiration
  • Combustion: burning fossil fuels and wood releases stored carbon as CO₂
  • Fossilisation: some dead organisms become fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) over millions of years
  • The carbon cycle maintains the balance of CO₂ in the atmosphere
Worked Example

"Describe how carbon moves from the atmosphere to a tree and back again." Answer: (1) CO₂ is absorbed from the atmosphere by leaves during photosynthesis. (2) Carbon is used to make glucose (organic molecules) in the tree. (3) When the tree (or its leaves) dies, decomposers break it down. (4) Decomposers respire, releasing CO₂ back into the atmosphere. Alternatively, the tree is burned (combustion), releasing CO₂ directly.

Common Misconception

"Carbon is only in the atmosphere"

Carbon is found in many reservoirs: atmosphere (CO₂), oceans (dissolved CO₂ and carbonates), rocks (limestone), fossil fuels, and all living organisms. Carbon moves between these reservoirs through the carbon cycle.

Differentiation

Support: Draw and label the water cycle. Focus on evaporation, rain, and rivers. For carbon: "Plants take in CO₂, animals breathe it out."
Stretch: Research the greenhouse effect. How does the carbon cycle relate to climate change?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Students draw a simple water cycle diagram from memory. Compare with a model.

Assessment

  • Got it: Can describe both cycles with correct terminology
  • Getting there: Can describe one cycle but not the other
  • Not yet: Cannot describe either cycle

Lesson 2: Decomposition

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Rotting

Ask: "Why does food go mouldy? What organisms cause this? What conditions speed it up?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Decomposition: the breakdown of dead organisms and waste by decomposers.
  • Decomposers: bacteria and fungi that secrete enzymes onto dead material and absorb the products of digestion (saprotrophic nutrition)
  • Detritivores: organisms that feed on dead material (e.g. earthworms, woodlice, maggots) — they break dead material into smaller pieces, increasing surface area for decomposers
Factors affecting rate of decomposition:
  • Temperature: warmer = faster (enzymes work faster up to optimum). Very cold = very slow.
  • Water/moisture: decomposers need water. Too dry = slow. Too wet = anaerobic conditions (different decomposers)
  • Oxygen availability: aerobic decomposition is faster than anaerobic. Well-aerated soil speeds decomposition.
  • Surface area: more surface area = faster (detritivores help by breaking material into smaller pieces)
  • Number of decomposers: more decomposers = faster decomposition
Importance of decomposition:
  • Returns nutrients to the soil for plants to absorb
  • Recycles carbon and other elements
  • Without decomposition, dead material would accumulate and nutrients would be locked away
Worked Example

"A student buries cloth bags of leaves in soil at different temperatures. Predict the results." Answer: The bag at the warmest temperature will lose the most mass (fastest decomposition). The bag at the coldest temperature will lose the least mass (slowest decomposition). This shows temperature affects decomposition rate.

Common Misconception

"Decomposers only live in soil"

Decomposers are found wherever there is dead organic material: in soil, in water, on surfaces, and even inside other organisms. They are essential in every ecosystem.

Differentiation

Support: "Decomposers = bacteria and fungi. They break down dead stuff. Warmer and wetter = faster." Provide a flow diagram.
Stretch: Design a fair test to investigate factors affecting decomposition rate. Identify variables and controls.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Students list three factors that affect decomposition rate and explain how each one works.

Assessment

  • Got it: Can explain decomposition and the factors affecting its rate
  • Getting there: Knows decomposers break down dead material but cannot explain factors
  • Not yet: Cannot explain decomposition

Homework Suggestions

  • Draw and label the water cycle and carbon cycle with at least 5 processes each
  • Set up a decomposition experiment: bury banana peels in different conditions and observe over 2 weeks
  • Research how deforestation affects the carbon cycle
  • Explain why compost heaps need to be turned regularly
