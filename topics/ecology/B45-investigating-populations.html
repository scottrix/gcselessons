B45: Investigating Populations

Foundation Higher All Boards

Learn how to estimate the size of animal and plant populations using sampling techniques including quadrats, transects, and the capture-recapture method.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3 | Tier: Foundation and Higher | Duration: 50 min per lesson | Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Understand why sampling is used to estimate population size
  • Use quadrats to estimate the population size of plants and slow-moving animals
  • Use the capture-recapture method to estimate the population size of mobile animals
  • Calculate population size from sampling data

Materials

  • Quadrats (1m x 1m frames), metre rulers, counting trays
  • Outdoor area for fieldwork (school field, park, garden)
  • Recording sheets, calculators

Lesson 1: Using Quadrats

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Estimation

Ask: "How would you estimate how many daisies are on the school field without counting every single one?" Discuss the idea of sampling.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Why sample? Counting every organism in a large area is impractical. Sampling estimates population size from a representative subset.
  • A sample is a small part of the population or area that is studied to make predictions about the whole
  • Must be random and representative — avoid bias
Using quadrats:
  1. Place a 1m x 1m quadrat randomly in the study area
  2. Count the number of organisms of one species inside the quadrat
  3. Repeat at least 5 times (more = more reliable) at random positions
  4. Calculate the mean number of organisms per quadrat
  5. Multiply the mean by the total area to estimate the population size
Random sampling:
  • Use random number generators for coordinates
  • Or throw the quadrat over your shoulder (not aimed)
  • Avoid choosing spots deliberately (this introduces bias)
Calculation:

Population estimate = (mean number per quadrat) x (total area of habitat)

Example: mean = 8 daisies per m², total field = 2,000m². Population = 8 x 2,000 = 16,000 daisies.
Worked Example

"A student counts daisies in five 1m² quadrats: 12, 8, 15, 10, 15. The field is 500m². Estimate the total number of daisies." Answer: Mean = (12+8+15+10+15)/5 = 60/5 = 12 daisies per m². Population estimate = 12 x 500 = 6,000 daisies.

Common Misconception

"One quadrat is enough to estimate population size"

One quadrat gives only one data point and may not be representative. You need at least 5 (ideally more) randomly placed quadrats to calculate a reliable mean. More samples = more accurate estimate.

Differentiation

Support: Use pre-placed quadrats (not random). Focus on counting and calculating the mean. Provide a simple calculation template.
Stretch: How could you use a systematic transect instead of random sampling? When would this be more appropriate?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Students calculate a population estimate from sample data provided.

Assessment

  • Got it: Can use quadrats correctly and calculate population estimates
  • Getting there: Can place quadrats and count but cannot calculate estimates
  • Not yet: Cannot use quadrats or calculate estimates

Lesson 2: Transects and Capture-Recapture

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Distribution

Ask: "Why might organisms not be evenly spread across a field? How could you study how their distribution changes from one edge to another?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Transects:
  • A line (transect) is laid across the study area
  • Quadrats are placed at regular intervals along the transect
  • Counts are made in each quadrat
  • Shows how distribution changes along the transect (e.g. from shade to light, from dry to wet)
  • Types: line transect (continuous recording along the line) or belt transect (quadrats placed at intervals)
Capture-recapture method (for mobile animals):
  1. Capture a sample of animals and mark them (with non-toxic paint, tags, or clips)
  2. Release them back into the habitat and allow them to mix with the population
  3. Recapture a second sample
  4. Count how many in the second sample are marked
  5. Use the formula:

Population size = (Number in first sample x Number in second sample) / Number marked in second sample

Example: First sample = 50 marked. Second sample = 40 caught, of which 10 are marked. Population = (50 x 40) / 10 = 200. Assumptions of capture-recapture:
  • No immigration, emigration, births, or deaths between captures
  • Marks are not lost or rubbed off
  • Marks do not affect the animal's survival (e.g. making it more visible to predators)
  • The marked animals mix randomly with the population before recapture
Worked Example

"A student captures and marks 30 woodlice. After one week, 20 are recaptured, of which 6 are marked. Estimate the population." Answer: Population = (30 x 20) / 6 = 600/6 = 100 woodlice.

Common Misconception

"Capture-recapture gives an exact count"

Capture-recapture gives an ESTIMATE, not an exact count. The accuracy depends on the assumptions being met (no births/deaths/movement, marks not lost, good mixing). If assumptions are violated, the estimate will be inaccurate.

Differentiation

Support: Provide the formula and a step-by-step calculation guide. Focus on simple numbers first.
Stretch: What happens to the estimate if marks fall off? Calculate the error and discuss reliability.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Students calculate three capture-recapture problems with increasing difficulty.

Assessment

  • Got it: Can use both quadrats and capture-recapture correctly with calculations
  • Getting there: Can use one method but not both
  • Not yet: Cannot use sampling methods or calculate estimates

Lesson 3: Fieldwork Investigation

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Plan

Ask: "How would you design an investigation to compare the number of daisies in sunny vs shady parts of a field?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Planning a fieldwork investigation:
  • Define the question: What are you investigating? (e.g. "Does daisy distribution change from sunny to shady areas?")
  • Choose the method: Quadrats for plants, capture-recapture for mobile animals
  • Identify variables:
    • Independent variable: what you change (e.g. light level, distance from hedge)
    • Dependent variable: what you measure (e.g. number of organisms)
    • Control variables: what you keep the same (e.g. quadrat size, time of day, weather conditions)
  • Take repeat readings: at least 5 per condition for reliability
  • Calculate means and present results in tables and graphs
  • Evaluate: Was the method reliable? What could be improved?
Practical considerations:
  • Weather conditions can affect results (rain, temperature, wind)
  • Time of day can affect animal activity
  • Disturbance from the observer can affect results
  • Be careful not to damage habitats during fieldwork
Worked Example

"Design an investigation to compare woodlice numbers under logs vs on open ground." Answer: Use 10 quadrats (or direct counting) under logs and 10 on open ground. Count woodlice in each. Calculate means. Compare means. Ensure: same time of day, same type of log, same area, count carefully to avoid disturbing them.

Common Misconception

"More samples always means better results"

More samples improve reliability, but they also take more time. A balance is needed. Also, if sampling is biased (e.g. choosing convenient spots), more samples will give a more precise but less accurate (biased) estimate.

Differentiation

Support: Provide a partially completed investigation plan. Focus on: question, method, what to measure.
Stretch: Design a complete investigation plan including risk assessment, equipment list, and detailed analysis plan.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Students write a method for a simple investigation in 5 numbered steps.

Assessment

  • Got it: Can plan, carry out, and evaluate a population investigation
  • Getting there: Can carry out an investigation but cannot plan or evaluate
  • Not yet: Cannot plan or carry out an investigation

Homework Suggestions

  • Complete 5 capture-recapture calculations with answers
  • Plan a fieldwork investigation to compare two habitats near your home
  • Research how ecologists use mark-release-recapture to study butterfly populations
  • Explain why random sampling is important and give an example of biased sampling
