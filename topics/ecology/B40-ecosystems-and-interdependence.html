B40: Ecosystems and Interdependence

Foundation Higher All Boards

Understand how organisms in an ecosystem depend on each other for survival, and how changes to one part of a food web can affect the whole community.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 3
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define ecosystem, community, population, habitat, and interdependence
  • Construct and interpret food chains and food webs
  • Understand the roles of producers, consumers, predators, and prey
  • Explain how changes in an ecosystem affect organisms and communities

Prerequisites

  • Basic knowledge of plant and animal cells (B1)
  • Understanding of adaptation (B33)

Materials & Equipment

  • Whiteboard and pens
  • Food web diagram worksheets
  • Local habitat information (e.g. park, garden, woodland)

Lesson 1: Ecosystems and Food Chains

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Think Local

Ask: "Think about a local area you know (a park, garden, or field). What living things live there? How do they depend on each other?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Key terms:
  • Ecosystem: all the living organisms (biotic) in a area, together with the non-living (abiotic) conditions (temperature, water, light, soil)
  • Community: all the living organisms in an ecosystem
  • Population: all the organisms of one species in an ecosystem
  • Habitat: the place where an organism lives
  • Interdependence: the dependence of organisms on each other for survival (food, shelter, pollination, seed dispersal)
Food chains:
  • A diagram showing the transfer of food energy from one organism to another
  • Starts with a producer (plant that makes its own food by photosynthesis)
  • Consumers eat other organisms:
    • Primary consumer: eats producers (herbivore)
    • Secondary consumer: eats primary consumers (carnivore or omnivore)
    • Tertiary consumer: eats secondary consumers (carnivore)
  • Predator: an animal that hunts and eats other animals
  • Prey: an animal that is hunted and eaten by predators
  • Arrows show the direction of energy flow (from the eaten to the eater)
Example food chain: Grass → Rabbit → Fox → Eagle
  • Grass = producer
  • Rabbit = primary consumer (herbivore)
  • Fox = secondary consumer (carnivore)
  • Eagle = tertiary consumer (apex predator)
Decomposers:
  • Bacteria and fungi that break down dead organisms and waste
  • Return nutrients to the soil for plants to use
  • Essential for recycling nutrients in ecosystems
Worked Example

"Construct a food chain in a pond ecosystem." Answer: Algae → Water flea → Stickleback → Pike. Algae is the producer (photosynthesises). Water flea is the primary consumer (herbivore). Stickleback is the secondary consumer (eats water fleas). Pike is the tertiary consumer (apex predator).

Common Misconception

"The arrow in a food chain points from the predator to the prey"

The arrow shows the direction of energy flow — it points from the organism being eaten TO the organism that eats it. So grass → rabbit means the rabbit eats the grass (energy flows from grass to rabbit).

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on: "Food chains show what eats what. Plants are producers. Animals are consumers. Arrows point from food to eater." Provide simple food chains to label. Use familiar examples (garden, pet food chain).
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Create a food web for a local habitat. How many food chains can you identify? What would happen if one species was removed?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Label

Students label a food chain: producer, primary consumer, secondary consumer, tertiary consumer, decomposer. Quick self-check.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can construct food chains and define key terms
  • Getting there: Knows that plants are producers but cannot construct food chains
  • Not yet: Cannot define key terms or construct food chains

Lesson 2: Food Webs and Interdependence

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Connection

Ask: "In a food chain, what happens if we remove one organism?" Discuss that other organisms that depend on it will be affected.

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Food webs:
  • A food web is a network of interconnected food chains
  • Shows the feeding relationships between ALL organisms in an ecosystem
  • More realistic than a single food chain — most organisms eat more than one thing
Interdependence:
  • Organisms in an ecosystem depend on each other for:
    • Food: predators depend on prey, herbivores depend on plants
    • Pollination: bees depend on flowers for nectar; flowers depend on bees for pollination
    • Seed dispersal: plants depend on animals (birds, squirrels) to spread their seeds
    • Shelter/habitat: many organisms depend on trees or other organisms for shelter
    • Competition: organisms compete for the same resources (food, water, light, territory)
  • Changes to one part of the ecosystem affect all other parts
Effect of removing a species:
  • If a predator is removed → prey population increases → overconsumption of food source → ecosystem imbalance
  • If a producer is removed → all consumers affected → ecosystem collapse
  • If a decomposer is removed → dead organisms accumulate, nutrients not recycled → soil becomes infertile → plants cannot grow
Abiotic factors:
  • Non-living factors that affect ecosystems:
    • Temperature, light intensity, moisture, soil pH, wind speed, CO₂ concentration
  • Changes in abiotic factors affect the organisms in the ecosystem
Worked Example

"Explain what would happen to a food web if the population of rabbits decreased." Answer: (1) Foxes that eat rabbits would have less food → fox population may decrease. (2) Grass that rabbits eat would increase in growth (less being eaten). (3) Other herbivores competing with rabbits for grass may increase. (4) Predators that eat foxes may decrease. The whole web is affected.

Common Misconception

"Ecosystems are balanced and stable"

Ecosystems are constantly changing. Populations fluctuate naturally due to seasonal changes, disease, predator-prey cycles, and environmental factors. The idea of a perfectly balanced ecosystem is a simplification.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on: "Food webs show all the connections. If one thing changes, everything else changes too." Provide a simple food web and ask "what happens if we remove X?" Use visual arrows.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Investigate trophic levels and energy transfer efficiency (only ~10% of energy transfers between trophic levels). How does this limit the number of organisms at each level?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Remove a Species

Students predict what would happen to a food web if one species was removed. Share predictions.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can construct food webs and explain interdependence
  • Getting there: Can read food webs but cannot predict effects of changes
  • Not yet: Cannot construct or interpret food webs

Lesson 3: Competition

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Competition

Ask: "What do plants compete for? What do animals compete for? Why is competition important in ecosystems?"

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Competition: organisms compete for limited resources. Types of competition:
  • Intraspecific: competition between organisms of the SAME species (e.g. two oak trees competing for light)
  • Interspecific: competition between organisms of DIFFERENT species (e.g. foxes and badgers competing for food)
What organisms compete for:
  • Plants: light, water, mineral ions (from soil), space, pollinators
  • Animals: food, water, mates, territory, nesting sites
Adaptations that help organisms compete:
  • Plants: tall trees grow above others to get light, deep roots to access water, wide leaves to capture more light
  • Animals: sharp teeth/claws for hunting, camouflage to avoid predators, fast running to catch prey or escape
Predator-prey relationships:
  • Predator and prey populations are linked
  • When prey is abundant → predator population increases (more food available)
  • When predator population is high → prey decreases (more being eaten)
  • When prey is scarce → predator population decreases (less food)
  • When predator population is low → prey increases (less being eaten)
  • This creates a cycle of population changes
Worked Example

"Explain why two plant species growing in the same area might compete." Answer: Both species need the same resources: light, water, and mineral ions from the soil. If these resources are limited, the species that is better adapted (e.g. taller to get more light, deeper roots to get more water) will outcompete the other. The less well-adapted species may decline or be unable to grow in that area.

Common Misconception

"Competition always means fighting"

Competition in biology means competing for resources, not necessarily fighting. Plants compete for light by growing taller — they don't fight. Animals may compete by being faster, stronger, or more efficient at finding food, not always by direct confrontation.

Differentiation

Support (SEN/Lower Ability):
Focus on: "Plants compete for light and water. Animals compete for food and territory." Provide examples of each. Use the predator-prey cycle as a visual diagram.
Stretch (Higher/Gifted):
Research the Lotka-Volterra predator-prey model. How do mathematicians model predator-prey cycles? What assumptions does the model make?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Compete For

Students list three things plants compete for and three things animals compete for. Quick share.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Can explain competition and predator-prey relationships
  • Getting there: Knows organisms compete but cannot explain the predator-prey cycle
  • Not yet: Cannot explain competition in ecosystems

Homework Suggestions

  • Create a food web for a habitat you have visited (garden, park, woodland)
  • Research the reintroduction of wolves to Yellowstone — how did it affect the ecosystem?
  • Investigate how changes in temperature affect organisms in a local habitat
  • Design a simple fieldwork investigation to compare two habitats
