C20: Chemical Cells and Fuel Cells

Foundation All Boards

Describe how chemical cells produce electricity and explain how fuel cells work, including hydrogen fuel cells.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Describe how simple chemical cells (batteries) produce electricity
  • Explain how fuel cells differ from chemical cells
  • Describe hydrogen fuel cells and their advantages and disadvantages

Prerequisites

  • Reactivity of Metals (C15) — reactivity series
  • Exothermic and Endothermic (C19) — energy changes

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Simple cell demonstration: zinc and copper strips, lemon or potato, wires, LED/voltmeter
  • Video: hydrogen fuel cell car

Lesson 1: Chemical Cells (Batteries)

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Battery Power

What batteries do you use at home? Remote controls, torches, phones, laptops. What do all these batteries have in common? How do they produce electricity?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Chemical cells (batteries) convert chemical energy into electrical energy through redox reactions.

How a simple cell works:
• Two different metals are placed in a solution containing their ions (or connected by a wire through an external circuit)
• The more reactive metal loses electrons (oxidised) → acts as the negative electrode (anode)
• The less reactive metal gains electrons (reduced) → acts as the positive electrode (cathode)
• Electrons flow through the external circuit from the more reactive metal to the less reactive metal → this flow of electrons is electricity
• The reaction is a redox reaction

Example: zinc-copper cell
• Zinc is more reactive than copper
• Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ (zinc loses electrons — oxidation at the negative electrode)
• Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (copper ions gain electrons — reduction at the positive electrode)
• Electrons flow from zinc to copper through the wire
• Overall: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu

Batteries (chemical cells) in everyday life:
• Alkaline batteries: zinc and manganese dioxide
• Lead-acid batteries: lead and lead(IV) oxide in sulfuric acid (car batteries)
• Lithium-ion batteries: lithium compounds (phones, laptops, electric cars)

Advantages of batteries:
• Portable — no wires needed
• No fuel supply needed (self-contained)
• Quiet and clean at point of use

Disadvantages:
• Must be recharged or replaced
• Some contain toxic/heavy metals (disposal problems)
• Finite lifespan
Lemon battery

Push a zinc nail and a copper coin into a lemon. Connect a wire between them through a voltmeter. The lemon juice acts as the electrolyte. Voltage is about 0.9V. Two lemons in series = about 1.8V — enough to light a small LED!

Why different metals?

The cell only works if the two metals have different reactivities. The greater the difference in reactivity, the higher the voltage. Zinc-copper gives about 1.1V. Zinc-silver would give more. Zinc-zinc gives zero — no potential difference.

Chemical cell: chemical energy → electrical energy

Negative electrode (anode): more reactive metal → oxidation
Positive electrode (cathode): less reactive metal → reduction

Example: Zn-Cu cell
Anode: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻
Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
Overall: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu

Common Misconception

Batteries store electricity.

Batteries store chemical energy, which is converted to electrical energy when the battery is used. The chemicals inside the battery undergo a redox reaction, and the flow of electrons through the external circuit is the electric current.

Differentiation

Support:
Use the lemon battery practical if available. Connect two lemons in series and measure voltage. Add a third lemon — what happens to the voltage?
Stretch:
Research: why are lithium-ion batteries used in electric cars instead of lead-acid? Compare energy density, weight, and rechargeability.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Explain how a zinc-copper cell produces electricity. Which metal is the negative electrode? Why? What is the overall reaction?

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains how chemical cells work, identifies the electrodes and the redox reaction, and gives everyday examples.
  • Getting there: Knows batteries produce electricity from chemical reactions but cannot explain the electrode processes.
  • Not yet: Cannot explain how batteries work. Reteach with the lemon battery demonstration.

Lesson 2: Fuel Cells

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Fuel vs Battery

A car can be powered by a battery or by petrol. A battery stores its energy inside. Petrol is continuously supplied. What are the advantages of each? What about a fuel cell?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Fuel cells are like batteries, but the fuel is continuously supplied from an external source rather than being stored inside.

Hydrogen fuel cell:
• Hydrogen gas is supplied to the negative electrode (anode)
• Oxygen (from air) is supplied to the positive electrode (cathode)
• Hydrogen is oxidised: H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ (at anode)
• Oxygen is reduced: O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O (at cathode)
• Overall: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
• The only product is water — no pollution!

How it works:
1. Hydrogen gas enters at the anode and splits into H⁺ ions and electrons
2. Electrons flow through the external circuit (this is the electric current — powers the car/device)
3. H⁺ ions pass through a membrane to the cathode
4. At the cathode, oxygen from air combines with H⁺ ions and electrons to form water

Advantages of hydrogen fuel cells:
• Only product is water — no CO₂, no pollution at point of use
• Hydrogen can be produced from water (using electrolysis with renewable energy)
• Refuelling is quick (like filling a petrol tank — unlike recharging batteries)
• High energy density — good for vehicles

Disadvantages:
• Hydrogen is expensive to produce (electrolysis needs lots of electricity)
• Hydrogen is difficult to store (needs high pressure or very low temperature)
• Fuel cells are expensive to manufacture (platinum catalyst needed)
• Infrastructure for hydrogen refuelling is limited
• Most hydrogen is currently made from natural gas (methane) — produces CO₂
• If hydrogen is made using fossil fuels, the overall carbon footprint is not zero
Hydrogen fuel cell car

Toyota Mirai and Hyundai Nexo use hydrogen fuel cells. Hydrogen from a tank combines with oxygen from air. Only water comes out of the exhaust. Range is about 400-600 miles. Refuelling takes about 5 minutes.

Fuel cell vs battery

Fuel cell: continuous fuel supply (like a petrol car). Battery: stores energy internally (needs recharging). Fuel cells are better for heavy vehicles (buses, lorries) because they're lighter than huge batteries. Batteries are better for small cars and phones because they're simpler.

Hydrogen fuel cell:
Anode: H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻
Cathode: O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O
Overall: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Chemical cell: stores energy inside (must recharge)
Fuel cell: fuel supplied continuously from outside

Common Misconception

Hydrogen fuel cells produce no pollution at all.

At point of use, the only product is water — no pollution. However, most hydrogen is currently produced from natural gas (methane), which releases CO₂. If hydrogen is produced by electrolysis using renewable electricity, then the whole process is zero-carbon.

Differentiation

Support:
Compare chemical cell and fuel cell side by side: where does the fuel come from? What is the product? Can it be recharged? What are the advantages?
Stretch:
Research: what is a 'hydrogen economy'? Why might hydrogen replace fossil fuels in the future? What are the barriers?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Compare chemical cells and fuel cells: three similarities, three differences. Which is better for a car? Justify your answer.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Explains how fuel cells work, compares them to chemical cells, and evaluates hydrogen fuel cell technology.
  • Getting there: Knows fuel cells use hydrogen and oxygen but cannot explain the electrode reactions.
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish fuel cells from chemical cells. Reteach using the 'continuous fuel supply' concept.

Homework Suggestions

  • Compare chemical cells and fuel cells in a table (energy source, products, advantages, disadvantages)
  • Research: how is hydrogen produced? Compare 'green', 'blue' and 'grey' hydrogen
  • Design a poster explaining how a hydrogen fuel cell works
