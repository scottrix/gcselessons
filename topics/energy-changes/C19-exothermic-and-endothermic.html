C19: Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions

Foundation All Boards

Distinguish between exothermic and endothermic reactions, and explain energy transfers in chemical reactions.

Lesson Overview

Estimated Lessons: 2
Tier: Foundation and Higher
Duration: 50 minutes per lesson
Exam Boards: AQA, Edexcel, OCR, CCEA

Learning Objectives

  • Define exothermic and endothermic reactions and give examples
  • Explain why temperature changes occur during chemical reactions using bond energy ideas
  • Interpret reaction profile diagrams for exothermic and endothermic reactions

Prerequisites

  • Atoms and Elements (C1) — chemical bonding basics
  • Bonding (C5-C6) — ionic and covalent bonds

Materials & Equipment

  • Periodic table (printed or wall chart)
  • Scientific calculator
  • Thermometers (if doing practical)
  • Test tubes and beakers
  • Calcium chloride and water for exothermic demo
  • Citric acid and sodium hydrogen carbonate for endothermic demo

Lesson 1: Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Hot and Cold

Which gets hotter: a hand warmer or an instant cold pack? What makes one hot and the other cold?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Exothermic reactions: release energy (usually as heat) to the surroundings. The temperature of the surroundings increases.

Examples of exothermic reactions:
• Burning (combustion): methane + oxygen → CO₂ + H₂O + heat
• Neutralisation: acid + base → salt + water + heat
• Rusting: iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide + heat
• Exothermic hand warmers (iron oxidation)
• Respiration in cells (glucose + oxygen → CO₂ + water + energy)

Endothermic reactions: absorb energy from the surroundings. The temperature of the surroundings decreases.

Examples of endothermic reactions:
• Thermal decomposition: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (needs heat)
• Photosynthesis: CO₂ + H₂O → glucose + O₂ (needs light energy)
• Instant cold packs: ammonium nitrate dissolving in water
• Citric acid + sodium hydrogen carbonate → fizzy, cold solution

Bond energy explanation:
• Energy is needed to break bonds (always endothermic)
• Energy is released when bonds form (always exothermic)
• If more energy is released forming bonds than is needed to break them → overall exothermic
• If more energy is needed to break bonds than is released forming them → overall endothermic
Hand warmers and cold packs

Hand warmer (exothermic): iron filings + salt + water + air → iron rusts → releases heat
Cold pack (endothermic): ammonium nitrate dissolves in water → absorbs heat → feels cold
Both are used in first aid. The chemical reaction determines whether energy is released or absorbed.

Why breaking bonds needs energy

Bond breaking and bond forming are opposite processes. Breaking a bond requires energy input (like pulling two magnets apart). Forming a bond releases energy (like letting two magnets snap together). The overall energy change depends on the balance between these two.

Exothermic: releases heat → surroundings get hotter
Endothermic: absorbs heat → surroundings get colder

Bond energy:
Breaking bonds = endothermic (energy absorbed)
Forming bonds = exothermic (energy released)

Overall: Energy to break bonds − Energy released forming bonds = ΔH
If ΔH < 0: exothermic (more energy released)
If ΔH > 0: endothermic (more energy absorbed)

Common Misconception

Bond formation releases energy, so it is endothermic.

Bond formation is exothermic — it releases energy. Bond breaking is endothermic — it requires energy. This is the opposite of what many students expect. Think of it like magnets: pulling them apart (breaking) needs effort, but letting them come together (forming) releases energy.

Differentiation

Support:
Use physical demonstrations. Dissolve calcium chloride (gets hot, exothermic). Dissolve citric acid + baking soda (gets cold, endothermic). Let children feel the temperature change.
Stretch:
Research: why do some hand warmers stay hot for hours while others cool quickly? What controls the rate?

Plenary (5 minutes)

Classify as exothermic or endothermic: photosynthesis, burning wood, dissolving NaOH in water, thermal decomposition of CaCO₃, respiration, neutralisation.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Defines exothermic and endothermic, gives multiple examples, and explains using bond energy ideas.
  • Getting there: Knows the difference and can give examples but cannot explain using bond energies.
  • Not yet: Cannot distinguish exothermic and endothermic. Reteach with physical demonstrations.

Lesson 2: Reaction Profile Diagrams

Duration: 50 minutes

Starter Activity (5 minutes)

Energy Hill

Imagine pushing a ball over a hill. You need energy to push it up (like breaking bonds). When it rolls down the other side, it releases energy (like forming bonds). If the ball ends up lower than it started, where did the extra energy go?

Main Content (35 minutes)

Teaching Notes:
Reaction profile diagrams show the energy changes during a reaction.

Exothermic profile:
• Energy of reactants is higher than energy of products
• Energy is released to surroundings
• ΔH is negative (energy of products − energy of reactants < 0)
• There is an 'energy hill' (activation energy) that reactants must overcome before the reaction can happen

Endothermic profile:
• Energy of reactants is lower than energy of products
• Energy is absorbed from surroundings
• ΔH is positive (energy of products − energy of reactants > 0)
• There is a larger 'energy hill' (activation energy) — the reaction needs more energy input to get started

Key terms on diagrams:
• Activation energy (Ea): the minimum energy needed for the reaction to start (energy needed to break initial bonds)
• ΔH (enthalpy change): the overall energy change (energy of products − energy of reactants)
• For exothermic: ΔH < 0, product energy level is lower
• For endothermic: ΔH > 0, product energy level is higher

Catalysts: lower the activation energy by providing an alternative reaction pathway. They do not change ΔH (the overall energy change).
Exothermic profile

Reactants start at high energy level → energy hill (activation energy) → products end at lower energy level. The difference is ΔH (negative). Energy released = energy of reactants − energy of products.

Endothermic profile

Reactants start at low energy level → large energy hill (activation energy) → products end at higher energy level. The difference is ΔH (positive). Energy absorbed = energy of products − energy of reactants.

Exothermic profile:
Reactants (high energy) → Ea → Products (low energy)
ΔH = negative (energy released)

Endothermic profile:
Reactants (low energy) → Ea → Products (high energy)
ΔH = positive (energy absorbed)

ΔH = Energy(products) − Energy(reactants)
Catalyst: lowers Ea but does not change ΔH

Common Misconception

Endothermic reactions have no activation energy.

Endothermic reactions still have an activation energy — the reactants need enough energy to get over the 'hill' before the reaction can proceed. This is why some endothermic reactions need to be heated to start, even though they absorb energy overall.

Differentiation

Support:
Draw the diagrams step by step. First draw the axes (energy vs reaction progress). Then draw the reactant line, then the product line (higher for endothermic, lower for exothermic). Then draw the 'hill' between them.
Stretch:
Draw a reaction profile showing both the uncatalysed and catalysed pathway on the same diagram. Label Ea and ΔH for each.

Plenary (5 minutes)

Draw a reaction profile for: (a) an exothermic reaction, (b) an endothermic reaction. Label reactants, products, activation energy, and ΔH on each.

Assessment Criteria

  • Got it: Draws and interprets reaction profile diagrams, labels activation energy and ΔH, and explains what they mean.
  • Getting there: Can draw the diagrams but cannot always explain what activation energy represents.
  • Not yet: Cannot draw reaction profiles. Reteach with the 'energy hill' analogy and step-by-step drawing.

Homework Suggestions

  • Draw reaction profiles for 4 different reactions (2 exothermic, 2 endothermic)
  • Research: what is the activation energy of the combustion of methane? Why does it need a spark to start?
  • Create a table comparing exothermic and endothermic reactions with examples
